## Core Finding

Ancient DNA analysis of nine individuals buried in Room 33 at Pueblo Bonito reveals that all share identical mitochondrial genomes (B2y1 haplotype), and nuclear genome SNP data identify two pairs of close kin: a mother-daughter pair (crania 1 and 7) and a grandmother-grandson pair (crania 8 and 10). High-precision AMS radiocarbon dating places these burials between approximately 800 and 1130 CE, a span of approximately 330 years. The authors conclude that an elite matrilineal dynasty persisted at the center of Chacoan complex society for the full duration of the Bonito phase.

## Key Data

- **Individuals analyzed**: 9 (mtDNA), 6 (nuclear SNPs)
- **Mitochondrial genome coverage**: Average 16,231 +/- 455 bp (98.1% of complete genome); 7.4x to 117x fold coverage
- **Nuclear SNPs recovered**: Average 73,247 +/- 46,843 per individual (range: 14,948-144,227)
- **mtDNA haplogroup**: B2y1 (low frequency among Southwest Native Americans)
- **Chronological span**: Burial 14 dated to 800-850 CE; latest burials to approximately 1130 CE
- **Genetic sex**: Three female, three male, two likely male (concordant with osteological determinations)
- **Interment pattern**: Continuous rather than episodic (based on radiocarbon simulations)

## Burial Context

Room 33 was constructed as part of Pueblo Bonito's initial building phase in the ninth century CE. It is a small (approximately 2m x 2m) room with a hatch-like entry, purpose-built as a burial crypt. Burial 14 (the lineage founder), a male in his 40s who died from head trauma, was interred with more than 11,200 turquoise and more than 3,300 shell beads and pendants, multiple abalone shells from the Pacific Coast, and a conch shell trumpet. This constitutes the richest burial known in the North American Southwest. Burial 13, also richly accompanied (more than 5,800 turquoise pieces), was placed above Burial 14, separated by a 70 cm sand layer, and covered by a plank floor. Twelve additional individuals were interred above the floor over the subsequent centuries. Adjacent rooms contained caches of ceremonial staffs, cylindrical ceramic vessels, scarlet macaw remains, and jewelry.

## Scholarly Context

The paper addresses the long-standing debate over whether Chacoan social organization followed a Western Pueblo model (matrilineally ranked groups controlling ritual power; cf. Hopi) or an Eastern Pueblo model (achievement-based ritual sodalities; cf. some Rio Grande Pueblos). The findings support the Western Pueblo model: leadership was transmitted through matrilineal descent rather than selected on the basis of individual achievement. The authors note that matrilineal systems are ethnographically documented in all Western Pueblos (Hopi, Zuni, Acoma, Laguna) and among Keresan-speaking Rio Grande Pueblos (Cochiti, San Felipe, Santo Domingo, Zia).

## Methods

- **aDNA extraction and library preparation**: Performed in dedicated sterile facilities at Penn State and Harvard Medical School; multiple decontamination protocols; negative controls throughout
- **mtDNA capture**: In-solution biotinylated RNA bait hybridization targeting five Native American mtDNA haplogroups (A2, B2, C1, D1, X2a)
- **Nuclear SNP genotyping**: Enrichment for 1,237,207 SNP targets (Affymetrix Human Origins, Illumina 610-Quad, Affymetrix 50k arrays)
- **Relatedness estimation**: Autosomal SNP mismatch rates with block jackknife standard errors; 856,473 SNPs on chromosomes 1-22 (excluding CpG context to avoid damage bias)
- **Authenticity validation**: aDNA damage patterns confirmed via mapDamage 2.0; independent replication at two labs; PMDtools filtering at PMD score >= 3 confirmed consensus haplotype at 100% of represented sites
- **Radiocarbon**: Bayesian chronological model in OxCal 4.2.3 with IntCal13 curve; calibration curve simulations to evaluate episodic vs. continuous interment

## Relevance to Costly Signaling Model

This paper is critical for **Layer 1 (individual signaling equilibrium)** of the three-layer model:

1. **Spence condition / quality differentiation**: The hereditary matrilineal dynasty provides direct evidence for the kind of quality differentiation that the Spence condition requires. If elite status is heritable (transmitted matrilineally), then signaler "quality" (q) is correlated within lineages, creating a stable basis for separating equilibria where high-quality signalers invest more in costly displays.

2. **Duration of the signaling equilibrium**: The 330-year persistence of the dynasty (800-1130 CE) demonstrates that the signaling equilibrium was not a transient phenomenon but endured across multiple generations, including periods of environmental variability. The dynasty's termination coincides with the collapse of the Chaco system, not with an internal failure of the signaling mechanism.

3. **Institutional foundation of inequality**: The purpose-built crypt and the founding burial with extraordinary wealth show that elite status differentiation was institutionalized at the very origin of Pueblo Bonito, not something that developed gradually during the Chaco florescence. This constrains models that treat inequality as an emergent consequence of the system's growth.

4. **Matrilineal organization and corporate groups**: The matrilineal structure provides a kinship mechanism for organizing the corporate groups that constitute the signaling units in Layer 2. Matrilineal clans are natural candidates for the "groups" in the intergroup signaling framework, with each clan's great house serving as the group signal.

5. **Exotic goods and ritual control**: The concentration of the highest-value exotic goods (turquoise, macaws, cacao vessels, conch shells, ceremonial staffs) in and around Room 33 supports the inference that the matrilineal dynasty controlled access to the costliest signaling media.
