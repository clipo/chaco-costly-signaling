## Core Argument

Wills, Drake, and Dorshow systematically challenge the widely accepted narrative that Chaco Canyon experienced catastrophic deforestation driven by unsustainable cultural practices, ultimately leading to societal collapse. The paper examines five lines of evidence commonly cited in support of the deforestation hypothesis: packrat midden data, pollen records, fuel use patterns, construction timber species, and strontium isotope provenance data. For each, the authors find that the evidence is either insufficient to support the deforestation claim or is consistent with alternative interpretations. They additionally present a GIS-based least-cost path analysis suggesting that eastern sources were competitive with the Chuska Mountains for ponderosa procurement. The paper concludes that there is no substantive evidence for deforestation at Chaco, no indication that agricultural fields were destroyed by deforestation, and no basis for using Chaco as a cautionary tale about societal "ecocide." The authors argue that the conclusion that deforestation caused Chaco's collapse has been widely accepted without adequate empirical confirmation.

## Key Data

1. **Packrat midden samples**: Only 2 radiocarbon-dated samples from the entire prehistoric ceramic occupation (ca. A.D. 500-1300), neither from the Pueblo Bonito area (p. 2).

2. **Fuel use**: Charcoal from all time periods dominated by shrubby species (Atriplex, Artemisia), not trees. Dried maize cobs were a significant but underrepresented fuel source (p. 3).

3. **Timber species correlations** (Table 1, p. 4): Spearman's rank correlations between species in construction wood. Ponderosa-spruce/fir: r=0.66 (P=0.01); ponderosa-pinyon: r=0.20 (P<0.05); ponderosa-juniper: r=0.13 (n.s.); spruce/fir-pinyon: r=0.30 (P<0.001); spruce/fir-juniper: r=0.23 (P<0.001).

4. **Strontium isotopes**: Kernel density plots (Fig. 6) show extensive overlap between Chaco Canyon drainage, Chuska Mountains, La Plata Mountains, San Pedro Mountains, and other potential sources. Source attribution is ambiguous (pp. 5-6).

5. **GIS path analysis**: Weighted-cost corridor surface (Fig. 7) identifies two broad zones east of Chaco (above 2,500 m elevation within the watershed) as least-cost ponderosa sources. La Plata Mountains to the north are no more costly than northern Chuska Mountains (p. 6).

6. **Spatial concentration of architecture**: 99% of great house architecture by volume within 40-km radius of Pueblo Bonito (p. 7; Fig. 9).

7. **Construction decline**: Energy investment in great houses began declining in the late A.D. 1000s, before the onset of documented drought periods (pp. 3-4; Fig. 5).

## Relevance to Signaling Model

Wills et al. 2014 contributes to the signaling model in three ways:

1. **Cost parameter stability**: If the deforestation narrative is overstated, the cost parameter C for construction signaling may not have increased as dramatically over time as other models assume. The finding that spruce/fir was always part of the construction "recipe" (not a late substitute for depleted ponderosa) suggests long-distance timber procurement was built into the cost structure from the beginning rather than being an escalating burden.

2. **Endogenous decline in signaling investment**: The observation that construction investment declined before drought onset (p. 4) is important for the model's collapse mechanism. Rather than exogenous environmental shock alone driving collapse, signaling investment may have declined due to endogenous factors: declining returns from cooperation networks, audience loss to competing centers (Aztec), or reaching the limit of feasible labor mobilization. This is consistent with Barclay et al.'s (2021) prediction that signaling ceases when no sender type can afford the cost or when receivers no longer demand signals.

3. **Spatial structure of the signaling system**: The finding that 99% of great house architecture is concentrated within 40 km of Pueblo Bonito constrains the spatial scale of the core signaling system. Combined with the observation that western outliers were "geographically and economically marginal," this suggests the effective signaling network was centered on the canyon drainage, with outliers representing a secondary extension.

## Connections to Other Sources

- **Lentz et al. 2021**: Directly contradicts Wills et al. 2014 on deforestation, presenting pollen evidence for progressive, anthropogenic woodland decline. The disagreement is an active empirical debate.
- **Wills 2017**: Same lead author. Wills 2017 extends the labor-recruitment argument without repeating the deforestation critique.
- **Dorshow & Wills 2022**: Same research group. Extends the GIS agricultural suitability analysis.
- **Guiterman et al. 2015**: Timber procurement data; Wills et al.'s critique of strontium isotope provenance challenges the geographic attribution of Guiterman et al.'s timber sources.
- **Van Dyke 2004**: Van Dyke's Late Bonito construction dates (A.D. 1100-1140) postdate the construction decline that Wills et al. identify in the late 1000s, suggesting the Late Bonito building program was a counter-trend response.
- **Kohler et al. 2023**: Both papers note declining energy investment in the late Bonito phase and the spatial concentration of great house architecture.

## Cautions

This paper has been contested, particularly by Lentz et al. 2021. The manuscript should present the Wills et al. critique alongside the Lentz et al. response, noting the active empirical debate rather than adjudicating between them. The signaling model should be robust to either interpretation: whether C was rising (deforestation driving cost escalation) or stable (long-distance procurement from the beginning), the model's qualitative predictions hold.
