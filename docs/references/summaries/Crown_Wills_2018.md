## Core Argument

Crown and Wills review the origins, construction sequence, and abandonment of Pueblo Bonito, drawing on their own 14 years of excavation and analysis alongside archival records and prior research. Their central thesis is that the great house was "a never-ending project of construction, remodelling, renewal and destruction" whose history may never be fully understood because its builders never considered it finished (p. 890). They propose that interpretation is confounded both by the complex excavation history (including destructive nineteenth-century looting) and by the inherently palimpsest-like nature of a structure continuously modified over 300 years.

The paper documents several key phases. The site was first occupied during the Basketmaker III Period (c. AD 450-750), with pithouses found 4 m below the current surface under the West Court. Construction of masonry rooms began in the ninth century, with diverse building techniques suggesting household-level construction rather than a centrally organized project. By the mid-tenth century, the northern portion contained approximately 25 ground-floor rooms, some of which were used for habitation, cooking, and storage before eventually becoming burial crypts (Rooms 33 and 56).

Tree-ring dates indicate a hiatus in building activity between c. AD 975 and 1040, during which existing rooms were left unmaintained and partly collapsed (Windes & Ford 1996: 301). When construction resumed, the surface of the West Court had risen to approximately the level of the first-storey rooftops. The eleventh century saw intensified construction: at least 80 rooms added from AD 1040-1050, 24 rooms from AD 1050-1070, and 160-170 rooms from AD 1070-1100 (Windes & Ford 1996: 302-303). Remodeling included replacement of walls with newer masonry styles, addition of upper storeys, and construction of new architectural features such as T-shaped doorways for ceremonial processions.

Around AD 1100, termination rituals involved sealing rooms with masonry and setting fires that destroyed rooms along the northern arc of the West Court. Room 28 contained 173 ceramic vessels, including 112 cylinder jars, the largest assemblage of whole vessels from any single room in the region. Post-florescence occupation continued into the AD 1200s, possibly by Mesa Verde migrants, but shifted from monumental construction to renovation of ritual structures and construction of small semi-subterranean houses.

## Key Data

1. **Scale**: Approximately 600 masonry rooms, up to four storeys, approximately 37 kivas (p. 892).

2. **Earliest occupation**: Basketmaker III pithouses under West Court, approximately 4 m below modern surface, radiocarbon-dated layers consistent with AD 450-750 occupation (pp. 892-893).

3. **Early construction (AD 850-925)**: Diverse masonry techniques including large sandstone blocks with little mortar, tabular sandstone, jacal, and widely-spaced stone courses with mud. Approximately 25 ground-floor rooms by mid-tenth century in the northern area (pp. 897-899).

4. **Construction hiatus (c. AD 975-1040)**: Approximately 65-year gap in tree-ring dates. Rooms left unmaintained, partly collapsed (p. 899; Windes & Ford 1996: 301).

5. **Classic Bonito Phase construction (AD 1040-1100)**: At least 80 rooms (1040-1050), 24 rooms (1050-1070), 160-170 rooms (1070-1100). Total of approximately 264-274 new rooms in 60 years (p. 899; Windes & Ford 1996: 302-303).

6. **Termination events (c. AD 1100)**: Room 28 sealed with 173 ceramic vessels including 112 cylinder jars. Fires set that partially vitrified floors and collapsed upper rooms. Similar fires in adjacent rooms along northern West Court arc (p. 901).

7. **Post-florescence**: Continued occupation into AD 1200s with reduced construction investment. Some rooms repurposed (possible turkey pen in Room 28b). Ancestral Puebloans probably abandoned by the fourteenth century (p. 901; Windes & Ford 1996: 297).

8. **Surface accumulation**: West Court surface rose to approximately first-storey roof level by AD 1000s through a combination of wind-blown sand, flood deposits, and possibly intentional filling (p. 899; Judd 1964: 60; Stein et al. 2003: 50).

## Relevance to Signaling Model

Crown & Wills 2018 contributes to the signaling model in four ways:

1. **Signal depreciation validation (delta parameter)**: The paper provides the strongest empirical support for the model's signal depreciation rate. The AD 975-1040 hiatus demonstrates what happens when maintenance investment ceases: rooms collapse, surfaces accumulate debris, and the signal degrades. The subsequent construction boom (264-274 rooms in 60 years) represents the cost of restoring signal value after a period of depreciation. This validates the model's prediction that continuous reinvestment is required to maintain signal effectiveness, and that the cost of restoration after neglect exceeds the cost of continuous maintenance.

2. **Construction as continuous process, not discrete event**: The "never-ending project" characterization challenges any model that treats monument construction as a single investment event with a one-time cost C. Instead, the total cost is better understood as an integral of ongoing investment over the building's lifetime, with each renovation cycle adding to (not replacing) the accumulated investment. The model's formulation of signal value as a function of ongoing investment minus depreciation captures this dynamic.

3. **Termination rituals as deliberate signal destruction**: The AD 1100 termination events, in which rooms were sealed, filled with valuable objects, and burned, represent intentional destruction of signal value. This is not captured by the delta parameter (which models gradual physical decay) and suggests an additional mechanism: ritual decommissioning that marks the end of a signaling cycle. The 112 cylinder jars entombed in Room 28 represent a substantial sacrifice of exotic goods signaling capital.

4. **Transition from decentralized to centralized construction**: The shift from diverse, household-level masonry techniques (AD 850-925) to standardized construction methods (post-AD 1020) documents the emergence of coordinated signaling investment. This transition is consistent with the model's prediction that collective signaling requires overcoming coordination problems, with early construction representing individual contributions to a proto-collective signal and later construction representing a centrally organized signaling program.

## Connections to Other Sources

- **Windes & Ford 1996**: Primary source for tree-ring dates and construction sequence. Crown & Wills synthesize and extend their chronological framework.
- **Lekson 1984**: Masonry typology (Types I-IV) and labor estimates used by Crown & Wills to characterize construction phases.
- **Wills et al. 2022**: Provides labor cost estimates (20,000-192,000 person-hours per construction stage) for the construction events documented by Crown & Wills.
- **Crown & Hurst 2009; Crown 2012**: The 112 cylinder jars sealed in Room 28 are the same vessels identified as cacao-consumption containers, linking the termination ritual to exotic goods signaling.
- **Plog & Heitman 2010**: The Room 33 burial crypts documented by Crown & Wills are the same elite burials dated by Plog & Heitman to AD 775-875.
- **Watson et al. 2015**: The early macaw dates (AD 885-990) overlap with Crown & Wills' early construction phase (AD 850-925), placing exotic goods acquisition alongside initial monument building.
- **Van Dyke 2004**: Late Bonito Phase construction costs (55,000-90,000 person-hours) represent the reduced investment phase that followed the intensive construction Crown & Wills document.
- **Safi 2015**: Outlier great house remodeling patterns parallel the continuous renovation Crown & Wills describe at the canyon core.
- **Lentz et al. 2021**: Environmental degradation context for the post-florescence decline in construction investment.
