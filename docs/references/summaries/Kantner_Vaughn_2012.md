## Relevance to Argument

This is the only published application of CST to Chaco Canyon. It provides a verbal framework (dual signals: center signals leader status, pilgrimage signals pilgrim commitment) that the mls-monuments three-layer model substantially extends and formalizes. Key empirical data on paleoclimate variability, violence patterns, and exchange network changes during the Bonito phase are directly relevant to model calibration and testing.

## Data Presented

- **Table 2**: Paleoclimate statistics for Chaco periods (precipitation mean, standard deviation, autocorrelation r1) derived from dendroclimatological reconstructions (Force et al. 2002, Grissino-Mayer 1996, 1997). Five periods from Basketmaker III through Late Bonito.
- **Figure 2**: Construction labor totals (person-hours/1000) for great houses at 5-year increments (from Lekson 1984, p. 263, Fig. 5.2) overlaid with outlier great house counts. Shows peak construction around AD 1080-1100.
- **Figure 3**: Paleoclimatic reconstruction of precipitation for Chaco Canyon region (AD 750-1200), with 10-year moving average. Shows high interannual variability throughout, with generally wetter conditions during Classic Bonito.
- **Table 1**: Framework table showing contexts for emergence and expected behavioral manifestations for both pilgrimage center and pilgrimage activity as costly signals.

## Methodological Notes

- The paper applies CST verbally, not formally. No game-theoretic model, no equilibrium analysis, no quantitative predictions. The framework is conceptual and the case studies are illustrative rather than testing specific predictions.
- The Chaco and Nasca cases are compared qualitatively. The comparison is useful for identifying commonalities but does not constitute a rigorous cross-cultural test.
- The paper does not address: group-level signaling (monuments deterring intergroup conflict), cooperation network formation through signaling, bet-hedging/risk-buffering, or the Spence condition formally. These are the major extensions that the mls-monuments model provides.
- The paleoclimate data in Table 2 are secondary (from Force et al. 2002, Grissino-Mayer 1996, 1997), not original analyses. The precipitation statistics are presented at the period level (50-200 year averages) rather than annual resolution.

## Connections to Other Sources

- **Depends on**: Bliege Bird and Smith 2005 and Boone 1998 for CST criteria; Neiman 1997 for monuments as costly advertising; Roscoe 2009 for group-level signaling (cited but not fully developed); Lekson 1984 for construction labor data; Force et al. 2002 for paleoclimate data.
- **Cited by**: The mls-monuments model cites Kantner & Vaughn's framework but substantially extends it with formal modeling, multi-scale signaling, and environmental feedback.
- **Corroborates**: Watson et al. 2015 (exotic goods and social complexity); Plog & Heitman 2010 (elite status differentiation); LeBlanc's Pax Chaco concept.
- **Conflicts with**: None directly, but the religious authority mechanism differs from the mls-monuments model's economic/environmental mechanism for why signaling intensifies.
