## Core Argument

Drinking vessels at Chaco Canyon document a gradual sequence of change in vessel forms from AD 500 to 1200, followed by a dramatic intensification of drinking activity in the AD 1000s. The most iconic form, the cylinder jar, was concentrated at Pueblo Bonito and likely served as corporate property of a specific social group rather than personal possessions. Cylinder jars contained cacao and/or holly beverages and were used in performative ritual contexts. Their abrupt termination around AD 1100, through deliberate smashing and burning, coincided with the end of the intense construction period and signals the collapse of one dimension of Chacoan hierarchy. After termination, individually owned drinking vessels (mugs from northern traditions) replaced the corporate cylinder jar system.

## Key Data

- **Cylinder jar inventory**: Roughly 200 cylinder jars in Chaco Canyon; approximately 175 from Pueblo Bonito (Table 1). Room 28 contained 112 vessels, Room 39B contained 23. No cylinder jars from kivas.
- **Size shift**: Early cylinder jars (Red Mesa B/W, AD 900s) had average orifice diameter of 15.1 cm (communal); later jars (Gallup/Chaco B/W, post-AD 1030) averaged 9.8 cm (individual) and were crafted in matched sets of 2-4 (Figure 4).
- **Heat retention**: Experimental test (Figure 6) of 1,000 cc vessels: cylinder jar form retained heat significantly longer than bowl or standard jar forms with 237 cc of boiling water.
- **Vessel sequence** (Figure 1): Decorated tecomates/seed jars (pre-AD 700) to gourd jars (AD 650+) to pitchers (AD 700+) to cylinder jars (AD 900-1100) to mugs (AD 1050+).
- **Residue evidence**: Cylinder jars and some mugs contain residue of cacao or holly (Crown et al. 2015).
- **Termination event**: 99 jars placed on shelf in Room 28, sprinkled with turquoise and shell beads, fire lit beneath, room sealed and burned. 10 more smashed on upper-story floor. Event dated to approximately AD 1100.
- **Mortuary context** (Figure 2): Pitchers are common burial accompaniments across age and sex groups. Only one cylinder jar associated with an individual burial (adult female, Skeleton #5/H3663, Room 33).
- **Use-wear**: 54% of cylinder jars show moderate to heavy external use-wear; at least 23% show reslipping/repainting.
- **Production** (Table 2): Most cylinder jars appear to be Cibola White Ware, possibly made in Chaco Canyon. Some intrusive jars from Chuska, Showlow, Flagstaff, and other areas.

## Methodological Notes

Crown draws on cross-cultural ethnographic and archaeological literature on drinking practices to interpret Chaco vessel forms. Vessel function is identified through physical attributes (form, orifice size, handles), analogies with traditional vessel use in the Southwest/Northwest, and residue analysis. The analysis integrates data from Pepper (1920), Judd (1954), Akins (1986), Crown and Wills (2003), and recent reexcavation of Room 28.

## Relevance to Costly Signaling Model

**Layer 1 (Individual signaling)**: Cacao and holly, imported from Mesoamerica, represent individual-level exotic goods signals. Their consumption in ritually charged settings at Pueblo Bonito constitutes a costly display: the ingredients required long-distance procurement networks (1,800+ km for cacao), specialized knowledge for preparation, and access to the exclusive ritual setting. The shift from communal to individual cylinder jars around AD 1030 indicates increasing competition for differentiation among participants in drinking rituals.

**Layer 2 (Intergroup assessment)**: Outsiders brought cylinder jars to Pueblo Bonito from distant communities (Chuska, Showlow), demonstrating that the ritual attracted participants from across the Chaco system. The jars remained at Pueblo Bonito, concentrating signaling capital at the center. Cylinder jar consumption likely involved processions and performative activities near the West Court (Mills 2008:105), making them visible community-scale displays.

**Layer 3 (Cooperation networks)**: The provision of exotic drinks to participants from outside the canyon would have created social debt (Dietler 1990; Henderson and Joyce 2006:141). Cylinder jar rituals thus served as mechanisms for building and maintaining cooperation networks, consistent with the model's prediction that signaling investment generates network benefits.

**System collapse**: The abrupt termination of cylinder jars around AD 1100 provides a precise chronological marker for the breakdown of one signaling channel. The return to individually owned vessels (mugs) represents a shift from corporate to individual identity expression, consistent with fragmentation of the cooperative system.
