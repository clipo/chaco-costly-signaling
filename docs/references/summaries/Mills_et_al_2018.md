## Relevance to Argument

This paper tracks the evolution of social networks across the Chaco World at 50-year intervals from AD 800 to 1200, providing the empirical foundation for understanding how cooperation networks (Layer 3) formed, expanded, and contracted during the Chaco phenomenon. The finding that Chaco's earliest connections ran to the south and west (not north) and that peak network connectivity coincided with peak monument construction (AD 1050-1100) supports the model's prediction that signaling investment and cooperation network value co-evolved. The identification of persistent network boundaries (the San Juan River gap) and their eventual bridging via specific infrastructure (the Great North Road) provides spatial structure for modeling how signaling systems expand.

## Data Presented

Ceramic similarity networks derived from 1.9 million sherds across 325 nodes (settlements with 30+ identified sherds) at 50-year intervals from AD 800 to 1200. Brainerd-Robinson similarity measures define weighted networks. Eigenvector centrality scores calculated for each node in each interval. Community-detection via walktrap algorithm (5-step random walks). Additional analysis of smudged ware distributions (unpainted technology) as independent evidence. All site data from Chaco Social Networks database (Peeples et al. 2016) supplemented by Chaco Landscapes Project data (Van Dyke et al. 2016).

## Methodological Notes

The Bayesian chronological apportioning method (Ortman 2016) provides systematic temporal control but introduces uncertainty in assigning ceramics to specific 50-year intervals. The 30-sherd minimum threshold is based on prior resampling analyses showing stability above this level. The Brainerd-Robinson similarity measure captures overall assemblage similarity but does not distinguish between different mechanisms of similarity (trade, migration, emulation, shared cultural heritage). The binarization of networks for visualization (upper quartile of similarity scores) may obscure important variation in tie strength. The walktrap community-detection algorithm is sensitive to step length; 5 steps were used based on prior work.

## Connections to Other Sources

- **Mills et al. 2013**: Earlier SWSN paper covering AD 1200-1450, documenting post-Chaco network transformation. Mills et al. 2018 extends the analysis backward to cover the Chaco period itself.
- **Dungan et al. 2018**: Companion paper on great house visibility. Together they show that great houses were both visually prominent and central to network connectivity.
- **Gauthier 2021**: Climate-structured interaction networks post-AD 1300. Mills et al. 2018 provides the pre-AD 1200 network structure that Gauthier's analysis extends.
- **Guiterman et al. 2015**: Timber sourcing data independently confirm the south/west connections identified by ceramic networks (Zuni Mountains sourcing for early Pueblo Bonito timber).
- **Plog & Heitman 2010**: Cited for evidence of early inequality at Pueblo Bonito. PDF not available in repository.
- **Kennett et al. 2017**: Cited (p. 930) for matrilineal descent evidence at Pueblo Bonito. PDF not available in repository.
- **Wills 2017**: Labor recruitment model compatible with the network expansion patterns documented here.
