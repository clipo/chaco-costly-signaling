## Core Argument

Van Dyke argues that the monumental architecture of Chaco Canyon was constructed to convey, reinforce, and challenge ideas about social, ritual, and cosmological order. Using the concept of "social memory," the paper examines how architecture was employed in the transformation of Chacoan society at the beginning of the Late Bonito phase (A.D. 1100-1140). During the preceding century, Classic Bonito phase architecture expressed basic tenets of a Chacoan worldview: directionality, balanced dualism, and the canyon as a center place. At the onset of the Late Bonito phase, confidence in the Chacoan ritual order was shaken by environmental and social developments. Leaders sought to re-formalize Chaco as a center place by instituting a new building scheme. Six new great houses were positioned on the landscape in a patterned, nested series of oppositional relationships, legitimated through direct alignments and indirect architectural references to the Classic Bonito past. The new buildings were meant to bolster confidence in leaders and to attract followers by offering a combination of the familiar and the novel.

## Key Data

1. **Late Bonito Great Houses** (Table 1, p. 419): Six structures built A.D. 1100-1140 in McElmo style masonry. Casa Chiquita (34 rooms, 1-3 stories), Headquarters Site A (39 rooms, 1 story), Kin Kletso (57 rooms, 2-3 stories), New Alto (32 rooms, 1-2 stories), Tsin Kletsin (55 rooms, 1-2 stories), Wijiji (104 rooms, 2-3 stories).

2. **Construction labor**: McElmo "Class II" construction required 55,000-90,000 person-hours, approximately one-third to one-half the labor of Classic Bonito "Class IV" construction (170,000-192,000 person-hours) (p. 423, citing Lekson 1986).

3. **Illusory architectural features**: Unfaced parallel walls along south sides of Casa Chiquita and Kin Kletso created the visual impression of greater mass. Internal crosswalls turned the space between walls into functionless 150 x 50 cm cells (p. 423).

4. **Spatial relationships**: Four Late Bonito houses on the canyon floor, two on the rim. Six houses form north-south axes referencing Classic Bonito alignments. Three houses (Casa Chiquita, Headquarters Site A, Wijiji) deliberately lack line-of-sight to any other great house (p. 426).

5. **Competition with Aztec**: Construction at Salmon Ruin began A.D. 1088; Aztec construction in the same decade. By the late 1100s, "Aztec won" (p. 426).

## Relevance to Signaling Model

Van Dyke 2004 contributes to the signaling model in five ways:

1. **Signal depreciation and renewal** (Layer 2): The Late Bonito building program represents an attempt to restore declining signal credibility. When Classic Bonito signals lost effectiveness (due to drought, social upheaval, and competition from Aztec), leaders invested in new construction that referenced the old while introducing novel elements. This maps onto a signal depreciation function where signal effectiveness decays and must be renewed through fresh investment.

2. **Cost reduction strategies**: The shift from Classic Bonito construction (170,000-192,000 person-hours) to McElmo-style construction (55,000-90,000 person-hours) documents a reduction in the cost parameter C. The use of standardized templates and illusory tricks (false walls) represents cost-saving innovation that maintains signal appearance while reducing actual investment.

3. **Signal honesty and deception**: The illusory architectural features (false parallel walls, unfaced crosswalls creating non-functional cells) are direct evidence of attempts to inflate signal value relative to actual cost. This raises questions about signal honesty: if buildings were designed to appear larger than they were, the Spence condition may have been weakening.

4. **Intergroup competition** (Layer 2): The simultaneous construction at Chaco and Aztec represents direct signaling competition between rival centers for the same regional audience. The model predicts that competing signalers should increase investment in the face of competition; the Late Bonito building burst, despite reduced labor capacity, is consistent with this prediction.

5. **System collapse**: The failure of the building program within a generation, with some buildings left incomplete, illustrates the model's prediction that signaling systems collapse when cost-benefit ratios become unfavorable. The incomplete structures provide physical evidence for the abruptness of the transition.
