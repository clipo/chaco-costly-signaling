# Summary: Hawkes & Bliege Bird 2002

**Full citation**: Hawkes, K., & Bliege Bird, R. L. (2002). Showing off, handicap signaling, and the evolution of men's work. *Evolutionary Anthropology*, 11(2), 58-67.

## Relevance to Argument

Hawkes & Bliege Bird 2002 establishes the theoretical and empirical foundation for the "show-off hypothesis," which explains individually costly behavior (hunting large game, wide sharing) as competitive signaling. Three contributions are directly relevant to the project: (1) the demonstration that costly signaling resolves collective action problems without reciprocity; (2) the distinction between wasteful and altruistic signals, with the argument that altruistic signals (which provide direct benefits to audiences) are favored by both signalers and receivers; (3) Zahavi's observation that "social prestige functions like a peacock's tail: it attracts collaborators and deters rivals," which captures the dual function underlying Layers 2 and 3 of the project model.

## Data Presented

The paper synthesizes ethnographic data from multiple forager societies:
- **Ache** (eastern Paraguay): ~50 hrs/week in food acquisition; better hunters have higher fertility (Hill & Hurtado 1996); hunter does not control meat distribution (Hill & Kaplan 1988)
- **Hadza** (northern Tanzania): big-game specialists; no reciprocity in meat sharing (R=0.216, p=0.39 for kg shared vs. received); better hunters married to harder-working wives
- **Meriam Islanders** (Torres Strait): turtle hunters have higher reproductive success; no reciprocity in turtle sharing (R=0.102, p=0.44); turtle = 80% of calories transferred between households during nesting season
- **!Kung** (Kalahari): better hunters spend more time hunting; self-effacing presentation of kills
- **Chimpanzees** (Gombe, Ngogo): hunting increases when mating competition is high; males who share meat share coalitionary support

## Methodological Notes

The paper uses a comparative approach across multiple forager societies to evaluate competing explanations (reciprocal altruism vs. costly signaling) for food sharing. The quantitative data on reciprocity (correlations between sharing given and received) provide particularly strong evidence against the reciprocity hypothesis. Box 2 presents the statistical analyses clearly with correlation coefficients and p-values.
