# Summary: Van Dyke et al. 2024

**Full citation:** Van Dyke, Ruth M., K.E. Primeau, Kellam Throgmorton, and David E. Witt. 2024. Seashells and Sound Waves: Modelling Soundscapes in Chacoan Great-House Communities. *Antiquity* 98(399):777-794.

**DOI:** 10.15184/aqy.2024.54

---

## Relevance to Argument

This paper provides novel evidence that great houses functioned as acoustic broadcast platforms. The consistent mapping of conch-shell trumpet audibility zones onto habitation site distributions across five independent communities supports the interpretation that great houses served as centers for community-wide signaling. While the authors focus on literal acoustic communication, the findings are directly relevant to the costly signaling framework in two ways.

First, conch-shell trumpets are themselves long-distance exotic goods (procured from the Pacific Ocean, ~1000 km), placing them in the same category as turquoise, macaws, and cacao as costly imported items. Their concentration at Chaco Canyon (17 of 46 known Southwest specimens) and association with the most elaborate burial at Pueblo Bonito (Room 33) connects them to elite status signaling.

Second, the acoustic reach of a trumpet blast from a great house defines a community boundary. The consistent ~1.4 km range maps onto the spatial scale of a great house community, suggesting that great houses were designed or positioned, in part, to maximize their acoustic command over surrounding settlements. This is functionally equivalent to the signaling model's Layer 2 prediction: great houses broadcast information about group capacity to surrounding communities, and habitation placement within the broadcast zone indicates community membership or affiliation. The non-random placement of habitation sites within audibility zones (p <= 0.001 at multiple communities) provides quantitative support for this interpretation.

## Data Presented

- Soundshed modelling parameters (Table 1): 96dB source, 6ft height, 330Hz, 30% humidity, 55.4F, 20.7dB ambient
- Soundshed raster maps for all five communities (Figures 2, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9)
- Statistical tests: Fisher's exact test for each community (Table S1); chi-square tests for cross-community comparison (Tables S2, S3); ancestral vs. scion comparison (Tables S4, S5); ERF audibility (Table S6)
- Viewshed-soundshed comparison for Padilla Wash (Figure 10)
- Lidar data: BLM Farmington, 1m resolution
- Community site location data for five communities

## Methodological Notes

- Soundshed Analysis Tool v.0.9.3 (Primeau 2022) within the Archaeoacoustics Toolbox for ArcGIS
- Parameters model dawn conditions on summer solstice (conservative for audibility estimation)
- Interaudibility classified into five categories: passively audible, actively audible, likely passively audible, likely actively audible, inaudible
- Conservative approach: only passively and actively audible classified as "audible" for analysis
- Modelling limitation: represents sound as originating from a single 1x1m point, regardless of actual building footprint
- Five communities chosen based on availability of lidar data and habitation site location data
- All communities in northern half of San Juan Basin; western and southern areas lack sufficient lidar
