## Relevance to Argument

This paper provides critical environmental baseline data for the Chaco signaling model. It documents progressive, anthropogenic deforestation of Chaco Canyon's pinyon-juniper woodland from ~600 BC through the Bonito phase. The evidence shows that the resource base supporting the Chacoan system was severely degraded before and during the period of peak signaling investment (great house construction), meaning: (1) construction timber had to be imported from 75+ km away by AD 850, increasing the cost parameter C; (2) daily fuel demands further stressed the woodland, creating a feedback loop between resource extraction and environmental vulnerability; (3) environmental uncertainty (sigma) was partly endogenous, amplified by the signaling-related activities themselves.

## Data Presented

- **Pollen profiles** (Fig 5): From Operations C-1/3 (top) and C-2 (bottom), showing arboreal and non-arboreal pollen percentages across time horizons from ~700 BC to AD 1700-1900. Juniper declines dramatically; disturbance taxa (Ambrosia, Solidago) increase.
- **Table 1**: Complete macrobotanical inventory from 2013-2015 excavations, including charcoal weights, ubiquity percentages, and chronological assignments. Covers seeds (Amaranthus, Atriplex, Chenopodium, Portulaca, Verbena) and charcoal (juniper, pine, hardwood, elderberry, sumac, cottonwood, willow).
- **Stratigraphic profiles** (Figs 3, 4): Operations C-1/C-3 and C-2 showing pollen sample locations, OSL dates, and radiocarbon dates, providing chronological control for the pollen sequence.
- **SEM micrographs** (Fig 6): Scanning electron micrographs of carbonized wood specimens (Pinus edulis, Juniperus monosperma, Rhus sp.), demonstrating identification methodology.
- **Maps** (Figs 1, 2): Regional context (San Juan Basin) and detailed hillshade of excavation area at confluence of Chaco and Escavada Washes.

## Methodological Notes

- Pollen analysis: 200 pollen grain counts per sample from 21 soil samples; processed at Texas A&M Pollen Laboratory. Lycopodium spore tracers used for concentration estimates.
- Macrobotanical analysis: 37 samples recovered by dry screening (0.25 inch mesh); charcoal identified by light microscopy and environmental SEM (Philips XL30 ESEM) using transverse and tangential sections.
- Dating: Combination of AMS radiocarbon and optically stimulated luminescence (OSL). Chronological assignments range from ~3100-3700 BC (earliest conifer charcoal) to AD 1676-1926 (most recent elderberry charcoal).
- Excavation locations: Seven operations targeting irrigation canals, check dams, rincon drainages, and agricultural terraces in and around Chaco Wash floodplain. Conducted 2013-2015 under NPS permit 15-CHCU-01.
- The study challenges Wills et al. (2014), who argued there is "no convincing evidence" for anthropogenic deforestation. Lentz et al. counter with new pollen and macrobotanical data showing the decline began before the droughts Wills invoked.

## Connections to Other Sources

- **Depends on**: Guiterman et al. 2016 (timber sourcing shift to Zuni/Chuska Mountains); Samuels and Betancourt 1982 (fuelwood consumption rates); Toll 1985, 1987 (Pueblo Alto archaeobotany); Dean et al. 2002 (dendroclimatology); Scarborough et al. 2018 (water management and erosion data from same field project).
- **Responds to**: Wills et al. 2014, who proposed deforestation was drought-driven, not anthropogenic. Lentz et al. directly counter this with their three-pronged argument (decline during mesic periods, survival in adjacent areas, prior drought tolerance).
- **Corroborates**: Betancourt and Van Devender 1981 (packrat midden evidence for PJW in early Chaco); Hall 1977 (Late Quaternary vegetation history); Kohler and Matthews 1988 (Anasazi land use and forest reduction at Mesa Verde).
- **Extends**: The deforestation findings complement the construction chronology data in Guiterman et al. 2015/2016 by showing that resource depletion was driven more by fuel consumption than by construction timber extraction.
- **Conflicts with**: Wills et al. 2014, who argued there was no direct proof of anthropogenic deforestation.
