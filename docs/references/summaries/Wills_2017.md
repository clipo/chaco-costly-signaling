## Overview

Wills presents a comprehensive reassessment of the archaeological evidence for water management and agricultural intensification at Chaco Canyon during the Bonito Phase (ca. AD 850-1200). Drawing on recent University of New Mexico fieldwork, archival records, GIS modeling, and re-examination of previously documented features, he concludes that the evidence for water control systems linked to agricultural surplus production is far weaker than conventionally assumed. The paper proposes an alternative political economy based on labor transactions and recruitment to corporate land-holding groups, rather than elite control of food surpluses generated through irrigation technology.

## Key Arguments

The paper examines five categories of water control features: (1) large artificial channels/canals, (2) gridded field systems, (3) small runoff collection systems (headgates), (4) terraces and retention basins, and (5) check dams. For each category, Wills finds the evidence substantially less compelling than the received narrative suggests.

Most previously identified canals have been reinterpreted as roads or natural channels. The only confirmed large artificial channel, the Pueblo Bonito diversion channel, shows sedimentology consistent with flashy high-energy flows rather than irrigation, and no headgates or agricultural plots are associated with it. The Chetro Ketl gridded field system, the signature water control feature at Chaco, postdates most of the Bonito Phase (after ca. AD 1100) and sits on sodium-rich clay soils considered poor for maize. The 18 documented headgate complexes are energetically trivial (less material than a single great house room) and appear to date late in the Classic Bonito Phase (after ca. AD 1075). Terracing is extremely limited (about 2 ha), and confirmed reservoirs and check dams are rare.

## Relevance to Costly Signaling Model

This paper is important for the costly signaling framework in several ways:

1. **Undermines the surplus-extraction model**: If great house construction was not financed by agricultural surpluses generated through water control, alternative explanations for the investment are needed. The costly signaling model provides one: great houses as signals that attract labor and cooperation partners.

2. **Supports a labor-recruitment political economy**: Wills explicitly argues that great house corporate groups grew through labor recruitment rather than surplus extraction. This aligns with Layer 3 of the signaling model (cooperation networks), where signaling investment attracts exchange partners.

3. **Late timing of water control features**: The finding that headgates and the Chetro Ketl field system postdate most great house construction (post-AD 1075-1100) is consistent with a model where water control was a reactive response to environmental stress rather than the economic foundation for monumental architecture.

4. **Dispersed extensive farming**: The characterization of Chaco agriculture as spatially extensive, risk-minimizing, and organized through corporate land-tenure groups provides context for the model's sigma parameter (environmental uncertainty). Under dispersed farming, the agricultural base is large but inherently uncertain, making cooperation networks (Layer 3) more valuable.

5. **Spatial concentration of great houses**: The finding that 90% of great house architecture is within 20 km of Pueblo Bonito constrains the spatial scale of the signaling model.

## Key Data Points

- Estimated cultivated area: ca. 2% (30 ha or less) of canyon floor (Loose and Lyons 1976:146)
- Chetro Ketl field complex: 2-3 ha with 42+ grids averaging 300 m2 each
- Hungo Pavi watershed: 1,190 ha, capable of supporting 175-250 people (Dorshow 2012)
- Total agricultural catchment (8-10 km radius around great houses): 11,000+ km2
- Only 1 confirmed reservoir in Chaco (at Tsin Kletzin, 1,200 m3 capacity)
- 15-18 documented headgate complexes in the Chaco Core
- Maize cultivation in canyon from at least 1200 BC (Simmons 1986)
- Great house architecture concentration: 90%+ within 20 km of Pueblo Bonito

## Limitations

The paper relies heavily on negative evidence (absence of confirmed water control features), which is inherently less conclusive than positive evidence. Wills acknowledges this, noting that ongoing fieldwork may reveal additional features. The alternative political economy model (labor recruitment through corporate land-holding) is proposed verbally without formal testing. Some productivity estimates depend on GIS models (Dorshow 2012) whose assumptions are not fully evaluated in this paper.
