## Overview

This dissertation is the only study other than Kantner and Vaughn (2012) to directly apply costly signaling theory (CST) to Chaco Canyon outlier great houses. Safi examines three Pueblo II period (PII) great houses in the southern Cibola sub-region (Largo Gap, Cox Ranch Pueblo, and Cerro Pomo), located approximately 11-30 km apart near Zuni Salt Lake, roughly 150 km south of Chaco Canyon. The study asks whether these great houses represent costly signals of competitive ability by local aspiring leaders, and how this model compares with three alternative accounts: Chaco-directed outpost, Chaco emulation, and Chaco as ideological system.

## Theoretical Framework

Safi draws primarily on Neiman (1997), Bliege Bird and Smith (2005a), and Kantner (1996) to construct a verbal model of great house construction as costly signaling. She does not develop a formal mathematical model. Her framework proposes that great houses functioned as costly signals at two levels simultaneously (Table 3.1, p. 50):

1. **Internal audience**: Aspiring leaders signal to potential community members to attract/retain them, offering access to local prestige, economic/ritual/political resources, and potential marriage partners. Community members respond by contributing labor to construction, maintenance, or resource procurement.

2. **External audience**: Great house leaders signal competitive qualities to rival great house leaders, enabling accurate assessment of competitive ability. This facilitates alliance formation, avoidance of unwinnable conflicts, and access to non-local prestige, resources, and exchange networks.

Safi identifies three viewer responses to competitive signals (from Neiman 1997:270, p. 48): (1) accurate interpretation leading to investment in the signaler; (2) recognition of superiority leading to reduced competition; (3) escalation through increased signal investment. These responses correspond to our model's Layer 1 (attraction of members), Layer 2 (conflict deterrence), and the signaling arms race dynamic.

A critical interpretive move: Safi argues that great houses serve as "stable, physical signal[s] of ability that could be reinforced by regular costly displays" (p. 59). The building is a permanent advertisement; feasting and ritual activities are periodic credibility-enhancing displays.

## Methodology

### Data Expectations

Safi derives 17 testable data expectations to distinguish costly signaling from alternative models (Table 3.2, pp. 60-61). Some expectations are unique to CST, while others are shared with one or more alternative models. The number of models distinguished from CST by each expectation ranges from 0 to 2. Expectations unique to or most diagnostic for CST include:

- Prominent landscape placement of great houses (expectation 7)
- Unique networks of household exchange not replicated at the great house (expectation 17)
- Unique patterns of obsidian procurement at each great house (expectation 16)

### Methods Applied

- **Full-coverage survey** of publicly accessible BLM lands around all three great houses (2002-2014)
- **Excavations** at Largo Gap great house (original fieldwork)
- **Ceramic analysis**: Classification of decorated and utility wares from 124 sites across three communities (91 with 20+ painted sherds)
- **Correspondence analysis**: Temporal seriation of ceramic assemblages
- **Technological analysis**: Utility ware manufacturing techniques (coil count, indentation patterns, thickness) to identify distinct traditions
- **INAA (Instrumental Neutron Activation Analysis)**: Compositional analysis of ceramics to define production groups and identify non-local trade items
- **XRF (X-Ray Fluorescence)**: Obsidian sourcing for 168 samples from Largo Gap and Cox Ranch Pueblo communities
- **Population estimates**: Momentary population based on room counts and assumed occupation spans

### Study Region

Southern Cibola, located at the southern periphery of the Chacoan sphere, approximately 150 km south of Chaco Canyon. Three great house communities studied, each within 3-8 km extent, with three great houses spaced 11-30 km apart. Up to seven PII great houses may exist in the sub-region.

## Key Findings

### Architecture and Construction

- Largo Gap: ~22 rooms, D-shaped plan, bounded plaza, blocked-in kiva, Type II masonry, compound walls, multiple remodeling episodes, visually prominent location (p. 229, Table 6.14)
- Cox Ranch Pueblo: ~44 rooms, rectangular, bounded plaza, internal kiva, Type II masonry, core-and-veneer walls, high-elevation timbers (spruce/fir from White Mountains, ~80 km), over-tall rooms (p. 170, Table 6.14)
- Cerro Pomo: ~43 rooms, rectangular, external kiva, foundation trench, footing stones, some Type II masonry, entrance road, large bermed depression (p. 229, Table 6.14)
- All three display Chacoan architectural features to varying degrees; variability appears to correlate with timing of construction (later structures have more Chacoan features, suggesting a signaling escalation dynamic)

### Population

- Momentary population estimates (Table 6.2, p. 162): Largo Gap 131-273; Cerro Pomo 73-168; Cox Ranch Pueblo 106-237
- None reproductively viable in isolation if 475-person threshold applies; all viable at 75-100-person minimum
- Aggregate population of three communities: 310-678, approaching viability
- Implication: inter-community connections (potentially facilitated by signaling) were necessary for demographic viability

### Ceramic Evidence

- Two manufacturing traditions (Mogollon brown ware, Pueblo gray ware) at all three communities, indicating multi-ethnic settlement (pp. 153-154)
- Great houses have higher proportional use of visually distinctive smudged brown and red wares compared to community sites dominated by white wares (pp. 151-155)
- Correspondence analysis confirms all three great houses and communities were roughly contemporaneous during PII occupation, a necessary condition for competitive signaling (pp. 165-169)

### Exchange Networks

- INAA identified two local production groups (SCib1, SCib2) and non-local ceramics from 7 sub-regional core groups (p. 215)
- 56 local sherds of all four ware types met criteria for membership in non-local core groups, found among all three communities (p. 215)
- Non-local ceramics not restricted to great house contexts; distributed across communities (p. 223)
- Approximately equal numbers of non-local core sherds at each great house (~11-15 each, p. 223)
- Great houses do not appear to have managed exchange networks for their communities (pp. 223, 257)
- Each great house has unique obsidian procurement patterns (Table 6.13, p. 226): Largo Gap community dominated by Red Hill (73%), while Cox Ranch and Cerro Pomo used more southern (Mule Creek) sources
- Obsidian from multiple sources (3-6 unique sources per great house) interpreted as symbolic display of diverse external connections (pp. 227-228)

### Model Evaluation

- Of 17 CST data expectations, 11 supported at community scale, 3 supported for individual great houses (p. 228)
- Chaco-directed outpost model: least supported; little material evidence linking canyon populations to non-canyon communities (p. 261)
- Chaco emulation model: partially supported but no driving mechanism for observed exchange patterns (p. 260)
- Ideological system model: partially supported, cannot be clearly distinguished from CST with available data (p. 260)
- CST model: most broadly supported, particularly by architectural variation, multi-ethnic settlement, feasting evidence, and decentralized exchange networks (p. 271)

## Relationship to Our Three-Layer Model

### What Safi's model shares with ours

- Multi-level signaling structure (internal: attract members; external: deter rivals/attract allies)
- Recognition that signal investment is quality-dependent (variation in architectural elaboration reflects variation in competitive ability)
- Prediction that signals attract followers and facilitate exchange relationships
- Emphasis on conflict avoidance as a benefit of signaling
- Architecture as both stable signal and platform for periodic credibility-enhancing displays (feasting, ritual)

### Where our model advances beyond Safi

1. **Formalization**: Safi's model is entirely verbal. Ours specifies equilibrium conditions (Spence condition), endogenous signaling returns (lambda(sigma)), and separating equilibria.
2. **Environmental uncertainty**: Safi explicitly rejects environmental instability as a driver of great house construction (p. 56), arguing construction occurs during productive periods. Our model incorporates environmental uncertainty (sigma) as a parameter that modulates signaling returns, making construction more valuable (not just more affordable) under uncertainty.
3. **Three distinct layers**: Safi conflates what we separate into three layers. She does not model intergroup assessment as a distinct mechanism (our Layer 2) or cooperation network formation as a distinct outcome (our Layer 3).
4. **Quantitative predictions**: Safi's 17 data expectations are qualitative (+/-). Our model generates quantitative predictions about construction timing, investment levels, and system dynamics.
5. **Dynamic model**: Safi treats signaling as a static equilibrium. Our model captures temporal dynamics: escalation, environmental feedback, and collapse.
6. **Exotic goods**: Safi focuses exclusively on great house architecture. Our model incorporates exotic goods as a second signaling channel operating at the individual level within great houses.

### Where Safi provides data our model needs

- Population estimates for outlier communities (calibration data for carrying capacity parameter)
- Empirical evidence on exchange network structure (test data for Layer 3 predictions)
- Evidence on timing of great house construction relative to community formation
- Architectural feature data for operationalizing signal investment
- Evidence that exchange networks are decentralized (constrains model structure)

## Key Limitations

- No formal model: all predictions are verbal and qualitative
- Small sample: three great houses in one sub-region
- Many data expectations shared across models, limiting discriminating power
- Population estimates depend on untestable assumptions about room use-life
- Ceramic seriation provides only relative chronology
- INAA and XRF sample sizes are small for definitive conclusions about exchange patterns
- Environmental analysis is limited; does not engage directly with paleoclimate data (PDSI)
- No simulation or formal modeling to derive expected patterns

## Assessment

Safi's dissertation is the most detailed empirical application of CST to Chaco outlier great houses. The multi-scale signaling framework and systematic comparison with alternative models are significant contributions. However, the entirely verbal nature of the model limits the precision of predictions and the ability to distinguish CST from alternatives. The empirical findings, particularly the decentralized exchange networks, multi-ethnic settlement, and evidence for feasting at great houses, provide important test data for our formal model. The finding that environmental productivity does not drive construction timing requires engagement in our manuscript, where we argue that environmental uncertainty (not current productivity) modulates signaling returns.
