## Claims

### Claim 1

**Statement**: The Southwest Social Networks (SWSN) database compiled more than 4.3 million decorated (painted) ceramics from more than 700 archaeological sites and more than 4,800 obsidian artifacts from 140 sites, covering 334,000 km-squared of the western Southwest from AD 1200 to 1450.
**Page**: p. 5785 (Methods/Dataset section)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Compiled from published sources, unpublished sources, museum reanalysis, and field analysis
**Depends on**: Data quality of contributing collections
**Notes**: This is the foundational dataset also used by Gauthier 2021 (SWSN v1.0). The scale (4.3 million artifacts, 700+ sites) makes it the most comprehensive regional interaction dataset for the prehistoric Southwest. Gauthier 2021 uses a version with ~500 sites aggregated to 10 km grid cells covering five 50-year intervals from 1200-1450 CE.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: The dataset is unprecedented in scale for Southwestern archaeology. Its comprehensiveness supports robust network analysis.

---

### Claim 2

**Statement**: Social networks were transformed dramatically across the region at previously undocumented spatial, temporal, and social scales, with a dramatic shift in network density and settlement centrality from the northern to the southern Southwest after AD 1300.
**Page**: p. 5785 (abstract)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Ceramic similarity networks and eigenvector centrality analysis across five 50-year time periods
**Depends on**: Ceramic assemblage data; network construction methodology
**Notes**: The north-to-south shift after AD 1300 corresponds to the post-Chaco reorganization. The Chaco system operated primarily in the northern Southwest; its collapse led to network fragmentation in the north and network growth in the south.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: The finding is well-supported by the large dataset and multiple analytic approaches (ceramic similarity, obsidian sourcing, centrality measures).

---

### Claim 3

**Statement**: The largest connected component (LCC) of the ceramic network shifted from the northern to the southern Southwest after AD 1300. The proportion of all settlement nodes in the LCC increased from 25% (AD 1300-1350) to 51% (AD 1400-1450), though the total number of nodes decreased.
**Page**: Table 2 (p. 5787)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Table 2 network topology metrics; binarized networks at 75% similarity threshold
**Depends on**: Ceramic similarity calculation; binarization threshold
**Notes**: Table 2 data: AD 1200-1250 LCC = 109 nodes (39%), AD 1250-1300 = 125 (40%), AD 1300-1350 = 44 (25%), AD 1350-1400 = 65 (45%), AD 1400-1450 = 23 (51%). Mean degree (k) varies: 46.84, 41.25, 18.05, 42.22, 22.67 across the five periods.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: The quantitative metrics provide clear evidence for the network transformation. The shift from a large northern LCC to a growing southern LCC documents the post-Chaco network reorganization at an unprecedented scale.

---

### Claim 4

**Statement**: Social distance does not always correlate with spatial distance. The Pearson's r between spatial concentration (potential connectivity) and degree centrality (actual social connectivity) never exceeded 0.34 for the study area as a whole. Sites in the southern Southwest showed weaker and sometimes negative correlations.
**Page**: pp. 5787-5788
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Pearson's r analysis of spatial vs. social connectivity; friction-surface-based distance calculations
**Depends on**: Cost-adjusted distance metrics; ceramic similarity-based centrality
**Notes**: The weak spatial-social distance correlation means that factors beyond distance structure social networks. This is consistent with Gauthier 2021's finding that climate regime differences explain additional variance beyond distance. For the model, this implies that network ties are selective rather than purely distance-dependent, consistent with signaling-based partner selection.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: The finding that spatial proximity is a poor predictor of social connectivity is important. It implies that network formation involves active choices, not just proximity-based interaction.

### Claim 5

**Statement**: Mean weighted degree centrality in the northern Southwest showed relatively stable values (0.159-0.237) across all periods, while the southern Southwest showed a marked increase from 0.105 (AD 1250-1300) to 0.497 (AD 1400-1450).
**Page**: Table 1 (p. 5787)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Standardized degree centrality calculations
**Depends on**: Ceramic similarity networks; normalization by number of sites per period
**Notes**: The southern network growth after AD 1300 was driven in part by the spread of Salado polychrome pottery, first made by Kayenta migrants who moved from northeastern Arizona to central Arizona in the AD 1280s. This represents a new integrative tradition that tied diverse communities together.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: The divergent trajectories of northern and southern networks document the fundamental reorganization of social infrastructure after Chaco's collapse.

---

### Claim 6

**Statement**: After AD 1300, a new network subgroup emerged in the south that tied together settlements from multiple valleys. All sites in this component shared high frequencies of Salado polychrome pottery, a widespread tradition first made by small groups of Kayenta immigrants and descendants who moved into central Arizona in the AD 1280s.
**Page**: pp. 5787-5788
**Type**: empirical/interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Network analysis; archaeological evidence for Kayenta migration
**Depends on**: Ceramic type identification; migration evidence
**Notes**: The Salado polychrome phenomenon represents a new form of social integration: a widely shared ceramic tradition that created network ties across previously unconnected areas. This is relevant to the model's Layer 3: after the Chaco-era signaling system collapsed, a different form of integration emerged in the south.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: The connection between Kayenta migration, Salado polychrome spread, and network growth is well-documented. This provides a concrete example of how new signaling/identity systems can replace collapsed ones.

---

### Claim 7

**Statement**: Many archaeologists believe the southern Southwest communities connected by Salado polychrome were diverse, even multiethnic, suggesting that participation in the network promoted integration rather than reflecting preexisting ethnic homogeneity.
**Page**: p. 5789
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: General archaeological literature on Salado communities
**Depends on**: Claim 6
**Notes**: If Salado polychrome participation integrated diverse communities, it functioned as a form of cooperative signaling: sharing a ceramic tradition signaled commitment to a shared social system, facilitating cooperation among otherwise unrelated groups. This is analogous to the pilgrimage-as-costly-signal framework of Kantner & Vaughn 2012.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: The multiethnic interpretation is debated but widely held. If correct, it supports the model's Layer 3 prediction that signaling facilitates cooperation among strangers.

---

### Claim 8

**Statement**: The mean distances among sites with strong social connections are surprisingly high, ranging between 70 and 120 km in all but the final period of analysis.
**Page**: p. 5788
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Distance calculations between strongly connected site pairs
**Depends on**: Cost-adjusted distance matrix; ceramic similarity threshold
**Notes**: Distances of 70-120 km represent multiple days of travel on foot. Maintaining strong ties over these distances requires substantial investment, consistent with the signaling interpretation: only groups genuinely committed to the network would bear the cost of maintaining such distant ties.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: The high mean distance of strong ties is an important finding. It implies that network maintenance was costly, supporting the premise that exchange partnerships represent a form of costly investment.

---

### Claim 9

**Statement**: Obsidian density increased more than 10-fold in many regions after AD 1300, with movement of raw material replacing finished tool circulation. The proportion of sites deviating from a distance-decay model more than doubled after AD 1300 (chi-squared = 10.4448, df = 1, p = 0.001).
**Page**: pp. 5788-5789
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: XRF sourcing of 4,805 obsidian pieces; gravity model comparison; chi-squared test
**Depends on**: Obsidian source identification; distance-decay model
**Notes**: The shift from finished tool to raw material circulation implies a change in exchange organization: from occasional, curated exchange to bulk material movement through established networks. The increase in distance-decay deviations after AD 1300 coincides with the southward shift in ceramic network centrality.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: The obsidian data provide an independent line of evidence confirming the ceramic-based network analysis. The shift in circulation patterns after AD 1300 documents a fundamental change in exchange organization.

---

### Claim 10

**Statement**: Sites where specific obsidian sources are overrepresented have significantly higher ceramic similarity scores with each other, and sites with overrepresented Mule Creek and Cow Canyon obsidian sources were dominated by Salado polychromes.
**Page**: p. 5789
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Cross-tabulation of obsidian source overrepresentation with ceramic similarity
**Depends on**: Claims 6, 9
**Notes**: The convergence between obsidian and ceramic evidence strengthens the conclusion that post-AD 1300 exchange networks were structurally organized rather than opportunistic. The Salado-obsidian correlation suggests that the Salado polychrome network also channeled obsidian exchange.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: The multi-material convergence is methodologically important: two independent material classes (ceramics and obsidian) track the same network structure.

---

### Claim 11

**Statement**: The large southern network that grew after AD 1300, tied together by Salado polychrome, ultimately proved unstable and disintegrated in the AD 1400-1450 period, while the smaller, more fragmented northern networks (Hopi and Zuni) persisted and "in fact still exist today."
**Page**: p. 5789
**Type**: empirical/interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Network topology analysis; historical continuity of Hopi and Zuni
**Depends on**: Complete temporal sequence of network data
**Notes**: The finding that smaller, more fragmented networks proved more sustainable than the large southern network is directly relevant to the model's predictions about signaling system stability. Larger networks may be more vulnerable to cascade failures when conditions change, while smaller, denser networks may be more resilient.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: The persistence of Hopi and Zuni networks vs. collapse of the Salado network is an important observation about network resilience. The model should account for the possibility that very large signaling-based networks are inherently less stable than smaller ones.

---

### Claim 12

**Statement**: Decorated ceramics served multiple social contexts including feasting and religious ceremonies, conveying messages about ideology and group identity. Similarities in assemblages can be generated through local transmission, exchange, emulation, population movement, shared ideologies, and active signaling of social boundaries.
**Page**: p. 5786
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: General archaeological literature
**Depends on**: None (framework statement)
**Notes**: The explicit mention of "active signaling of social boundaries" as one mechanism generating ceramic similarity is noteworthy. This acknowledges that ceramic distributions are not passive reflections of interaction but may reflect deliberate identity signaling.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (as a methodological framing)
**Assessment**: The acknowledgment that ceramic similarity may reflect active signaling supports the project's use of ceramic data as evidence for signaling-based social processes.

## Claim Type Definitions

- **Empirical**: Direct observation or measurement reported by the author
- **Interpretive**: Inference from empirical data; not directly observable
- **Speculative**: Acknowledged by the author as tentative, or basis unclear
- **Methodological**: Claim about how to conduct or interpret research

## Evidence Strength Ratings

- **Strong**: Claim is well-supported by the evidence presented
- **Moderate**: Claim is partially supported; some evidence but gaps remain
- **Weak**: Claim is poorly supported by the evidence presented
- **Assertion without evidence**: No evidence cited; claim is stated as fact without support

## Summary Assessment

Mills et al. 2013 is the foundational paper for the Southwest Social Networks (SWSN) database and provides the first comprehensive formal network analysis of the post-Chaco reorganization (AD 1200-1450). The paper documents the dramatic north-to-south shift in network density and centrality after AD 1300, the emergence of the Salado polychrome-based integrative network, the weak correlation between spatial and social distance, and the ultimate collapse of the large southern network while smaller northern networks (Hopi, Zuni) persisted. For the signaling model, the paper's most important contributions are: (1) the SWSN dataset provides the empirical foundation for testing Layer 3 predictions about cooperation networks, (2) the finding that social distance does not track spatial distance supports signaling-based partner selection, (3) the mean tie distances of 70-120 km imply costly network maintenance, and (4) the differential stability of large vs. small networks has implications for signaling system resilience. The paper does not address costly signaling theory but its data and methods are essential infrastructure for the project.
