## Claims

### Claim 1

**Statement**: "There is no substantive evidence for deforestation at Chaco and no obvious indications that the depopulation of the canyon in the 13th century was caused by any specific cultural practices or natural events." The archaeological record does not support the hypothesized linkage between deforestation and societal collapse at Chaco.
**Page**: p. 1 (abstract)
**Type**: interpretive/critique
**Evidence cited by author**: Review of packrat midden data, pollen records, tree-ring data, fuel use evidence, strontium isotope data, and GIS path-distance analysis
**Depends on**: Comprehensive re-evaluation of multiple lines of evidence
**Notes**: This is the central argument of the paper: the widely accepted "ecocide" narrative for Chaco is not supported by the available evidence.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (the paper demonstrates weaknesses in existing evidence for deforestation but cannot prove deforestation did not occur)
**Assessment**: This is a provocative and important critique that challenges a major element of the collapse narrative. For the signaling model, the implication is that the cost parameter C for construction may not have increased as dramatically due to local resource depletion as other studies suggest. However, Lentz et al. 2021 subsequently presented pollen evidence contradicting several of Wills et al.'s arguments.

### Claim 2

**Statement**: Only two radiocarbon samples from packrat middens date to the entire prehistoric ceramic occupation of the canyon (ca. A.D. 500-1300), and neither was obtained from the area around Pueblo Bonito and surrounding great houses. The paucity of dated material may reflect packrat consumption by the human population rather than tree-cutting.
**Page**: p. 2
**Type**: empirical/critique
**Evidence cited by author**: Refs. 7, 8 (packrat midden studies); Refs. 19, 22 (localized nature of midden evidence)
**Depends on**: Review of packrat midden dating record
**Notes**: Previous researchers attributed the paucity of dated midden material to tree-cutting (refs. 7, 8). Wills et al. propose an alternative: "High rates of predation by the human population is a potential explanation for few dated nests" (p. 2; citing refs. 23, 24 on packrat consumption in the northern Southwest).

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (the critique of sample size is valid; the packrat predation hypothesis is speculative)
**Assessment**: The critique highlights the fragile evidentiary base for deforestation claims. However, the alternative hypothesis (packrats eaten, not trees cut) is itself speculative. The low sample size (n=2) weakens both interpretations.

### Claim 3

**Statement**: Pollen records for Chaco have been interpreted as indicating only scattered trees along cliffs and escarpments above the canyon rather than woodlands within the canyon at any time in the past 2,000 years. Packrat investigators could not estimate prehistoric tree density in the canyon before the Bonito Phase.
**Page**: p. 2
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Refs. 17, 20, 31 (pollen records); Ref. 8 (packrat limitation)
**Depends on**: Pollen analysis from canyon deposits
**Notes**: This directly contradicts the pre-deforestation woodland baseline assumed by other researchers (e.g., Lentz et al. 2021). If there were never dense woodlands in the canyon, there was nothing to "deforest."

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (pollen data are real; interpretation contested by Lentz et al. 2021)
**Assessment**: This claim was subsequently challenged by Lentz et al. 2021, who presented pollen data showing juniper pollen at 20% before 600 B.C. declining to 2% by A.D. 1100. The disagreement centers on interpreting pollen abundance and whether canyon floor vs. escarpment sampling sites are representative.

### Claim 4

**Statement**: Fuel from all time periods at Chaco was dominated by shrubby plant species such as Atriplex and Artemisia, not trees. Dried maize cobs "actually one of the most common types of fuel debris found at Chaco sites" were fragile and less likely to be preserved than wood charcoal and thus their importance is generally underestimated. "There is evidence that residents had access to fuel throughout the canyon occupation but there is no indication that canyon residents were very reliant on trees for fuel at any time."
**Page**: p. 3
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Ref. 30 (National Park Service charcoal studies from the 1970s)
**Depends on**: Charcoal recovery from Chaco archaeological sites
**Notes**: This challenges the assumption that Chaco's fuel economy was tree-dependent. If shrubs and maize cobs were the primary fuel, timber extraction was primarily for construction rather than fuel, reducing the scale of deforestation pressure.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (the NPS charcoal data are from the 1970s; sampling and preservation biases may affect interpretation)
**Assessment**: If correct, this significantly reduces the estimated deforestation pressure. Lentz et al. 2021's estimate of over 1 million cubic meters of fuelwood needed during the Bonito phase would need revision. For the signaling model, this means the endogenous degradation component of sigma may be less significant than Lentz et al. propose.

### Claim 5

**Statement**: The assertion that "all late-period pottery in Chaco Canyon was imported" is not supported by the empirical record. At most 50% of grayware cooking vessels (utility wares) were obtained from sources to the west during the late A.D. 1000s, a percentage similar to that at sites elsewhere in the San Juan Basin, including those closer to modern timber sources. The highest estimate for potential overall import of pottery is ca. 50% in the mid-12th century A.D., and this estimate is uncertain.
**Page**: p. 3
**Type**: empirical/critique
**Evidence cited by author**: Ref. 28 (ceramic sourcing data); Ref. 29 (import estimates)
**Depends on**: Ceramic compositional analysis
**Notes**: This challenges the use of imported ceramics as evidence for local fuel depletion. If ceramic import levels at Chaco were similar to those at other San Juan Basin sites, the imports cannot be attributed to Chaco-specific fuel exhaustion.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: This contextualizes Chaco's ceramic imports within regional patterns, suggesting the imports reflect general exchange patterns rather than local resource stress.

### Claim 6

**Statement**: Great house construction timber included Douglas fir (Pseudotsuga menziesii) and higher-elevation fir (Abies sp.), spruce (Picea sp.), and possibly aspen (Populus tremuloides), in addition to ponderosa pine. Spruce/fir was a consistent part of great house construction throughout the Bonito Phase, representing a third of all timber elements in the 11th century, not a replacement for depleted ponderosa. Ponderosa and spruce/fir covary strongly through time (Spearman's rank correlation: r=0.66, P=0.01), indicating they "were part of a mix or 'recipe' for construction."
**Page**: pp. 3-4 (Table 1)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Tree-ring database; Table 1 (Spearman's rank correlation coefficients); refs. 4, 35, 36
**Depends on**: Tree-ring data from Chaco great houses
**Notes**: The 10 largest ruins required approximately 45,000 high-elevation trees from as far as 75 km away (ref. 37). Spruce/fir was already being used during the Early Bonito Phase (ca. A.D. 860-1020), indicating long-distance timber procurement was not a late response to local depletion.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (statistical analysis of tree-ring data)
**Assessment**: The covariance of ponderosa and spruce/fir argues against the "replacement" hypothesis (spruce/fir substituted as local ponderosa was exhausted). Instead, the mixed-species construction pattern suggests builders had established procurement relationships with distant mountain sources from the beginning. For the signaling model, this means long-distance timber procurement was part of the cost structure from the outset, not an escalating cost.

### Claim 7

**Statement**: Strontium isotope ratios from archaeological timber do not clearly discriminate between Chaco Canyon and Chuska Mountain sources. Despite published claims that most imported ponderosa came from the Chuska Mountains, there is "too much variation among the samples and overlap between the sources" to reliably trace wood to individual geographic locations. Chaco and Chuska radiogenic strontium isotope ratios cannot be fully differentiated from each other, and isotopic ratios from individual archaeological specimens match soil-water samples obtained in the canyon drainage.
**Page**: pp. 5-6
**Type**: empirical/critique
**Evidence cited by author**: Refs. 5, 35 (original strontium studies); Ref. 42 (soil and water samples); Table S1
**Depends on**: Reanalysis of published strontium isotope data with larger sample sizes
**Notes**: The critique is specifically directed at Reynolds et al. 2005 (ref. 5) and English et al. 2001 (ref. 35). The kernel density plots (Fig. 6) show extensive overlap between Chaco and Chuska source signatures.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (the overlap in isotope ratios is clearly demonstrated, but the alternative interpretation that trees grew locally remains unconfirmed)
**Assessment**: This challenges the widely cited claim that timber was transported 75-80 km from the Chuska Mountains. If some ponderosa grew in the Chaco drainage, the transport cost component of construction signaling would be lower than commonly assumed. However, the paper acknowledges that the strontium data alone cannot resolve the question.

### Claim 8

**Statement**: GIS least-cost path analysis identifies areas to the east of Chaco Canyon as least-cost potential ponderosa sources, including two broad zones within its watershed at elevations above 2,500 m. The Los Pinos Mountains to the east, the San Mateo Mountains to the southeast, and the Chuska Mountains to the west all offer least-cost access corridors to ponderosa and spruce/fir stands. The La Plata Mountains to the north are no more costly a source than the northern Chuska Mountains.
**Page**: p. 6
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: ArcGIS Path Distance and Corridor modeling; Fig. 7 (weighted-cost corridor surface)
**Depends on**: Topographic data (slope, elevation, vegetation); modern ponderosa distribution
**Notes**: Areas to the east and south have rapidly rising elevation, reaching timberline within 40-100 km. The Chaco Core sits at an elevation cusp just under 2,000 m, at the lower limits of pinyon and juniper.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (modern vegetation used as proxy for prehistoric distribution; present-day climate differs from Bonito Phase)
**Assessment**: The GIS analysis provides an alternative reconstruction of timber procurement geography. If eastern sources were competitive with or preferable to the Chuska Mountains, the assumed west-to-east timber transport corridor (and its associated political implications) needs revision. For the signaling model, the key question is whether timber transport distance and cost increased over time, as would be predicted if local sources were depleted.

### Claim 9

**Statement**: Construction patterns indicate that overall energy investment in Chaco great houses began to decline in the late A.D. 1000s, before the onset of any documented drought periods. Ponderosa pine and spruce/fir timber were used predominantly during the main period of great house construction between A.D. 1000 and 1117, although most of the dated specimens between A.D. 1095 and 1110 came from a single great house (Pueblo del Arroyo).
**Page**: pp. 3-4 (Fig. 5)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Tree-ring database; Fig. 5 (stacked bar chart of outer ring dates)
**Depends on**: Dendrochronological data
**Notes**: The decline in construction before drought onset contradicts models that attribute construction cessation to drought-induced resource stress.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (dendrochronological data are precise)
**Assessment**: The pre-drought decline in construction is an important observation for the signaling model. It suggests that signaling investment may have been declining for reasons other than environmental stress, perhaps due to declining returns from the signaling system itself (audience loss to competing centers, reduced payoff from cooperation networks).

### Claim 10

**Statement**: The paleoclimate record provides no obvious correlation between drought and societal collapse. The abandonment of the canyon great house community was not abrupt, although the exact process leading to depopulation by the end of the 13th century is unclear. "It is plausible that in the agricultural period the occupation of Chaco Canyon impacted local tree populations through fuel use and building construction, unintentionally destroying agricultural fields, but there simply is no evidence supporting this scenario."
**Page**: p. 7
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Refs. 4, 17 (paleoclimate data and construction patterns)
**Depends on**: Comprehensive review of paleoclimate and archaeological evidence
**Notes**: The authors argue that construction patterns "indicate that overall energy investment in Chaco great houses began to decline in the late AD 1000s, before the onset of any documented drought periods" and "the paleoclimate record provides no obvious correlation between drought and societal collapse" (pp. 3, 7).

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (absence of correlation does not prove no relationship)
**Assessment**: This is a strong critique of simple drought-collapse models, and it has implications for the signaling model. If construction investment declined before drought, the model needs to account for endogenous mechanisms (competition from Aztec, declining returns) in addition to exogenous environmental shock. However, the claim of no correlation between drought and collapse has been contested.

### Claim 11

**Statement**: The Chaco Core and adjacent drainages contain more than 99% of all great house architecture by volume within a 40-km radius of Pueblo Bonito. Overall, the canyon great houses occupy a high-potential agricultural zone that extends into a relatively productive natural resource zone on higher-elevation resource areas to the east. At least 11 great houses extended along 32 km of the canyon, linked by a prehistoric road from Penasco Blanco at the west end to Pueblo Pintado at the eastern end.
**Page**: p. 7 (Fig. 9)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Archaeological Records Management Section data (www.nmhistoricpreservation.org/arms.html); Fig. 9
**Depends on**: Survey data and site distribution analysis
**Notes**: The concentration of great house architecture near Pueblo Bonito (99% within 40 km) is a key spatial observation. Western outliers were "geographically and economically marginal to the concentration of great house communities in Chaco" (p. 7).

**Evidence strength**: Strong (based on comprehensive survey data)
**Assessment**: The extreme spatial concentration of great house architecture supports the signaling model's prediction that monument construction should cluster near the core of the cooperation network. The 40-km radius corresponds roughly to the spatial scale of direct interaction, consistent with Neiman's (1997) finding of 50-km political competition spheres in the Maya case.

### Claim 12

**Statement**: "We do not think Chaco is an example of societal 'ecocide.' Our objection to the hypothesized linkage between deforestation and societal collapse has been popularized... not because that purported connection has been confirmed, when in fact it has not."
**Page**: p. 7
**Type**: critique
**Evidence cited by author**: Ref. 1 (Diamond 2005, Collapse); Ref. 2 (Diamond 2010); Refs. 55, 56 (McAnany and Yoffee 2010, Middleton 2012)
**Depends on**: Entire body of evidence reviewed in the paper
**Notes**: The authors frame their critique as a methodological objection to using Chaco as a cautionary tale for modern society. "Scientists have a responsibility to engage with the public about the way in which their research is used by nonspecialists" (p. 7).

**Evidence strength**: N/A (normative claim about scientific communication)
**Assessment**: The anti-ecocide argument does not directly address the signaling model, but it challenges the premise that endogenous environmental degradation drove system collapse. If Wills et al. are correct, the signaling model's collapse mechanism should focus more on declining returns from signaling investment (audience loss, competitive pressure from Aztec) than on escalating costs from resource depletion.

## Summary Assessment

Wills et al. 2014 provides a systematic critique of the "deforestation and collapse" narrative for Chaco Canyon. The paper challenges several commonly cited lines of evidence: packrat midden data (too few samples, alternative explanation for paucity), pollen records (may indicate no canyon woodland), fuel use (shrubs and maize cobs, not trees), ceramic imports (similar to regional patterns), strontium isotopes (cannot discriminate sources), and construction timber (spruce/fir was always part of the mix, not a late substitute). The paper introduces important data including the Spearman's rank correlation between ponderosa and spruce/fir (r=0.66), the GIS least-cost path analysis identifying eastern sources, and the observation that construction investment declined before documented drought periods.

For the signaling model, the most consequential claim is that construction investment declined before drought onset, suggesting endogenous mechanisms (declining returns, audience loss to Aztec) rather than purely exogenous environmental shock drove the reduction in signaling investment. The paper also challenges the assumption that the cost parameter C increased over time due to resource depletion, though this claim was subsequently contested by Lentz et al. 2021. The disagreement between Wills et al. 2014 and Lentz et al. 2021 represents an active empirical debate that the model should acknowledge rather than adjudicate.
