## Claims

### Claim 1

**Statement**: Chaco's earliest ninth-century networks show interaction with areas to the west and south, rather than migration to the Canyon from the Northern San Juan. There is little evidence that Chaco's origins in the AD 800s corresponded to migration out of the Northern San Juan.
**Page**: pp. 927-929 (Migration scenario 1 results)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Ceramic similarity networks for AD 800-850 and AD 850-900 intervals; community-detection analyses showing distinct northern and southern subgroups; analysis of Woodruff Smudged ware distribution
**Depends on**: Brainerd-Robinson ceramic similarity measures; Bayesian chronological apportioning (Ortman 2016); sample size (325 nodes with 30+ identified sherds from 1.9 million total)
**Notes**: This contradicts the long-standing Northern San Juan migration hypothesis for Chaco's founding. Strong ties are present between Chaco, the Chuska slope, and the Upper Rio Puerco. Price et al. (2016) isotope analysis also found Pueblo Bonito individuals' isotopic signatures did not overlap with northern sites.
**Evidence strength**: Strong

### Claim 2

**Statement**: The San Juan River functioned as a significant connectivity gap between northern and southern components of the Chaco World throughout its history. A prominent gap in relations between the Southern and Northern San Juan persisted from the AD 800s until the eleventh century.
**Page**: pp. 927-929
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Network diagrams for AD 800-1000 (Figures 3-4); community-detection analyses consistently showing northern and southern subgroups
**Depends on**: Ceramic similarity networks
**Notes**: This persistent gap challenges models that assume continuous north-south integration from Chaco's founding.
**Evidence strength**: Strong

### Claim 3

**Statement**: The establishment of ties across the San Juan River gap occurred via Aztec Ruins and neighboring sites in the late AD 1000s and 1100s, supporting bi-directional migration between Chaco Canyon and the Middle and Northern San Juan along the Great North Road.
**Page**: p. 931
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Network diagrams for AD 1050-1100 (Figure 7); eigenvector centrality scores; community-detection analyses
**Depends on**: Ceramic similarity networks; construction dating
**Notes**: This corresponds to Migration scenario 2 and coincides with the peak florescence of the Chaco World.
**Evidence strength**: Strong

### Claim 4

**Statement**: During the AD 1050-1100 interval, the Chaco World reached its maximum extent, with more nodes and more connected nodes present throughout the network. The Great North Road and sites along it became highly central for the first time.
**Page**: p. 931
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Figure 7; eigenvector centrality analyses (OSM Tables S1-8)
**Depends on**: Ceramic similarity networks; chronological apportioning
**Notes**: This is the peak connectivity period, coinciding with peak monument construction.
**Evidence strength**: Strong

### Claim 5

**Statement**: After AD 1150, the cluster of Chaco structures near Aztec is not as central to the network as might be expected, although Aztec's ascendancy as the capital of the post-Chaco World is especially evident in the AD 1150-1200 interval. The greatest density of sites with high centrality values remains within Chaco Canyon and to the south and southwest.
**Page**: pp. 934-935
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Figure 8; eigenvector centrality scores (OSM Tables S7-8)
**Depends on**: Ceramic similarity networks
**Notes**: This nuances the narrative of Aztec as a straightforward successor to Chaco. The persistence of southern connectivity is important.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate

### Claim 6

**Statement**: During the AD 1100-1150 interval, community-detection analyses show three communities: the large northern and southern communities divided along the same lines as earlier intervals, plus a third component (shown in yellow) representing sites along the edges of the two larger communities, characterized by transitional ceramic assemblages.
**Page**: p. 933
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Community-detection analysis (walktrap algorithm); Figure 8 (left)
**Depends on**: Ceramic similarity networks; walktrap algorithm parameters (5-step random walks)
**Notes**: The emergence of a transitional community at the boundary is consistent with signaling at network edges. Although the first half of the twelfth century marks the end of the Bonito Phase, there were still many connected great houses and great kivas.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate

### Claim 7

**Statement**: Inequality was part of Chaco's social fabric dating to Pueblo Bonito's earliest construction in the ninth century AD. Several Chaco scholars have explicitly tied this inequality to immigration. Differences between the two major burial clusters at Pueblo Bonito and the contrast between small and great house architecture within the canyon are evidence for the presence of people with disparate origins and identities.
**Page**: p. 923
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Plog & Heitman 2010; Crown & Wills 2018; Akins 1986; Wills 2009
**Depends on**: Archaeological evidence from excavations at Pueblo Bonito
**Notes**: This connects migration to inequality, which is relevant to the signaling model's assumption of quality variance among groups.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate

### Claim 8

**Statement**: The available evidence indicates that the original inhabitants of Chaco Canyon, especially Pueblo Bonito, had stronger ties to settlements to the south and west rather than with the Northern San Juan region. This is supported by ceramic similarity networks, construction timber sourcing (Guiterman et al. 2015), isotopic analysis of human remains (Price et al. 2016), and the distribution of smudged wares.
**Page**: pp. 927-929
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Network analysis results; Guiterman et al. 2015 (timber from Zuni Mountains); Price et al. 2016 (isotopic signatures); Crown 2016 (smudged ware distributions)
**Depends on**: Multiple independent lines of evidence
**Notes**: The convergence of ceramic, isotopic, and timber sourcing data strengthens this interpretation.
**Evidence strength**: Strong

### Claim 9

**Statement**: The analysis uses 467 settlements with a great house and/or great kiva dating between AD 800 and 1200, with 325 nodes having 30 or more identified ceramic sherds (totaling more than 1.9 million sherds). Networks are defined using Brainerd-Robinson similarity on apportioned ceramic type-ware counts at 50-year intervals.
**Page**: pp. 923-925
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Mills et al. 2013a, 2013b, 2015; Peeples & Roberts Jr. 2013; Ortman 2016
**Depends on**: Accuracy of ceramic classification and dating
**Notes**: This represents the largest database of ceramics yet compiled from Chaco great houses and great kivas. The 30-sherd minimum is based on prior resampling analyses. The Bayesian apportioning method (Ortman 2016) provides systematic chronological control.
**Evidence strength**: Strong

### Claim 10

**Statement**: Smudging (found primarily on the interior of unpainted red and brown wares) provides evidence independent of the painted ware networks. Higher than expected proportions of smudged brown and red wares in Pueblo Bonito's assemblage suggest long-distance exchange networks and possible migration histories, with the earliest smudged ware (Woodruff Smudged) produced as early as AD 500 and concentrated in Chaco great houses between AD 800 and 1000.
**Page**: pp. 929-930
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Crown 2016; Hays-Gilpin & van Hartesveldt 1998; Figure 5
**Depends on**: Ceramic identification and dating
**Notes**: The concentration of Woodruff Smudged in great houses and its association with Room 33 elite burials connects this ware to early ritual and status practices. Crown (2016) argues iridescent smudged wares may have been used as mirrors.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate

### Claim 11

**Statement**: Chuskan ceramics (white and grey wares) were first distributed throughout much of the Chaco area, up to 75 km from their production sources, during the AD 850-900 period. This is also when large quantities of ritual objects (burden baskets, ceramic effigies, twined yucca sandals, wooden ritual objects) appear in the archaeological record.
**Page**: p. 929
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Toll 2006; Webster et al. 2014
**Depends on**: Production source identification; dating
**Notes**: The co-occurrence of wide ceramic distribution and ritual object proliferation at AD 850-900 marks a significant expansion of the Chaco interaction sphere.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate

### Claim 12

**Statement**: Migration both preceded and was the result of movement of people at the regional scale, and social network connectivity played a key role. People who study migration recognize that people generally do not move to areas with which they have had no prior social contact.
**Page**: p. 923
**Type**: interpretive / methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Mills et al. 2013a, 2015; Ortman & Cameron 2011
**Depends on**: General migration theory
**Notes**: This principle is relevant to the signaling model because it implies that the expansion of the Chaco network required prior social connections, which could have been established through signaling mechanisms.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate

## Summary Assessment

Mills et al. 2018 provides the most comprehensive dynamic social network analysis of the Chaco World, tracking ceramic similarity networks at 50-year intervals from AD 800 to 1200. The key finding for the costly signaling model is that the Chaco network expanded primarily from south and west connections, not from Northern San Juan migration, and that network connectivity peaked during AD 1050-1100 (the period of maximum monument construction). The persistent connectivity gap defined by the San Juan River and its eventual bridging via the Great North Road and Aztec provide a spatial structure for understanding how signaling-based cooperation networks expanded and eventually contracted. The paper's evidence for early inequality and ritual specialization at Pueblo Bonito supports the signaling model's assumption of quality variance among groups.
