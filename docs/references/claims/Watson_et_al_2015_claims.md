## Claims

### Claim 1

**Statement**: New AMS 14C dates of scarlet macaw (Ara macao) skeletal remains provide the first direct evidence that these Neotropical birds were procured from Mesoamerica by Pueblo people as early as approximately A.D. 900-975. Twelve of the 14 sampled macaws from Pueblo Bonito predate the apogee of architectural expansion (A.D. 1040-1110).
**Page**: p. 8238 (abstract)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Direct AMS 14C dating of 14 macaw skeletal remains
**Depends on**: AMS radiocarbon dating methodology; IntCal13 calibration curve
**Notes**: The 14C dates are calibrated using OxCal version 4.2.3 with IntCal13 curve (p. 8243). The dates are 50-150 years earlier than previously postulated.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (direct dating of artifacts, not contextual association)
**Assessment**: This is a key finding that overturns the previous consensus chronology. The early dates mean exotic goods acquisition preceded, rather than followed, the peak of great house construction, challenging models where monumental architecture generates the social complexity required for long-distance exchange.

### Claim 2

**Statement**: A group of six macaws from Room 38 at Pueblo Bonito have 2-sigma (SE) ranges that fall between A.D. 885 and 990. Their uncalibrated 14C ages are consistent with a single procurement event (chi-squared = 5.59; df = 5; P > 0.05). These are among the earliest scarlet macaws known in the Southwest.
**Page**: p. 8241 (Results and Discussion)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Statistical analysis of 14C dates; Table 1 (p. 8241)
**Depends on**: AMS dating of six specimens from Room 38
**Notes**: Only scarlet macaws from Snaketown and Pueblo Grande in the Hohokam region of southern Arizona are likely to predate the Pueblo Bonito macaws (refs. 3, 6, 53). The dates are "50-150 y earlier than the dates for scarlet macaw procurement previously postulated by Chaco scholars" (p. 8241).

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: The clustering of six dates consistent with a single procurement event suggests organized, deliberate acquisition rather than sporadic or incidental trade. This supports the interpretation of macaw procurement as a planned signaling investment.

### Claim 3

**Statement**: Of the 34 scarlet macaws from Chaco analyzed by Hargrave (1970), only a single macaw may have reached breeding age. No nestlings or juveniles (less than 4 months) were found. These age groups should be well-represented in a breeding population. No evidence of breeding pens or macaw eggshells has been reported from Pueblo Bonito.
**Page**: p. 8240
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Hargrave 1970 (ref. 5); refs. 5, 29
**Depends on**: Faunal analysis by Hargrave
**Notes**: Scarlet macaws classified as: aged (1), immature (4-11 months, n=1), newly fledged (11-12 months, n=24), or adolescent (1-3 years, n=8). By contrast, at Paquime in northern Mexico, 322 scarlet macaws were recovered along with breeding pens and eggshells (p. 8240; ref. 15).

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: The absence of a breeding population at Pueblo Bonito means all macaws were imported. This has direct implications for the cost of exotic goods signaling: each macaw required a procurement trip to Mesoamerica or a well-organized long-distance exchange network, making macaws genuinely costly signals.

### Claim 4

**Statement**: The acquisition of scarlet macaws likely required lengthy trips between the northern Southwest and the lowland tropical forests of Mesoamerica. These birds historically lived as far north as the Gulf Coast state of Tamaulipas near Tampico, approximately 1,800 km from Chaco. The northern limit of the species along the Pacific coast was southern Oaxaca, even more distant (approximately 2,500 km) from Chaco.
**Page**: p. 8238
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Refs. 11, 12 (biogeographic range data)
**Depends on**: Biogeographic range data for Ara macao
**Notes**: Historical accounts document that Pueblo people exchanged turquoise and hides for macaws or their feathers (refs. 13, 14). The possibility that scarlet macaws were procured from the closer prehispanic center of Paquime (13th-15th century) is noted but Paquime postdates many Chaco macaws.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: The 1,800+ km procurement distance establishes macaw acquisition as among the costliest signaling investments at Chaco. The distance alone makes macaws a quality-dependent signal: only groups with sufficient organizational capacity, exchange networks, and resources could sustain procurement over such distances.

### Claim 5

**Statement**: Room 33 in Pueblo Bonito, one of two burial crypts in the pueblo, contained 14 human interments associated with large quantities of turquoise, shell, and flutes. Two individuals (nos. 13 and 14) buried below a rare wooden plank floor were associated with rich grave assemblages. These burials, previously assumed to postdate A.D. 1040, have been directly AMS 14C dated to no later than A.D. 775-875, suggesting hierarchical sociopolitical structures evolved earlier in Chaco than previously assumed.
**Page**: p. 8239
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Refs. 22-24 (previous chronological assumptions); Ref. 16 (Plog and Heitman 2010 for 14C dates of Room 33 burials)
**Depends on**: AMS 14C dating by Plog and Heitman 2010
**Notes**: Room 33 is "in one of the crypts located in the oldest part of the Pueblo" (p. 8239). Considerable evidence, including persistent use as a burial crypt for 200-250 years, indicates the northern section of Pueblo Bonito was central to ritual activity throughout the great house's long occupation.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (direct AMS dates from Plog and Heitman 2010)
**Assessment**: The early dates for elite burials, combined with early macaw dates, demonstrate that social hierarchy and exotic goods acquisition both emerged in the 9th-10th centuries, well before the Classic Bonito architectural florescence. This supports a model where elite status signaling (Layer 1) precedes and drives monument construction (Layer 2), rather than the reverse.

### Claim 6

**Statement**: Scarlet macaws and their feathers are of particular significance in Pueblo cosmology and ritual today. Macaw feathers are highly prized for ceremonial and cosmological purposes. Macaws are a primary species marking directional association by color (red/orange) correlating with shell and other red material and designating southern and/or nadir positions in Pueblo cosmological systems.
**Page**: p. 8240
**Type**: empirical/ethnographic
**Evidence cited by author**: Refs. 13, 37 (ceremonial use of feathers); Ref. 38 (directional association); Refs. 13, 36-39 (macaw feathers on prayer-sticks, costumes, masks)
**Depends on**: Ethnographic documentation of Pueblo practices
**Notes**: "Religious power is thus strongly associated with sociopolitical power" in Pueblo society, and "those who controlled key cosmological knowledge and symbols also had significant influence over social, economic, and political relations" (p. 8240; refs. 38, 40).

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (ethnographic analogy applied to prehistoric context)
**Assessment**: The cosmological significance of macaws means they functioned as more than mere luxury items. Their ritual importance suggests that macaw acquisition signaled access to cosmological knowledge and authority, a dimension of "quality" that goes beyond simple wealth. This is relevant to defining the quality dimension in the Spence condition.

### Claim 7

**Statement**: Down-the-line exchange of scarlet macaws is unlikely because of the difficulties in raising and transporting newly fledged macaws. Acquisition of such juvenile birds was "a formidable task, requiring the removal of nestlings within the first few months of their birth, along with specialized knowledge, perhaps by trained aviculturists (whether Mesoamericans or Puebloans), who could keep young scarlet macaws alive during long trips."
**Page**: pp. 8239-8240
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Refs. 6, 11, 29, 30 (macaw husbandry knowledge)
**Depends on**: Faunal analysis showing predominance of juvenile/newly fledged birds
**Notes**: The age distribution (predominantly newly fledged, 11-12 months) rules out simple down-the-line exchange and implies direct procurement requiring specialized knowledge.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: The requirement for specialized knowledge in macaw procurement adds another cost dimension beyond simple distance. Successful macaw acquisition required both logistical capacity (long-distance travel) and technical knowledge (aviculture), making it a multidimensional costly signal.

### Claim 8

**Statement**: An intermediate explanation suggests Chacoans obtained scarlet macaws from the Mimbres people, approximately 350 km distant in southwestern New Mexico. The height of Mimbres culture (A.D. 1000-1130) coincides with the most intensive era of great house construction in Chaco Canyon. Three of the four Mimbres macaw dates also fall within the same period as the middle cluster of Pueblo Bonito macaw dates.
**Page**: pp. 8240-8241
**Type**: empirical/interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Refs. 3, 20, 30-32 (Mimbres-Chaco connection); Mimbres 14C dates from Table 1
**Depends on**: 14C dates of four Mimbres region macaws
**Notes**: Four Mimbres macaws were dated: Old Town tibiotarsus (945 +/- 15 BP, A.D. 1025-1155), Old Town humerus (965 +/- 15 BP, A.D. 1020-1155), Old Town tibiotarsus (975 +/- 15 BP, A.D. 1015-1150), Mitchell left humerus (1,075 +/- 20 BP, A.D. 895-1020). The Mitchell specimen is the only Mimbres macaw that predates the Pueblo Bonito early cluster.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: If Chacoans obtained macaws through the Mimbres region, the exchange network itself constituted a costly signal of interregional organizational capacity. The temporal overlap between Mimbres culture height and Chaco's construction peak suggests the exchange relationship was a component of the broader signaling system.

### Claim 9

**Statement**: Three separate and isolated procurement events could account for the observed temporal patterning of macaw 14C dates: an early cluster (6 macaws, A.D. 885-990), a middle cluster (6 macaws, A.D. 970-1035), and a late group (2 macaws, A.D. 1015-1155). However, a model of continuous macaw procurement throughout A.D. 900 to 1150 cannot be rejected, as the apparent episodic structure is "partly attributable to reversals and plateaus in the radiocarbon calibration curve from A.D. 900 to 1150."
**Page**: p. 8241
**Type**: empirical/methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Simulated calibrations described in SI Text; Fig. S1 and S2
**Depends on**: Radiocarbon calibration curve structure (IntCal13)
**Notes**: Chi-squared tests show each of the first two clusters is consistent with a single procurement event (early: chi-squared=5.59, df=5, P>0.05; middle: chi-squared=8.98, df=5, P>0.05).

**Evidence strength**: Strong (statistical tests reported; calibration curve effects acknowledged)
**Assessment**: Whether procurement was episodic or continuous has implications for the signaling model. Episodic procurement (large batches at intervals) might represent organized expeditions, while continuous procurement would suggest a sustained exchange network. Both interpretations support costly signaling, but through different organizational mechanisms.

### Claim 10

**Statement**: Scarlet macaws at Pueblo Bonito were not bred locally. In addition to the absence of breeding-age birds and breeding infrastructure, the proportion of scarlet macaws classified as nestlings, juveniles, or breeding age constitutes only 2.9% of the Chaco population, compared to 14.0% of the Paquime population (z = 1.82; P < 0.05, one-tailed test).
**Page**: p. 8241
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Ref. 29 (Paquime population data); Ref. 51 (isotopic analysis)
**Depends on**: Faunal analysis; statistical comparison with Paquime
**Notes**: The statistical test compares age distributions between Chaco (n=34) and Paquime (n=322). The Paquime population includes a breeding program with pens and eggshells (ref. 15).

**Evidence strength**: Strong (statistical test with significant result)
**Assessment**: The statistical confirmation that Chaco macaws were imported, not bred, strengthens the cost basis for macaw procurement as a costly signal. Each macaw represents the full acquisition cost (long-distance travel, specialized knowledge, exchange goods) rather than the lower marginal cost of local breeding.

### Claim 11

**Statement**: "We propose that the hierarchical sociopolitical foundation of Chacoan society was established during the initial era of great house construction (no later than the mid-ninth century) and that this foundation was reinforced during the late 9th and 10th centuries by the acquisition of scarlet macaws and other cosmologically powerful agents from Mesoamerica."
**Page**: p. 8242
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: AMS 14C dates from this study; Ref. 16 (Room 33 burial dates)
**Depends on**: All dating evidence presented in the paper
**Notes**: This reverses the standard narrative. Rather than macaw procurement being a consequence of the A.D. 1040-1110 florescence, social hierarchy was established first (by mid-800s), reinforced by exotic goods acquisition (900s-1000s), and only later manifested in the massive architectural expansion.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (chronological data are strong, but causal directionality is inferred)
**Assessment**: This chronological resequencing is important for the signaling model. If individual-level status signaling through exotic goods (Layer 1) precedes and facilitates group-level monument signaling (Layer 2), this suggests a bottom-up emergence where individual signalers bootstrap the social hierarchy that later produces collective monumental investment.

### Claim 12

**Statement**: 35 scarlet macaw skeletons have been recovered in Chaco Canyon, with 30 from Pueblo Bonito alone. The skeletal remains of more than 400 scarlet macaws have been recovered from the Southwest overall, with concentrations at the Mimbres region (n >= 10), the Mogollon Rim area of east-central Arizona (n = 22), Wupatki in north-central Arizona, and Paquime in northern Mexico (n = 322).
**Page**: p. 8238
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Refs. 5, 6 (Pueblo Bonito counts); Ref. 26 (35 in canyon)
**Depends on**: Archaeological recovery data from multiple excavations
**Notes**: The concentration of 30 macaws at a single site (Pueblo Bonito) out of 35 in the canyon underscores the restricted distribution of this exotic good.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: The concentration of macaws at Pueblo Bonito (86% of all canyon macaws) demonstrates that access to these exotic goods was highly restricted, consistent with quality-dependent signaling where only the highest-quality signaler(s) can afford the cost.

### Claim 13

**Statement**: Marine shell ornaments from Chaco number in the thousands, approximately two dozen copper bells or pieces of raw copper have been excavated, and cacao residue has been discovered in distinctive ceramic vessels found only in Chaco and resembling Mesoamerican chocolate pots. These quantities are "orders of magnitude greater than in most sections of the northern SW."
**Page**: p. 8239
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Refs. 2, 25 (copper bells/raw copper); Ref. 4 (cacao residue)
**Depends on**: Archaeological inventory from Chaco excavations
**Notes**: The variety of exotic goods (macaws, shell, copper, cacao) demonstrates multiple signaling channels, each requiring different procurement networks and specialized knowledge.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: Multiple exotic goods types represent redundant signaling channels. The diversity of exotics (biological, mineral, consumable) means that elite status signaling at Chaco was multidimensional, not dependent on a single commodity. This redundancy strengthens signal reliability and may reflect different audience preferences or different dimensions of "quality" being signaled.

## Summary Assessment

Watson et al. 2015 provides the most important direct chronological data for exotic goods acquisition at Chaco Canyon. The key finding, that macaw procurement began by A.D. 900, 50-150 years earlier than previously assumed, has fundamental implications for the signaling model. It establishes that individual-level status signaling through exotic goods (Layer 1) was contemporaneous with or preceded the escalation of group-level monument construction (Layer 2), supporting a bottom-up model of signaling system emergence. The paper also demonstrates that macaw procurement was genuinely costly (1,800+ km distance, specialized knowledge required, no local breeding) and highly restricted (86% of canyon macaws at Pueblo Bonito), satisfying the key conditions for quality-dependent costly signaling. The combination of elite burials dating to the 9th century (from Plog and Heitman 2010) and early macaw dates from this study establishes that the social hierarchy underlying the signaling system was in place well before the architectural florescence that most scholars identify as the hallmark of Chacoan complexity.
