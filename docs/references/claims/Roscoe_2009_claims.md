## Claims

### Claim 1

**Statement**: "The organization of small-scale society was oriented, among other goals, towards biological and social reproduction, subsistence optimization, and military defense."
**Page**: p. 69 (abstract)
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Contact-era New Guinea ethnography (not specifiable from abstract alone)
**Depends on**: Ethnographic research on New Guinea societies
**Notes**: Roscoe identifies three functional goals of small-scale social organization: reproduction, subsistence, and defense. The signaling model engages primarily with defense (Layer 2: conflict deterrence) and subsistence optimization (Layer 3: cooperation networks for crisis buffering). The multi-goal framework is important because it recognizes that signaling serves multiple functions simultaneously, consistent with the mls-monuments model's three-layer structure.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (well-grounded in extensive ethnographic literature on New Guinea; specific evidence not assessable from abstract)
**Assessment**: The three-goal framework is analytically useful and well-established in the ethnographic literature. The integration with signaling theory is Roscoe's key contribution.

### Claim 2

**Statement**: "To advance these multiple collective interests, however, these communities had to deal with three problems: an optimality problem, a conflict-of-interest problem, and a free-rider problem."
**Page**: p. 69 (abstract)
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: [Cannot verify from abstract alone]
**Depends on**: Collective action theory applied to small-scale societies
**Notes**: The three-problem framework maps onto formal game theory: the optimality problem relates to coordination (finding efficient solutions), the conflict-of-interest problem relates to bargaining (distributing gains), and the free-rider problem relates to enforcement (preventing defection). All three problems are addressed in the mls-monuments model through different mechanisms.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (standard collective action problems applied to ethnographic context)
**Assessment**: The problem decomposition is analytically clear. The contribution is applying it specifically to the organization and maintenance of small-scale societies rather than to cooperation in general.

### Claim 3

**Statement**: "The optimality problem was solved with a modular (or segmented) social structure, the conflict-of-interest problem by a process of social signaling, and these two solutions together operated to resolve the free-rider problems they created."
**Page**: p. 69 (abstract)
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: [Cannot verify from abstract alone; based on New Guinea ethnography]
**Depends on**: Claims 1 and 2
**Notes**: This is the paper's central argument. Social signaling resolves conflicts of interest by providing reliable information about group capacities and commitments, reducing the need for actual conflict. This maps directly onto the mls-monuments model's Layer 2 (mutual assessment). The modular social structure (segmented communities) corresponds to the model's spatial structure (great house communities as modules). The key insight is that signaling and modularity work together, which the model captures through the interaction between group-level signals (Layer 2) and individual/subgroup signals (Layer 1).

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (the claim integrates two established ideas, modularity and signaling, in a novel way; specific evidence not assessable from abstract)
**Assessment**: The signaling-modularity integration is an important contribution. The claim that signaling resolves conflicts of interest (rather than just advertising quality) aligns with Barclay et al. 2021's distinction between quality signals and intent signals.
