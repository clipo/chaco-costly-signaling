## Claims

### Claim 1

**Statement**: Persistent differences in wealth and power among prehispanic Pueblo societies are visible from the late AD 800s through the late 1200s, measured by Gini coefficients calculated from house size distributions. High Ginis are positively related to settlement persistence and inversely related to available unoccupied dry-farming niche.
**Page**: p. 1 (abstract)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Gini coefficients from house size data; "room to walk" metric for farmland availability
**Depends on**: House size as a proxy for household wealth; accurate room counts and dating
**Notes**: The dual finding (inequality correlates with persistence and inversely with farmland) suggests that inequality increases as populations become more constrained and less mobile. This is relevant to the model's environmental uncertainty parameter: inequality and signaling may co-evolve under resource stress.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: The use of house size Gini coefficients is well-established in Southwestern archaeology. The dual correlation is an important finding for understanding the social context of signaling.

### Claim 2

**Statement**: The transition to Malthusian dynamics in the VEPIIN area (central Mesa Verde) is not abrupt but extends over centuries, contrasting with models predicting 5-6 decade transitions.
**Page**: p. 1 (abstract)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Puleston et al. model for comparison; Village Ecodynamics Project (VEP) data
**Depends on**: Population estimates and carrying capacity calculations
**Notes**: The gradual transition to Malthusian dynamics implies that environmental constraints were felt incrementally, not as a sudden threshold. This has implications for how sigma (environmental uncertainty) should be modeled: it may have increased gradually rather than stepping up.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: The finding depends on the resolution of population estimates and the definition of "Malthusian dynamics."

---

### Claim 3

**Statement**: Crude birth rate in the Southwest increased slowly from approximately 1100 BC to approximately AD 500, remaining high with some variability to approximately AD 1100, reached a nadir at approximately AD 1200, and made a final increase to AD 1300, followed by a very marked decline. From about AD 100 to AD 1400, the crude birth rate exceeded that of the world's fastest-growing country (Niger) in 2013.
**Page**: Section 2(a)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Bioarchaeological dataset analysis
**Depends on**: Skeletal age-at-death distributions; life table methods
**Notes**: The extremely high growth rates provide context for the demographic pressure that may have driven both inequality and signaling investment. The AD 1200 nadir corresponds to the period of Chaco's decline and system reorganization.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: Life table estimates from skeletal populations have known biases, but the broad temporal pattern is consistent across multiple independent studies.

### Claim 4

**Statement**: When Gini coefficients, driven by the appearance of Chacoan Great Houses, increased markedly for the northern Southwest as a whole around AD 900, they simultaneously declined in the CMV (central Mesa Verde). The steep decline in CMV Ginis suggests that the Chaco model of wealth inequality (and the social/ceremonial system to which that was presumably coupled) was actively resisted.
**Page**: Section 2(f)
**Type**: empirical/interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Figure 1e; spatial patterns of Gini values
**Depends on**: House size data with accurate dating; regional Gini calculations
**Notes**: The divergence between CMV and regional Gini trends is a key finding. If Chacoan great houses drove regional inequality, then the CMV's rejection of this pattern suggests that the Chacoan signaling system was not universally accepted. The authors suggest possible expansion into Fremont territory "precisely to escape Chacoan domination."

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: The finding that the CMV resisted Chacoan inequality has important implications for the signaling model. If some populations rejected the signaling system, this constrains the model's predictions about how far monument-based signaling can extend.

### Claim 5

**Statement**: Periods with high wealth inequality are followed by periods of high violence, and conversely periods of high violence are followed by periods of high wealth inequality. This is a statistically significant tendency documented for the VEPIIN area.
**Page**: Section 2(f)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Statistical analysis of temporal correlation; VEP data
**Depends on**: Independent measures of inequality (house size Gini) and violence (bioarchaeological evidence)
**Notes**: This bidirectional relationship between inequality and violence is directly relevant to the model's Layer 2 (intergroup assessment and conflict deterrence). If monument signaling reduces conflict (Pax Chaco), then the model predicts: (1) high signaling investment -> low conflict during florescence, (2) collapse of signaling -> return of conflict, which is consistent with the observed pattern.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (statistical significance reported)
**Assessment**: The temporal pattern supports the model's predictions about the relationship between signaling investment and conflict levels, though the mechanism (signaling deterrence vs. other explanations) is not tested.

### Claim 6

**Statement**: Typical house use-life was sub-generational until about AD 1020 (for hamlets), never less than about 28 years in villages. Frequent movement likely reduced intergenerational transmission of wealth, especially inheritance of superior fields. As "room to walk" (available farmland) decreases, duration of house occupation increases.
**Page**: Section 2(c)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Village Ecodynamics Project data
**Depends on**: Dendrochronological dating of residential structures
**Notes**: The relationship between mobility and inequality is important for the model: high mobility (pre-AD 1020) should reduce the benefit of signaling investment because groups can escape exploitative signaling systems by relocating. As mobility decreased, groups became more committed to place, increasing the value of local signaling for conflict deterrence and cooperation.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: The finding that settlement persistence increased as farmland decreased is well-documented and provides a mechanism for why signaling investment became more valuable over time.

### Claim 7

**Statement**: The rise of the Chaco system in the San Juan Basin in the mid-to-late 800s takes place towards the end of the Copial Phase, during still-rapid population growth and favorable climates. Chaco's post-AD 1030 expansion happened just as the constraints of the Malthusian Transition Interval were beginning to be felt.
**Page**: Section 3
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Population and settlement data; climate reconstructions
**Depends on**: Copial Phase/Malthusian Transition chronology
**Notes**: This timing is important for the model: Chaco's signaling system emerged during favorable conditions (Copial Phase) but expanded precisely when carrying capacity constraints began binding (Malthusian Transition). This is consistent with the lambda(sigma) prediction: signaling returns increase as uncertainty about resource access rises.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: The interpretation depends on the Copial/Malthusian framework, which is one way to characterize the demographic trajectory.

### Claim 8

**Statement**: Chaco had enough characteristics of a tributary state as to be unable to survive a lack of taxes or tribute flowing to the centre from the periphery. Mid-1100s drought and replacement of El Nino by La Nina conditions in the 1200s caused production failures that undermined this system.
**Page**: Section 3
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: General Chaco literature; climate data
**Depends on**: Chaco-as-tributary-state interpretation
**Notes**: If Chaco was a tributary system dependent on surplus extraction, then the signaling model's Layer 2 (intergroup assessment) takes on a coercive dimension: monument signaling may have facilitated tribute extraction by demonstrating the center's power. The model would need to account for this exploitative signaling channel alongside the cooperative one.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: The tributary interpretation is one of several. Others (pilgrimage, reciprocal exchange) would change the signaling dynamics.

### Claim 9

**Statement**: Male-biased sex ratios in the CMV in the 1200s coupled with female-biased ratios in Chaco and the Aztec (Middle San Juan) areas in the 1000s and 1200s have been used to suggest that Chaco and its follow-on Aztec raided areas for women that were not yet incorporated in their system.
**Page**: Section 3
**Type**: empirical/interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Sex ratio analyses (unspecified source)
**Depends on**: Bioarchaeological sexing of skeletal remains
**Notes**: If accurate, bride capture or raiding for women would represent a form of coercive extraction that complicates the cooperative signaling interpretation. This would be evidence against a purely cooperative Pax Chaco and might suggest that monument signaling had a coercive component.

**Evidence strength**: Weak (secondary claim; source not fully specified; sex ratio estimates from skeletal remains are methodologically challenging)
**Assessment**: This is a provocative claim that, if supported, would require the model to incorporate coercive as well as cooperative signaling channels. However, the evidence is uncertain.

### Claim 10

**Statement**: The best time to found a village-spanning polity is when growth is still very rapid and has not quite become throttled by density-dependent constraints. At that point a polity will look attractive for its ability to move production around to buffer spatial and temporal shortfalls.
**Page**: Section 4 (Postscript)
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Conceptual argument
**Depends on**: General political ecology framework
**Notes**: This timing prediction aligns with the model's predictions about when signaling investment would be most beneficial: during the transition from growth to constraint, when the returns to cooperation-network building are highest because groups need buffering but still have surplus to invest.

**Evidence strength**: Weak (speculative argument, not tested)
**Assessment**: The prediction is consistent with the model's lambda(sigma) framework and with the observed timing of Chaco's expansion (late Copial Phase / early Malthusian Transition).

### Claim 11

**Statement**: Patchy resources resulting in different household opportunities, and increasing constraints on mobility, are key ingredients for the development of inequality. Social stratification increases significantly as environmental heterogeneity and environmental circumscription increase.
**Page**: Section 4 (Postscript)
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Cross-cultural research (unspecified)
**Depends on**: Comparative ethnographic/archaeological data
**Notes**: This connects inequality to environmental structure. For the model, resource patchiness and mobility constraints create the conditions under which signaling becomes valuable: heterogeneous environments make exchange partners valuable, while reduced mobility makes signaling investment worthwhile because groups cannot simply relocate.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (supported by cross-cultural research but specific citations not provided here)
**Assessment**: The claim is well-supported in the broader literature and directly relevant to the model's assumption that environmental uncertainty drives signaling investment.

### Claim 12

**Statement**: Larry Benson sampled and analyzed soils from several regions in the Southwest, suggesting that the most favored locations could produce 10 bushels of maize per year for only about 18 years under the most favorable conditions, while the best locations in Morefield Canyon could possibly last three times that long.
**Page**: Section 2(e)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Benson's soil analyses (unspecified year)
**Depends on**: Soil nitrogen analyses; maize productivity estimates
**Notes**: The limited field-life of even the best soils means that agricultural sustainability depended on access to multiple field locations and/or fallow rotations. This resource constraint is important for the model's carrying capacity parameter and the assumption that environmental shortfalls are frequent.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: Specific soil productivity data provides ground-truth for the model's agricultural parameters.

## Claim Type Definitions

- **Empirical**: Direct observation or measurement reported by the author
- **Interpretive**: Inference from empirical data; not directly observable
- **Speculative**: Acknowledged by the author as tentative, or basis unclear
- **Methodological**: Claim about how to conduct or interpret research

## Evidence Strength Ratings

- **Strong**: Claim is well-supported by the evidence presented
- **Moderate**: Claim is partially supported; some evidence but gaps remain
- **Weak**: Claim is poorly supported by the evidence presented
- **Assertion without evidence**: No evidence cited; claim is stated as fact without support

## Summary Assessment

Kohler et al. 2023 provides a comprehensive analysis of wealth inequality dynamics in the prehispanic Pueblo Southwest, using Gini coefficients from house size distributions tracked over several centuries. For the signaling model, the paper's most important contributions are: (1) documenting that Chacoan great houses drove a marked increase in regional inequality while some areas (CMV) resisted, (2) establishing a statistically significant bidirectional relationship between inequality and violence that supports the model's Layer 2 predictions, (3) showing that settlement persistence increased as farmland became scarce, creating conditions under which signaling investment became more valuable, and (4) framing Chaco's expansion as occurring at the Copial/Malthusian transition when cooperation-network buffering would have been most attractive. The paper's treatment of Chaco as a tributary state and the suggestion that coercive extraction (including raiding for women) may have been involved complicates a purely cooperative signaling interpretation and suggests the model should allow for coercive as well as cooperative signaling channels.
