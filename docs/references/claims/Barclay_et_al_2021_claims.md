## Claims

### Claim 1

**Statement**: "Helping is a costly signal of stake: helping is worthwhile for those who value the recipient... but is not worthwhile for those who do not value the recipient."
**Page**: Introduction (Section 1)
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Conceptual extension of costly signaling theory; welfare trade-off ratio literature (Tooby & Cosmides 2008)
**Depends on**: Costly signaling theory (Zahavi 1975, Grafen 1990); fitness interdependence concept
**Notes**: This is the paper's central claim. It reframes costly signaling from signaling quality/ability to signaling intent/stake. Directly relevant to Layer 3 of the mls-monuments model: monument construction signals a group's stake in the cooperative network, not just its competitive quality.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: The theoretical logic is well-developed and formally modeled. The shift from signaling quality to signaling stake/intent is a significant contribution that extends CST beyond the traditional handicap paradigm.

---

### Claim 2

**Statement**: Fitness interdependence describes situations where "one person's fitness can positively impact another's" through kinship, cooperative partnerships, or group benefits. The welfare trade-off ratio (WTR) quantifies "how much you value another person, relative to how much you value yourself," with helping occurring when WTR x b > c.
**Page**: Section 1(a): Fitness interdependence
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Roberts 2005, Tooby and Cosmides 2008, Aktipis et al. 2018
**Depends on**: Inclusive fitness theory, interdependence theory
**Notes**: The WTR condition (WTR x b > c) is analogous to the Spence condition in the mls-monuments model. The key extension is that WTR can arise from many sources beyond kinship, including cooperative partnerships and group-level effects.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: Well-established concept with formal definition. The WTR framework provides a more general foundation for the signaling cost condition than the traditional handicap principle.

### Claim 3

**Statement**: Most signaling models involve "signals of quality or ability" but signals can also indicate "intent, a signal to prove that the signaller intends to follow through on a threat or promise." Signals of cooperative intent are "maintained by the long-term benefits accruing to honest signallers."
**Page**: Section 1(b): Signaling of intent
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Silk et al. 2000, Zahavi 1977
**Depends on**: Distinction between ability signals and intent signals
**Notes**: This distinction is critical for archaeological applications. Monuments may signal both ability (we can afford to build this) and intent (we intend to maintain cooperative relationships). The mls-monuments model's Layer 2 (conflict deterrence) captures the ability dimension; Layer 3 (cooperation networks) captures the intent dimension.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: The distinction between ability and intent signals is well-argued but underexplored empirically. The authors acknowledge the categories can overlap.

### Claim 4

**Statement**: "Generosity" is "one of the most important signals of cooperative intent." Costlier or more "genuine" forms of generosity engender more trust than more selfish or aggrandizing forms. Among Martu, sharing a higher proportion of income, not just larger absolute amounts, generates reputations for generosity.
**Page**: Section 1(b): Signaling of intent
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Barclay 2010, 2016; Bliege Bird et al. 2012, 2018; Bliege Bird and Power 2015
**Depends on**: Martu Aboriginal ethnographic research
**Notes**: The proportion-based criterion (share of income, not absolute amount) maps onto the Spence condition: the cost relative to capacity must be quality-correlated. For Chaco, this would predict that the ratio of construction investment to community size (not just absolute monument size) is the relevant signal.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: Well-supported empirically through the Martu ethnographic work. The proportion-based finding is important for archaeological operationalization.

### Claim 5

**Statement**: The model uses a two-stage process: a "helping game (which serves as a signal)" followed by a "trust game." Four game types are analyzed depending on temptation (T) and sucker's payoff (S): Prisoner's Dilemma (T > R, P > S), Snowdrift (T > R, S > P), Stag Hunt (R > T, P > S), and Harmony (R > T, S > P).
**Page**: Section 2(a-b): Trust decision and signalling; Game types
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Formal model construction
**Depends on**: Game theory framework
**Notes**: The Stag Hunt game type is most relevant to the Chaco context: groups face coordination problems where mutual cooperation yields the highest payoff, but coordination failure is costly. Monument construction could serve as the signal in the first stage, with trust (exchange, cooperation during crisis) as the second stage.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (as formal model)
**Assessment**: Well-specified formal framework. The multiple game types provide flexibility for different empirical contexts.

### Claim 6

**Statement**: The combined honest equilibrium condition (Inequality 6) is: V_st(b + R - P) + (R - P) > c > V_su(b + S - P) + (T - P), where V_st is the trustworthy sender's valuation and V_su is the untrustworthy sender's valuation. Signals are honest when this inequality holds.
**Page**: Section 2(e): Analysing the honest equilibrium
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Formal derivation from the model
**Depends on**: The sequential helping-then-trust game structure
**Notes**: This is the paper's core formal result. The condition specifies that signal cost must be high enough to deter untrustworthy senders but low enough that trustworthy senders can afford it. This parallels the Spence condition in the mls-monuments model but is derived from a different underlying game structure (trust game rather than labor market screening).

**Evidence strength**: Strong (as formal derivation)
**Assessment**: The equilibrium condition is rigorously derived. Its application to archaeological contexts requires operationalizing V, b, c, and the payoff matrix, which the paper does not attempt.

### Claim 7

**Statement**: "Low-cost signals can often be honest, especially when the temptation to defect T is less than the reward for mutual cooperation R." Signals can be "extremely cheap" in Stag Hunts and Harmony Games but "may need to be expensive" in Snowdrift Games.
**Page**: Section 2(e), discussion of Inequalities 7-8
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Derived from the formal model
**Depends on**: Claim 6
**Notes**: This finding challenges the conventional assumption that costly signals must always be expensive. For archaeological monuments, this suggests that in contexts where coordination is the primary challenge (Stag Hunts), even relatively modest construction could serve as an effective signal. However, where free-riding is a greater temptation (Prisoner's Dilemma), more expensive signals are needed.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (as formal result)
**Assessment**: This is an important theoretical nuance. The implication for the C = 0.35 parameter is that the required cost depends on the underlying game structure, not just the absolute magnitude of the signal.

### Claim 8

**Statement**: A signaling system exists "when receivers demand signals and only honest signallers are willing to pay the cost to signal." Signaling occurs within pairs with "medium interdependence": "highly interdependent pairs will have no signalling because receivers do not demand signals, whereas weakly interdependent pairs will have no signalling because senders are unwilling to pay costs."
**Page**: Section 2(g): When will signalling systems exist?
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Derived from the formal model
**Depends on**: Inequalities for receiver demand and sender willingness
**Notes**: The "medium interdependence" finding is directly relevant to Chaco. Signaling via monuments would be most valuable between communities that have some but not full mutual knowledge: close neighbors who already know each other would not need monument signals, and completely isolated communities would not benefit from signaling. This predicts signaling investment along the margins of the interaction network, which aligns with the outlier great house distribution.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (as formal result)
**Assessment**: Generates testable spatial predictions for monument placement.

### Claim 9

**Statement**: "The 'help' need not even provide any real benefit to the receiver, it can still signal the sender's willingness to cooperate at b = 0." This matches "existing models of courtship signalling, where a suitor may give costly but useless gifts to signal his intention to stay."
**Page**: Section 3: Discussion
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Sozou and Seymour 2005, Szamado 2011
**Depends on**: Claim 6 (equilibrium condition holds at b = 0)
**Notes**: This result is significant for monumental architecture: the monument itself need not provide direct utilitarian benefits to other groups. The signal value lies in the demonstrated willingness to bear costs, not in the monument's utility. This distinguishes the signaling interpretation from functional explanations of great houses (storage, defense, ritual space).

**Evidence strength**: Strong (as formal result)
**Assessment**: This is a key theoretical contribution for archaeological applications. It provides formal justification for why "non-functional" investment in monumental construction can be adaptive.

### Claim 10

**Statement**: "We might expect to find costlier forms of generosity signalling where communities are more fluid and residentially mobile, conflict and competition are more common" and "more widespread if one must sometimes cooperate with one's competitors."
**Page**: Section 3: Discussion
**Type**: speculative
**Evidence cited by author**: Conceptual extension from the model
**Depends on**: Medium-interdependence result (Claim 8)
**Notes**: This prediction maps well onto the Chaco context: the San Juan Basin had fluid population movement (pilgrimage model), potential for intergroup competition (multiple great houses), and periodic need for cooperation (drought buffering). These conditions predict substantial signaling investment, consistent with the empirical pattern.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (prediction not tested)
**Assessment**: Generates testable predictions but requires empirical specification of "fluidity" and "competition" for archaeological testing.

### Claim 11

**Statement**: "Signals can start out as cues: if a cue is correlated with some hidden trait, observers will start attending to that cue, whereupon it pays to invest in actively signalling that trait."
**Page**: Section 3: Discussion
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Conceptual argument from signaling evolution literature
**Depends on**: Distinction between cues and signals (Maynard Smith and Harper 2003)
**Notes**: This evolutionary trajectory (cue to signal) may apply to Chaco great house development. Early pithouses and great houses may have been cues of community size/investment that later became active signals through deliberate escalation of construction scale. The shift from locally sourced to distant timber (ca. AD 1020, Guiterman et al. 2015) could mark this transition.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (well-established in signaling theory; application to architecture is novel)
**Assessment**: Provides a mechanism for how monument construction could evolve incrementally from practical to signaling function.

### Claim 12

**Statement**: Martu Aboriginal women hunters form foraging groups and practice generous meat sharing despite personal cost. Better hunters give away larger proportions of their harvest than poorer hunters. Women explain benefits come from "friendships generated through generous sharing," which builds pukurrpa (happiness and social trust). Women pair with other generous partners, suggesting reputation effects and partner choice.
**Page**: Section 1 (Introduction) and throughout
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Bliege Bird et al. 2012, 2018; Bliege Bird and Power 2015
**Depends on**: Martu ethnographic fieldwork
**Notes**: The Martu case provides the empirical foundation for the model. The key parallel to Chaco: costly investment (sharing/construction) builds reputation for cooperativeness, which attracts partners (exchange networks), which provides benefits during uncertainty (crisis buffering). The partner choice mechanism (pairing with generous partners) is the ethnographic analogue of Layer 3.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: Well-documented ethnographic case. The parallel to monument construction is analogical rather than direct, but the underlying mechanism (costly generosity builds cooperative partnerships) is the same.

### Claim 13

**Statement**: Each agent knows its own stake in the other, but not the other's stake in it. This information asymmetry is "what it is trying to assess" through signaling. Stake is "relationship-specific" and can be "asymmetric: you may depend upon someone more than they depend on you."
**Page**: Section 2(c): Value placed on partners
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Model assumption
**Depends on**: Information asymmetry as foundation for signaling
**Notes**: The asymmetric stake concept is relevant to Chaco's core-periphery structure: outlier communities may have had greater stake in the canyon center (for drought buffering, ceremonial access) than vice versa, generating asymmetric signaling incentives. This predicts that outlier great houses (signaling toward the center) would be built by communities with higher environmental vulnerability.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (as formal assumption)
**Assessment**: The asymmetry assumption generates testable spatial predictions about differential signaling investment between Chaco core and periphery.

---

### Claim 14

**Statement**: "Signalling systems are likely in Stag Hunts, Harmony Games and Prisoner's Dilemmas, but not in Snowdrift Games." This is because in Snowdrift Games, even untrustworthy senders face low costs from being trusted (decent sucker's payoff S > P), so the cost needed to separate honest from dishonest signalers becomes prohibitively high.
**Page**: Section 2(g): When will signalling systems exist?
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Formal result from the model
**Depends on**: Equilibrium analysis across game types
**Notes**: Identifying the game type that characterizes Chaco intergroup interactions is important for determining whether signaling systems should be expected. If Chaco communities faced Stag Hunt dynamics (high returns to coordination, high cost of coordination failure during drought), then signaling is predicted. If they faced Snowdrift dynamics (can survive alone but free-riding is tempting), signaling systems would be harder to sustain.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (as formal result)
**Assessment**: Provides a clear diagnostic criterion for when signaling systems should arise. The Stag Hunt interpretation is most consistent with the Chaco evidence (drought cooperation, shared labor for construction).

## Claim Type Definitions

- **Empirical**: Direct observation or measurement reported by the author
- **Interpretive**: Inference from empirical data; not directly observable
- **Speculative**: Acknowledged by the author as tentative, or basis unclear
- **Methodological**: Claim about how to conduct or interpret research

## Evidence Strength Ratings

- **Strong**: Claim is well-supported by the evidence presented
- **Moderate**: Claim is partially supported; some evidence but gaps remain
- **Weak**: Claim is poorly supported by the evidence presented
- **Assertion without evidence**: No evidence cited; claim is stated as fact without support

## Summary Assessment

Barclay et al. 2021 provides the formal theoretical foundation for extending costly signaling theory from quality signals to intent/stake signals. The central contribution, that helping can honestly signal one's stake in another's welfare, directly supports Layer 3 of the mls-monuments model (cooperation networks and crisis buffering). The formal equilibrium condition (Inequality 6) parallels the Spence condition but derives from a trust-game structure rather than labor market screening. Key results include: (1) signals need not benefit receivers to be effective; (2) low-cost signals can be honest under certain game structures; (3) signaling emerges at intermediate levels of interdependence; and (4) different cooperative dilemmas require different signal costs. The Martu ethnographic data provide empirical grounding. The paper does not address monumental architecture or archaeological contexts directly, but its theoretical framework maps directly onto the monument-as-cooperation-signal interpretation.
