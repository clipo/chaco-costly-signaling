## Claims

### Claim 1: Over 200,000 turquoise pieces recovered from Chaco Canyon
"Over 200,000 pieces of turquoise have been recovered throughout the canyon (Harbottle and Weigand, 1992; Mathien, 1981) which is extraordinary when compared with other archaeological sites throughout the American Southwest." (p. 187)

**Relevance**: Establishes the sheer scale of turquoise accumulation at Chaco. This volume represents massive investment in long-distance acquisition over centuries, supporting the interpretation of turquoise as a costly signaling medium. The quantity far exceeds any other site in the region, indicating concentrated control over this resource.

---

### Claim 2: Nearest turquoise source is over 200 km away at Cerrillos Hills
"The amount of turquoise recovered from Chaco Canyon is remarkable considering that the nearest known source of turquoise is over 200 km in the Cerrillos Hills Mining District, near present-day Santa Fe, New Mexico." (p. 187)

**Relevance**: The minimum 200 km acquisition distance establishes the cost floor for turquoise procurement. This distance, across arid terrain without pack animals, represents a substantial labor investment per unit of turquoise, making procurement cost genuinely quality-dependent in the signaling framework.

---

### Claim 3: Hydrogen and copper isotopes identify geological source of turquoise artifacts
"We developed a technique using the isotope ratios of hydrogen (2H/1H) and copper (65Cu/63Cu) and the microanalytical abilities of a secondary ion mass spectrometer (SIMS) establishing a comparative database consisting of over 800 analyses from 22 turquoise resource areas in the western United States and northern Mexico." (p. 188)

**Relevance**: The isotopic sourcing technique enables direct empirical testing of turquoise trade network models. For the signaling model, isotopic provenance allows tracking which communities accessed which source areas, revealing the spatial extent and directional structure of exchange networks.

---

### Claim 4: 74 turquoise artifacts analyzed from multiple Puebloan sites
"We analyzed 74 turquoise artifacts from Puebloan sites in the San Juan Basin and the Virgin Puebloan area in southern Utah and the Moapa Valley in Nevada." Sites include Pueblo Bonito (29), Marcia's Rincon (9), Aztec Ruin (13), Salmon Ruin (8), Steve Perkins (5), Yamashita Y3 (3), and Eagle's Watch (7). (pp. 187-188, 191)

**Relevance**: The sample covers both core Chaco Canyon sites and peripheral/Virgin Puebloan sites, allowing comparison of procurement patterns across the social hierarchy and geographic extent of the Chaco system.

---

### Claim 5: Pueblo Bonito turquoise overwhelmingly sourced from Rio Grande Rift deposits
"Of the 29 turquoise artifacts analyzed from Pueblo Bonito, we identified the geological source of 16 artifacts of which 13 were linked with turquoise resource areas along or near the Rio Grande Rift including Villa Grove, Cerrillos Hills, and Orogrande." (p. 191)

**Relevance**: 81% (13/16) of sourced Pueblo Bonito turquoise came from the eastern deposits along the Rio Grande Rift. This indicates a distinct, possibly controlled, procurement strategy for the premier great house, consistent with elite control over specific trade routes.

---

### Claim 6: Small sites in Marcia's Rincon sourced turquoise exclusively from western (Nevada) deposits
"Out of the nine artifacts that were analyzed from the five archaeological sites in Marcia's Rincon, we identified two Nevada deposits as the geological sources for six artifacts, Royston and Lone Mountain near Tonopah, with three unknowns... the inhabitants of the small sites in Marcia's Rincon preferred the Nevada turquoise deposits with 100% of the identified sample from the western resource areas and the lack of any turquoise linking with deposits along the Rio Grande Rift." (p. 191)

**Relevance**: The complete divergence in sourcing between Pueblo Bonito and the small sites demonstrates that different social groups within the same canyon had access to different trade networks. This supports the interpretation that turquoise procurement was socially mediated, not simply a function of geographic proximity, and that elites at Pueblo Bonito controlled access to the closer Rio Grande Rift sources.

---

### Claim 7: Two distinct turquoise trade networks operated simultaneously within Chaco Canyon
"Differences in turquoise procurement patterns between Pueblo Bonito and the small sites in Chaco Canyon add support to the hypothesis that there were diverse populations living contemporaneously in the canyon. The inhabitants of Pueblo Bonito may have been involved in organizing expeditions and mining the turquoise deposits along the Rio Grande Rift... In contrast, the inhabitants that worked turquoise and produced ornaments in Marcia's Rincon were either long-distance merchants or linked to a trade network that extended to the western resource areas." (p. 193)

**Relevance**: The coexistence of two distinct procurement networks within one community directly supports the Layer 1 prediction of quality-dependent signaling. Elite households (great house) accessed one set of sources; non-elite households (small sites) accessed another. This is consistent with the Spence condition: signal cost (procurement route) differed by signaler quality (social position).

---

### Claim 8: Aztec Ruin procurement matches Pueblo Bonito; Salmon Ruin matches Marcia's Rincon
"There are some similarities in the procurement patterns of Pueblo Bonito and Aztec Ruin (high percentage of the use of the Rio Grande Rift deposits), and in those of Salmon Ruin and the small sites in Marcia's Rincon (use of the western turquoise resource areas)." (p. 193)

**Relevance**: The persistence of procurement pattern differences across sites (Pueblo Bonito/Aztec vs. Salmon/Marcia's Rincon) suggests these were not site-specific preferences but reflected deeper social distinctions. The Chacoan-affiliated sites accessed eastern sources; non-Chacoan or post-Chacoan populations accessed western sources. This pattern persisted as the system expanded northward, supporting the interpretation of socially structured exchange networks.

---

### Claim 9: Trade was bidirectional, with eastern turquoise reaching western sites
"Our study also shows that the trade routes were not just used to move goods in only one direction. Turquoise from Villa Grove was linked to the Moapa Valley (Steve Perkins site) and turquoise from Orogrande was linked to Eagle's Watch." (p. 193)

**Relevance**: The bidirectional movement of turquoise indicates that exchange networks were not simple extraction systems but involved reciprocal flows. For the signaling model, this supports the interpretation that turquoise exchange was embedded in broader social relationships (alliances, cooperation networks) rather than representing one-directional tribute or extraction.

---

### Claim 10: Room 33 burials at Pueblo Bonito contained thousands of turquoise artifacts
"Two 10th century burials recovered in Room 33 were exceptional as they each contained the most elaborate turquoise artifacts and thousands of turquoise pieces (Akins, 1986; Judd, 1954; Pepper, 1996)." (p. 190)

**Relevance**: The concentration of the most elaborate turquoise assemblages in the Room 33 burials (the matrilineal dynasty documented by Kennett et al. 2017) directly links turquoise accumulation to hereditary elite status. This supports the Spence condition: the highest-quality signalers invested the most in signaling goods.

---

### Claim 11: Turquoise manufacturing debris found at Marcia's Rincon small sites
"At one site in particular, the Spadefoot Toad site located in Marcia's Rincon, turquoise debris was recovered suggesting that turquoise ornaments were manufactured there (Mathien, 2001, 1993, 1984; Windes, 1993)." (p. 190)

**Relevance**: Turquoise working was not limited to the great houses, but the production-consumption relationship differed: small sites manufactured ornaments while great houses accumulated finished goods (and also produced, per Mattson 2025). The spatial separation of production and consumption is consistent with a division of labor where artisans worked material from western sources while elites consumed goods from eastern sources.

---

### Claim 12: Virgin Puebloan communities may have been intermediaries in western turquoise trade
"Lost City and other communities throughout the Moapa Valley may have been responsible for the collection and transport of trade commodities such as salt, shell, turquoise and possibly cotton fabrics (Lyneis, 1986); as well as sponsoring mining expeditions that ventured to the western turquoise resource areas (Rafferty, 1990)." (p. 191)

**Relevance**: The identification of intermediary communities in the trade network reveals a multi-node exchange system. For the signaling model, this means the cost of turquoise acquisition was not simply a function of distance to source but was mediated by the structure of the exchange network and relationships with intermediary groups, adding a social dimension to procurement costs.

---

### Claim 13: Eagle's Watch juvenile burial contained extraordinary turquoise and shell assemblage
"One juvenile burial in particular, between the age of three and four, contained an unusual number of grave goods that had been scattered over the remains, including 101 shell disk beads, two cut shell pendants, 56 worked pieces of turquoise, and 121 unworked nuggets of turquoise." (p. 191)

**Relevance**: The association of substantial exotic goods with a juvenile burial (age 3-4) at a Virgin Puebloan site indicates that status was ascribed (inherited) rather than achieved, consistent with the hereditary status differentiation documented at Pueblo Bonito by Kennett et al. 2017. This extends the evidence for ascribed status beyond the Chaco core to the network periphery.

---

### Claim 14: Turquoise deposits in comparative database span 22 resource areas across five states and Mexico
Table 1 (p. 189) provides hydrogen and copper isotope ratios for 22 characterized turquoise resource areas in Arizona (4), California (1), Baja California (1), Colorado (4), Nevada (9), and New Mexico (3).

**Relevance**: The geographic spread of the comparative database (spanning from Baja California to Colorado) establishes the maximum potential spatial extent of turquoise trade networks. The 22 source areas with distinct isotopic fingerprints enable differentiation of procurement routes and identification of shifts in trade network structure over time.

---

### Claim 15: Pueblo Bonito accessed at least five distinct turquoise source areas
Table 2 (p. 192) shows Pueblo Bonito turquoise sourced to Cerrillos Hills (6 artifacts), Villa Grove (5), Orogrande (1), Crescent Peak (3), and Lone Mountain (1), with 12 unidentified.

**Relevance**: The diversity of sources accessed by Pueblo Bonito (five distinct geological deposits spanning from New Mexico to Nevada) demonstrates the breadth of the great house's exchange network. This level of network diversity requires substantial social investment to maintain multiple procurement relationships, consistent with the interpretation that turquoise acquisition cost was a function of network maintenance, not just travel distance.

---

## Summary Assessment

Hull et al. 2014 provide the most direct isotopic evidence for the structure of turquoise trade networks at Chaco Canyon. The central finding for the signaling model is the stark divergence in procurement patterns between Pueblo Bonito (81% from eastern/Rio Grande Rift sources) and the small sites in Marcia's Rincon (100% from western/Nevada sources), demonstrating that socially distinct populations within the same canyon accessed different exchange networks. This supports the Spence condition: turquoise acquisition costs were quality-dependent because access to specific procurement routes was socially mediated. The bidirectional nature of trade and the role of Virgin Puebloan intermediaries reveal a complex multi-node exchange system rather than a simple core-periphery extraction model.
