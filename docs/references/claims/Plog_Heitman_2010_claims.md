## Claim 1: Social differentiation began 150-200 years earlier than previously believed

- **Statement**: New AMS radiocarbon dates and reanalysis of stratigraphy and spatial distribution of materials in the Room 33 mortuary crypt demonstrate that significant social differentiation began in Chaco ca. 150-200 years earlier than suggested by previous research.
- **Page**: 19619 (abstract)
- **Type**: Empirical finding
- **Evidence cited**: New AMS radiocarbon dates on burials from Room 33, reanalysis of Pepper's archival records including unpublished notes and drawings
- **Depends on**: Accuracy of AMS dating, stratigraphic interpretation of Room 33
- **Notes**: Prior consensus placed the rich Room 33 deposits after AD 1020-1030 based on ceramic decorative styles
- **Evidence strength**: Strong (multiple independent dating methods, archival cross-checks)
- **Assessment**: Supported. Two independent labs confirm early dates for Burials 13 and 14.

---

## Claim 2: Burials 13 and 14 date to the late ninth century, not the eleventh century

- **Statement**: Combined AMS dates for Burial 13 yield a median of AD 781 (2-sigma range 691-877) and for Burial 14 a median of AD 873 (2-sigma range 690-873), using Oxcal R_combine. A third individual (femur B) also dates to the ninth century (2-sigma range 676-894).
- **Page**: 19623
- **Type**: Empirical finding
- **Evidence cited**: Table 1 lists 12 AMS dates from two laboratories (AA and CAMS series); paired dates for Burials 13 and 14 from different labs are consistent
- **Depends on**: Calibration accuracy, sample integrity, association of femora with correct burials
- **Notes**: Prior dates for Burials 13 and 14 (AA57713, AA57715; Coltrain et al. 2007) had median dates of AD 821 and 817 respectively, which the new dates confirm
- **Evidence strength**: Strong (replicated across labs, consistent with stratigraphic position)
- **Assessment**: Supported. The consistency of paired samples from different laboratories strengthens confidence.

## Claim 3: Room 33 was used as a mortuary crypt for approximately 300-400 years

- **Statement**: Interment in Room 33 began soon after the initial construction of Pueblo Bonito and continued into the late 11th and possibly early 12th centuries, potentially a span of 300-400 years.
- **Page**: 19623
- **Type**: Empirical finding
- **Evidence cited**: AMS dates spanning from the ninth through twelfth centuries; ceramic decorative styles ranging from Red Mesa (AD 875-1040) to late 11th/early 12th century designs; stratigraphic complexity with 1.2 m separating lowest and uppermost burials
- **Depends on**: Correct association of dates with stratigraphic positions, accuracy of ceramic chronology
- **Notes**: This long duration explains the disarticulated state of many skeletons above the wooden floor; cramped 2x2 m room with 12 burials in 0.5 m deposit made disturbance of prior interments inevitable
- **Evidence strength**: Strong (multiple lines of evidence converge)
- **Assessment**: Supported. The long-duration use strengthens the ancestor-veneration interpretation.

## Claim 4: More than 25,000 turquoise pieces were recovered from Room 33, exceeding all other Southwest sites combined

- **Statement**: More turquoise, at least 25,000 pieces, was recovered from this one small room than from all other prehistoric sites in the entire Southwest combined.
- **Page**: 19622
- **Type**: Empirical finding
- **Evidence cited**: Pepper's catalog of more than 30,000 objects from Room 33, of which >95% were beads, pendants, or other items of turquoise, jet, or shell; Snow 1973 for comparative data
- **Depends on**: Snow (1973) for the comparative total; completeness of Pepper's catalog
- **Notes**: Room 33 is approximately 2x2 m. Most sediment was not screened, suggesting the true count is even higher. Turquoise source is at least 170 km distant (Cerrillos Hills).
- **Evidence strength**: Strong for Pueblo Bonito counts; comparative claim depends on Snow 1973
- **Assessment**: Supported, though the comparative total from Snow (1973) may be outdated.

## Claim 5: Burial 14 was interred with more than 10,000 turquoise beads and 500 turquoise pendants

- **Statement**: Burial 14 was accompanied by more than 10,000 turquoise beads, 500 turquoise pendants, and 3,000 shell beads and pendants, along with unusual items such as a shell trumpet. Burial 13 had almost 6,000 turquoise beads and pendants.
- **Page**: 19624
- **Type**: Empirical finding
- **Evidence cited**: Pepper's excavation records and catalogs
- **Depends on**: Accuracy of Pepper's original counts and associations
- **Notes**: These are the two richest burials in the prehispanic Southwest. They were interred below the wooden plank floor.
- **Evidence strength**: Strong (primary excavation data, large sample)
- **Assessment**: Supported. These quantities are consistent with the Kennett et al. 2017 report of 11,200+ turquoise pieces for Burial 14.

## Claim 6: The wooden plank floor symbolized the boundary between the underworld and upper world

- **Statement**: The wooden floor in Room 33 may have physically symbolized the boundary of the underworld and upperworld, a proposal strengthened by Pepper's discovery that a hole 4 inches in diameter had been cut near the eastern end of one of the planks, perhaps as a sipapu.
- **Page**: 19624
- **Type**: Interpretation
- **Evidence cited**: Pueblo cosmological parallels (White 1935, 1962; Ortiz 1969); Pepper's description of the 4-inch hole in the wooden plank; the placement of Burials 13 and 14 below the floor
- **Depends on**: Validity of ethnographic analogy from historic Pueblo groups; Pepper's observation of the hole
- **Notes**: All Pueblo origin stories describe emergence from the underworld through a small hole (sipapu). This interpretation connects Room 33 to foundational Pueblo cosmological principles.
- **Evidence strength**: Moderate (ethnographic analogy is plausible but not directly testable)
- **Assessment**: Plausible but not demonstrated. The physical evidence (planks, hole) is consistent with the interpretation but does not exclude alternatives.

## Claim 7: Corner caches in Room 33 represent offerings to the four sacred directions

- **Statement**: Caches of artifacts in each corner of Room 33, some in association with corner roof-support posts, included more than 1,500 turquoise pieces, wooden ceremonial sticks, eight of nine flutes, burial mats, and human mandibles. The corner caches show offerings to the four sacred directions, a microcosm of the Chacoan cosmos.
- **Page**: 19625
- **Type**: Interpretation
- **Evidence cited**: Pepper's descriptions of corner-specific deposits at various depths; Pueblo cosmological principles of quadripartite organization (White 1935, 1962; Ortiz 1969; Marcus 2007)
- **Depends on**: Pepper's spatial recording accuracy; ethnographic analogy
- **Notes**: The northeastern and northwestern corners contained a significant proportion of cached materials. Patterned deposition occurred both above and below the wooden floor, indicating sustained practice over time.
- **Evidence strength**: Moderate (spatial pattern is clear; cosmological interpretation is analogical)
- **Assessment**: Supported as patterned deposition; the cosmological interpretation is plausible but relies on ethnographic analogy.

## Claim 8: Pepper's water-disturbance hypothesis for disarticulated remains is incorrect

- **Statement**: The flow of water had not disturbed Room 33 or Room 32. Multiple lines of evidence contradict Pepper's hypothesis that disarticulated burials resulted from water flow through the eastern doorway.
- **Page**: 19622
- **Type**: Methodological finding
- **Evidence cited**: Highly patterned association of vertebrae and burial goods in Room 32; preservation of ceremonial sticks, arrows, and cloth; patterned stratigraphic sequence of alternating sand and black soil layers; denser artifact concentrations in the eastern half of Room 33 (away from the doorway); Pepper's own observation that sediment was very dry
- **Depends on**: Accuracy of Pepper's spatial descriptions; the assumption that water flow would have dispersed materials toward the doorway
- **Notes**: Alternative explanations for disarticulation include disturbance during later interments (room only 2 m wide, 12 burials above the floor in 0.5 m deposit) and secondary burial
- **Evidence strength**: Strong (multiple independent lines of evidence)
- **Assessment**: Supported. The preservation of fragile materials (cloth, wood) is incompatible with significant water disturbance.

## Claim 9: The Chaco mortuary record is significantly underestimated due to historical looting

- **Statement**: Known burials from Chaco significantly underestimate the actual number of individuals who were once interred in the canyon. Extensive excavation of small house cemeteries by professionals and nonprofessionals from the 1880s to the 1930s removed large numbers of burials.
- **Page**: 19620
- **Type**: Empirical finding
- **Evidence cited**: Archival records from Chaco Research Archive; Moorehead (1906) recovering 40-50 pots (~25-35 burials) from a single cemetery in one visit; Hrdlicka (1899) finding burial mounds "ransacked by Navajos"; Judd (1920) noting no undisturbed burial mounds remained
- **Depends on**: Archival reliability; Akins (1986) baseline of 270+ documented individuals from 30+ small houses
- **Notes**: Akins documented 1.5 vessels per small-house burial on average, used to estimate burial counts from pot recovery records
- **Evidence strength**: Strong (multiple independent archival sources converge)
- **Assessment**: Supported. The systematic looting documented from multiple sources makes underestimation certain, though the magnitude is not precisely quantifiable.

## Claim 10: Great house burials were restricted to elites, explaining the absence of great house cemeteries

- **Statement**: The long-observed disparity in burial numbers between small houses and great houses resulted from cultural rules specifying that only some individuals could be buried in Pueblo Bonito and other great houses. Only those with the highest social ranking were interred in great houses.
- **Page**: 19625
- **Type**: Interpretation
- **Evidence cited**: Absence of cemeteries at great houses despite extensive excavation; the spatial-cosmological patterns and symbolically charged materials in Room 33; restricted access (rooms could not be entered from the plaza); higher delta-15N values for great house burials indicating higher animal protein diet (Table 1; Coltrain et al. 2007)
- **Depends on**: Representative sampling of great house areas; delta-15N interpretation
- **Notes**: In most hierarchically organized groups, elites constitute only a small proportion of the population. The dietary evidence is consistent but not conclusive; small-house skeletal comparison needed.
- **Evidence strength**: Moderate to strong (multiple lines converge, but alternative explanations exist)
- **Assessment**: Plausible and well-supported. The alternative (few people lived in great houses) is also noted by the authors but considered less likely given the evidence.

## Claim 11: Chacoan society was organized as a "house society" in the Levi-Strauss sense

- **Statement**: Chacoan society became hierarchically organized in a manner similar to what Levi-Strauss and others have referred to as house societies, where social inequality is legitimized through claims of proximity to cosmological origins and ancestral powers.
- **Page**: 19624-19625
- **Type**: Interpretation
- **Evidence cited**: Levi-Strauss (1988) house society concept; ethnographic parallels from Austronesian societies (Fox 1993, Waterson 1990, McKinnon 1991, 1995); Helms (1998) on access to origins; the material pattern in Room 33 (ancestor burials, cosmological symbols, corner post offerings)
- **Depends on**: Applicability of house society model to pre-contact Southwest; ethnographic analogy validity
- **Notes**: House societies occur at periods of social transformation where new forms of inequality emerge in kin-based societies. Inequality is accepted through claims of autochthony (proximity to cosmological origins).
- **Evidence strength**: Moderate (model fits the data, but alternatives exist)
- **Assessment**: Plausible but not demonstrated. The house society framework is one of several possible interpretive models.

## Claim 12: Delta-15N values indicate great house occupants had higher animal protein diets than other Southwestern populations

- **Statement**: The delta-15N values for Room 33 burials suggest that those buried in Pueblo Bonito had a diet higher in animal protein, a pattern commonly associated with elites, than other individuals from other areas of the northern American Southwest.
- **Page**: 19625; Table 1
- **Type**: Empirical finding
- **Evidence cited**: Table 1 shows delta-15N values ranging from 10.0 to 12.4 per mil for Room 33 burials; Coltrain et al. 2007 for comparative data
- **Depends on**: Coltrain et al. 2007 comparative dataset; standard interpretation of delta-15N as protein intake indicator
- **Notes**: Small-house burial analysis needed to confirm the elite-commoner dietary distinction within Chaco Canyon specifically
- **Evidence strength**: Moderate (data are clear but comparative sample is from outside Chaco)
- **Assessment**: Supported for elevated protein relative to regional averages. The elite-commoner inference within Chaco requires additional data.

## Claim 13: Corner posts in Room 33 may have symbolized sacred mountains bounding the Chacoan world

- **Statement**: Rather than functional roof supports, the corner posts may have referred to the sacred mountains that bounded the Chacoan world. A small room did not need five vertical roof-support posts; at least one did not reach the ceiling and another was leaning against an adjacent wall.
- **Page**: 19625
- **Type**: Interpretation
- **Evidence cited**: Pepper's observations that posts did not serve structural function; ethnographic evidence that beams and posts serve as sites of ritual investment in house societies (Fox 1993, Waterson 1990, McKinnon 1991, 1995); Pueblo cosmology with lands bounded by four sacred mountains
- **Depends on**: Ethnographic analogy; Pepper's observations
- **Notes**: Great house construction consumed enormous quantities of wood from mountains up to 70 km away, possibly because these mountains were sacred cosmological markers
- **Evidence strength**: Weak to moderate (ethnographic analogy; no direct test possible)
- **Assessment**: Plausible but speculative. The non-functional posts are documented; the symbolic interpretation is analogical.

## Claim 14: The 11th-century construction expansion resulted from social processes initiated 150+ years earlier

- **Statement**: Although the significant increase in the size and number of great houses did not commence until about AD 1030, the ritual, political, and social processes that generated that expansion had begun to unfold at least a century and a half earlier.
- **Page**: 19625-19626
- **Type**: Interpretation
- **Evidence cited**: AMS dates showing ninth-century burials with rich assemblages; construction of Room 33 as a mortuary crypt during or immediately after the first room blocks; continued interment through the 10th-12th centuries
- **Depends on**: The dating evidence for early social differentiation (Claims 1-3)
- **Notes**: This challenges the prevailing view that Chacoan complexity was ephemeral or limited to the 11th century
- **Evidence strength**: Strong (the chronological evidence is robust)
- **Assessment**: Supported. The dating evidence clearly demonstrates pre-florescence social differentiation.

## Summary Assessment

Plog & Heitman 2010 is a landmark paper that revises the chronology of social differentiation at Chaco Canyon by approximately 150-200 years. The empirical core (AMS dating, stratigraphic reanalysis of Room 33) is strong and has been independently confirmed by subsequent work (Kennett et al. 2017). The interpretive framework (house societies, cosmological symbolism) is well-argued but relies on ethnographic analogy. For the project's costly signaling model, the key contributions are: (1) establishing that elite status differentiation (a prerequisite for quality-dependent signaling) was present from the founding of Pueblo Bonito, not just during the florescence; (2) documenting extreme concentration of turquoise (25,000+ pieces in one room) as evidence for quality-dependent access to the costliest signaling medium; (3) providing dietary evidence (delta-15N) for differential resource access between great house and small house populations; and (4) demonstrating that mortuary investment persisted for 300+ years, consistent with a stable signaling equilibrium rather than an ephemeral phenomenon.
