## Claim 1: Nine Room 33 individuals share identical mitochondrial genomes

**Statement**: All nine successfully sampled individuals buried in the Room 33 crypt at Pueblo Bonito have identical mitochondrial DNA genome sequences, belonging to a B2y1 haplotype.

**Page**: 3

**Type**: Empirical finding (primary data)

**Evidence cited**: Complete or near-complete mtDNA genomes reconstructed for nine individuals; consensus sequences averaged 16,231 +/- 455 bp (98.1% of the complete mitochondrial genome); fold-sequence coverage ranged from 7.4x to 117x (Supplementary Data 1). All nine sequences were identical (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 5).

**Depends on**: Ancient DNA extraction, capture, and sequencing methods; sequence read quality and damage filtering (Supplementary Fig. 4); minimum 2x independent read coverage per nucleotide position.

**Notes**: The B2 haplogroup has been observed at low frequency among Southwest Native Americans based on hypervariable region sequences (citing Snow et al. 2011). Identical mtDNA across all nine individuals is consistent with membership in a single matriline.

**Evidence strength**: Strong. Complete mitogenomes with high coverage; replicated independently at Penn State and Harvard Medical School for three individuals with identical results.

**Assessment**: This is the central empirical finding of the paper. The identical mitogenomes across nine individuals spanning approximately 330 years provide direct molecular evidence for a matrilineal descent group.

## Claim 2: Nuclear genome data identify first-degree and second-degree kin relationships

**Statement**: Nuclear genome SNP genotyping of six Room 33 individuals identified two pairs with significant genetic relatedness: crania 1 and 7 were likely first-degree relatives (mother-daughter), and crania 8 and 10 were first- or second-degree relatives (grandmother-grandson).

**Page**: 4-5

**Type**: Empirical finding (primary data)

**Evidence cited**: Genotyping of approximately 1 million SNPs across the nuclear genome; average of 73,247 +/- 46,843 SNPs recovered per individual (range: 14,948-144,227). Relatedness coefficient r estimated from intersecting SNPs for each pair; 95% confidence intervals computed using block jackknife. Analysis restricted to 856,473 autosomal SNPs not in CpG dinucleotide context to avoid postmortem damage bias (Supplementary Fig. 6, Supplementary Table 3).

**Depends on**: Sufficient DNA preservation for nuclear genome analysis; statistical power from overlapping SNP coverage between pairs.

**Notes**: All significant relationships were "lineal" (direct line of descent) rather than "collateral." Cranium 1 = 35-45-year-old female; cranium 7 = 23-27-year-old female (one generation apart, suggesting mother-daughter). Cranium 8 = 30-40-year-old female; cranium 10 = 30-35-year-old male (two generations apart, suggesting grandmother-grandson).

**Evidence strength**: Moderate to strong. Nuclear SNP coverage was limited (14,948-144,227 SNPs per individual), but the statistical significance of the relatedness estimates was supported by jackknife standard errors. The authors state this is "the first study using genome-wide data to document hereditary relationships among individuals within an elite lineage using archaeogenomics, in the absence of a written record, anywhere in the world" (p. 5).

**Assessment**: The identified kin relationships are consistent with matrilineal descent. The grandmother-grandson and mother-daughter pairs confirm that the shared mtDNA reflects actual matrilineal kinship, not coincidental haplogroup sharing.

## Claim 3: The Room 33 crypt was used continuously from approximately 800 to 1130 CE

**Statement**: AMS radiocarbon dating places Burial 14 (the lineage founder) at 800-850 CE, with persistent burial continuing until approximately 1130 CE, a span of approximately 330 years.

**Page**: 2-3

**Type**: Empirical finding (chronological)

**Evidence cited**: Bayesian stratigraphic model of AMS 14C dates (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 1); bone collagen extracted and purified using ultrafiltration; stable isotope measurements consistent with maize agriculture (delta-13C: -7 to -9.5 per mille VPDB; delta-15N: 11.1 to 13.3 per mille Atm N2; Supplementary Table 1). Chronological simulations using OxCal v4.2.3 indicate continuous rather than episodic interment (Supplementary Figs 2 and 3).

**Depends on**: AMS 14C calibration via IntCal13 curve; stratigraphic integrity below plank floor; Bayesian modelling assumptions.

**Notes**: Direct dates on abalone shells (Haliotis fulgens) from the southern California coast buried with Burial 14 corroborate the early ninth-century date (Supplementary Table 2). C/N ratios for all samples fell between 3.11 and 3.16, indicating good collagen preservation.

**Evidence strength**: Strong. High-precision AMS dating with Bayesian modelling; multiple dating approaches (bone collagen, associated shell) converge on the same chronology.

**Assessment**: The 330-year span encompasses the entire Bonito phase of Chaco florescence, from initial great house construction through abandonment.

## Claim 4: Burial 14 is the richest burial known in the North American Southwest

**Statement**: Burial 14 was adorned with more than 11,200 turquoise and more than 3,300 shell beads and pendants (necklaces, anklets, and bracelets), along with multiple abalone shells from the Pacific Coast and a conch shell trumpet. The abundance of turquoise alone makes this the richest burial known in the North American Southwest.

**Page**: 2

**Type**: Empirical description (archaeological context)

**Evidence cited**: Citing Pepper 1909 (ref. 19) and Pepper 1920 (ref. 20) for the grave goods inventory. Also references Plog & Heitman 2010 (ref. 11) for the construction and burial context.

**Depends on**: Accuracy of original 1896 excavation records (Pepper).

**Notes**: Burial 14 was a male in his 40s who died from a lethal blow to the head (citing Marden 2011, ref. 18). The body was placed on a prepared floor of sand and wood ashes, then entombed by a 70 cm layer of clean sand. A second individual (Burial 13), also associated with more than 5,800 turquoise pieces, was placed above the sand layer and covered with a unique plank floor.

**Evidence strength**: Strong for the material inventory (based on well-documented 1896 excavation). The claim of "richest burial in the North American Southwest" is the authors' characterization but is consistent with the documented quantities.

**Assessment**: The scale of grave goods associated with the lineage founder provides material evidence for elite status from the very beginning of the dynasty.

## Claim 5: Room 33 was purposely constructed as an elite burial crypt in the ninth century CE

**Statement**: Room 33 was part of the initial construction phase of Pueblo Bonito in the ninth century CE and was purposely constructed as a crypt for a high-status member of the nascent community (Burial 14) and ultimately his lineal descendants.

**Page**: 2

**Type**: Interpretation (archaeological)

**Evidence cited**: Room 33 was small (approximately 2m x 2m) with an atypical, hatch-like entry in the eastern wall just below the roof. Interment inside Chacoan settlements was unusual; most individuals were buried outside in mounds of domestic debris (citing Plog & Heitman 2010, ref. 11).

**Depends on**: Architectural analysis of Room 33 as part of the initial construction phase; interpretation of the hatch entry as purpose-built for body introduction.

**Notes**: The unusual burial pattern (interior crypt vs. exterior trash mound burial) marks the Room 33 occupants as exceptional within Chacoan mortuary practice.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate. The architectural interpretation is reasonable but necessarily involves inference about intent. The stratigraphic relationship to the initial construction phase is well established.

**Assessment**: If Room 33 was purpose-built as a crypt from the beginning, it implies that elite status differentiation was institutionalized at the founding of Pueblo Bonito, not something that developed gradually.

## Claim 6: Hereditary inequality and societal complexity emerged in Chaco by the early ninth century CE

**Statement**: The results indicate that hereditary inequality and societal complexity emerged in Chaco by the early ninth century CE and that this matrilineal dynasty persisted at the centre of this complex society for approximately 330 years until its rapid collapse in the early twelfth century CE.

**Page**: 2

**Type**: Interpretation (synthesis)

**Evidence cited**: Combination of the elite burial context, the 800-850 CE date for the lineage founder, and the continuous use of the crypt through approximately 1130 CE.

**Depends on**: Claims 1-5 above.

**Notes**: This is the paper's primary interpretive conclusion. The authors explicitly frame it as demonstrating that hereditary leadership preceded the fluorescence of Chaco as a regional center, not that it developed during or after the peak.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate. The evidence for early elite status (Burial 14 in the early ninth century) is strong; the inference that this constitutes "hereditary inequality" requires the assumption that the crypt's continuous use by a single matriline reflects institutionalized leadership succession, which is plausible but not directly demonstrated.

**Assessment**: Critical for the costly signaling model: hereditary status provides the quality differentiation that the Spence condition requires. If quality (elite vs. non-elite) is heritable, then the signaling equilibrium is more stable because signalers' type is correlated across generations.

## Claim 7: Chacoan society may have been organized similarly to historic Western Pueblo peoples with ranked matrilineal groups

**Statement**: There is debate over whether Chacoan society was organized like the Western Pueblos (e.g., Hopi), where social groups with a matrilineal core were ranked and controlled ritual sources of power, or like some Eastern Pueblos, where non-kinship-based ritual sodalities selected leaders based on ability rather than descent.

**Page**: 2

**Type**: Framing of scholarly debate

**Evidence cited**: Western Pueblo model citing Whiteley 2008 (ref. 12); Eastern Pueblo model citing Ware 2014 (ref. 13). Broader debate on kinship and unilineal descent groups in complex society evolution citing Godelier 2012 (ref. 14) and Levi-Strauss 1982 (ref. 15); Pueblo social organization debate citing Peregrine 2001 (ref. 16) and Schillaci & Stojanowski 2002 (ref. 17).

**Depends on**: N/A (scholarly background).

**Notes**: The authors' findings support the Western Pueblo model (matrilineal ranking) over the Eastern Pueblo model (achievement-based sodalities). This is significant for the signaling model because it distinguishes between ascribed status (hereditary) and achieved status.

**Evidence strength**: N/A (framing).

**Assessment**: The distinction between these models has direct consequences for Layer 1 of the signaling model. Hereditary status implies that signaler quality (q) is correlated within matrilines, whereas achievement-based status implies that q is individually determined. The paper's findings favor the hereditary model.

## Claim 8: Genetic sex estimates are 100% concordant with osteological determinations

**Statement**: Genetic sex estimates for Room 33 individuals (three female, three male; two oldest individuals likely male but ambiguous) are 100% concordant with the most recent osteological sex determinations (Marden 2011).

**Page**: 4

**Type**: Methodological validation

**Evidence cited**: Ratio of chrY to chrX+Y mapped reads (Ry approach, citing Skoglund et al. 2013); confirmed for six individuals using nuclear genome SNP data. Figure 2c shows Ry ratios with 95% confidence intervals.

**Depends on**: Sufficient X+Y mapped reads (range: 159-28,275 total X+Y reads per individual).

**Notes**: Resolves a discrepancy between two previous osteological studies (Marden 2011 vs. Stewart 1936) for one individual.

**Evidence strength**: Strong for the six confidently sexed individuals; moderate for the two ambiguous cases.

**Assessment**: Confirms the reliability of both the genetic and osteological data, strengthening confidence in the broader results.

## Claim 9: Matrilineal systems are ethnographically documented in all Western Pueblos and among Keresan-speaking Rio Grande Pueblos

**Statement**: Matrilineal systems are present ethnographically in all the Western Pueblos (Hopi, Zuni, Acoma, Laguna) and the Rio Grande Pueblos (Cochiti, San Felipe, Santo Domingo, and Zia) that speak the Keresan language. Pueblo oral traditions, especially among Hopi and Zuni, emphasize matrilineal clans as founding social units and matrilineal leaders as key protagonists in social history.

**Page**: 5

**Type**: Ethnographic context

**Evidence cited**: Citing Whiteley 2008 (ref. 12); Spanish colonial records; Supplementary Note 1.

**Depends on**: Ethnographic and ethnohistoric documentation.

**Notes**: The authors acknowledge that "the relationship between prehistoric people from Chaco and specific modern Native American groups remains uncertain" (p. 5). The parallel is presented as consistent with, not proof of, continuity.

**Evidence strength**: Strong for the ethnographic pattern; the connection to Chaco is an analogy, not a direct demonstration of continuity.

**Assessment**: Relevant for parameterizing the model: matrilineal organization is a plausible structural assumption for Chacoan corporate groups, supported by both the aDNA evidence and the ethnographic record.

## Claim 10: Ritually important objects cached in rooms adjacent to Room 33

**Statement**: Rooms adjacent to Room 33 contained multiple caches of ritually important objects: hundreds of wooden ceremonial staffs; scores of cylindrical ceramic vessels; the remains of scarlet macaws (Ara macao); and jewelry.

**Page**: 2

**Type**: Empirical description (archaeological context)

**Evidence cited**: Citing Pepper 1920 (ref. 20) and Watson et al. 2015 (ref. 21).

**Depends on**: Accuracy of original excavation records and the contextual association between Room 33 and adjacent rooms.

**Notes**: The conjunction of the elite burial crypt with adjacent ritual object caches suggests that the matrilineal dynasty controlled both mortuary privilege and access to ritual materials. Cylindrical vessels were used for cacao consumption (Crown & Hurst 2009, cited elsewhere). Scarlet macaws were imported from Mesoamerica beginning around 900 CE (Watson et al. 2015).

**Evidence strength**: Strong for the material inventory. The association between Room 33 and adjacent rooms is spatial, not stratigraphic.

**Assessment**: The concentration of high-cost ritual materials (macaws, cacao vessels, ceremonial staffs) in the immediate vicinity of the elite crypt supports the interpretation that this matriline controlled access to the most costly exotic goods, consistent with the Spence condition for quality-dependent signaling costs.

## Claim 11: Pueblo Bonito was the largest great house with approximately 650 rooms

**Statement**: Pueblo Bonito was the largest (approximately 650 rooms) of more than a dozen multistoried masonry great houses in Chaco Canyon. By the end of the ninth century CE, there were no fewer than three multistoried great houses in the canyon.

**Page**: 2

**Type**: Empirical description (architectural)

**Evidence cited**: Citing Lekson 1984 (ref. 9) for room counts and great house architecture.

**Depends on**: Lekson's architectural survey.

**Notes**: The paper also states that by the eleventh and early twelfth centuries, at least 10 new great houses were constructed in the canyon, plus scores of outlying settlements that adopted Chacoan architectural style (citing Kantner & Mahoney 2000, ref. 10).

**Evidence strength**: Strong (well-established architectural data).

**Assessment**: Establishes that the matrilineal dynasty documented in Room 33 was based at the largest and most architecturally prominent structure in the Chaco system.

## Claim 12: Interments in Room 33 occurred continuously rather than episodically

**Statement**: Chronological simulations indicate interments occurred continuously, rather than episodically, during the approximately 330-year period, suggesting persistent transgenerational use of the crypt throughout the growth and decline of Chaco as a regional political and religious centre.

**Page**: 2-3

**Type**: Empirical finding (statistical simulation)

**Evidence cited**: Radiocarbon simulations using OxCal v4.2.3 (Supplementary Figs 2 and 3). The simulations modelled potential biases from calibration curve plateaus between 900 and 1150 CE. Ten dates were simulated with +/- 20 14C year precision every 25 calendar years from 750 to 1250 CE (total 210 simulated dates), compared with the observed 11 dates.

**Depends on**: Assumptions of the simulation model; small sample size (n=11) relative to the 330-year period.

**Notes**: The authors acknowledge that gaps in the 14C ages correspond to periods where the calibration curve is relatively steep and are likely artefacts of the curve rather than pauses in interment (p. 6).

**Evidence strength**: Moderate. The simulation approach is appropriate but the sample size is small, limiting resolution.

**Assessment**: Continuous use of the crypt implies that the matrilineal dynasty maintained its elite status without interruption through the entire Bonito phase, including periods of environmental stress and architectural expansion.

## Claim 13: This is the first archaeogenomic documentation of hereditary elite relationships without a written record

**Statement**: To the authors' knowledge, this is the first study using genome-wide data to document hereditary relationships among individuals within an elite lineage using archaeogenomics, in the absence of a written record, anywhere in the world.

**Page**: 5

**Type**: Methodological priority claim

**Evidence cited**: Previous studies of hereditary relationships using short-tandem repeat genotyping in historical contexts (Tutankhamun's family, Hawass et al. 2010; historical genealogies, Gerstenberger et al. 1999; the Romanov family, Rogaev et al. 2009), all of which involved literate societies.

**Depends on**: Comprehensive knowledge of prior studies.

**Notes**: The priority claim pertains specifically to the combination of genome-wide data and the absence of writing. The methodological advance is the application of nuclear SNP genotyping (not just mtDNA) to reconstruct kin relationships in a prehistoric, non-literate society.

**Evidence strength**: N/A (priority claim, plausible as of 2017 publication).

**Assessment**: The methodological precedent is relevant because it demonstrates that hereditary leadership can now be directly tested in archaeological contexts where it was previously inferred solely from material culture.

## Summary Assessment

Kennett et al. 2017 provides the most direct evidence currently available for hereditary elite status at Chaco Canyon. The combination of identical mitogenomes across nine individuals spanning 330 years, nuclear DNA-based kin relationships (mother-daughter, grandmother-grandson), the extraordinary wealth of the lineage founder's burial, and the purpose-built crypt establishes that a single matrilineal dynasty occupied the most privileged mortuary context at the largest structure in the Chaco system from its inception through its collapse. The evidence directly addresses the Spence condition for the costly signaling model: hereditary status creates the quality differentiation (elite vs. non-elite lineages) upon which quality-dependent signaling costs can operate. The persistence of the dynasty through the full 330-year span of Chaco's florescence indicates that the signaling equilibrium was stable across multiple generations.
