## Claim 1: Drinking rituals intensified in Chaco in the AD 1000s and terminated abruptly around AD 1100

- **Statement**: Drinking rituals in Chaco intensified in the AD 1000s, followed by upheaval with the termination and rejection of their most iconic vessel form around AD 1100.
- **Page**: 387
- **Type**: Empirical finding
- **Evidence cited**: Sequence of drinking vessel forms (Figure 1); chronological analysis of cylinder jar production and termination; destruction of Room 28 cylinder jars around AD 1100
- **Depends on**: Ceramic chronology; correct identification of vessel function
- **Notes**: The intensification coincides with peak construction activity and the shift to Gallup Black-on-white decorative style around AD 1030
- **Evidence strength**: Strong (multiple independent lines of vessel evidence)
- **Assessment**: Supported. The chronological pattern is well documented through ceramic sequences and stratified deposits.

## Claim 2: Cylinder jars were crafted with cacao or holly residues beginning in the AD 900s

- **Statement**: Cylinder jars have residue of caffeinated plants interpreted as cacao or holly. Manufacture began with Red Mesa Black-on-white designs that suggest production in the AD 900s.
- **Page**: 394
- **Type**: Empirical finding
- **Evidence cited**: Crown et al. 2015 for residue analysis; ceramic typology for dating
- **Depends on**: Crown et al. 2015 residue identification; ceramic dating
- **Notes**: Early cylinder jars are large and wide (average orifice diameter 15.1 cm) and appear to be Cibola White Ware. They likely served as communal drinking/serving vessels, not personal cups.
- **Evidence strength**: Strong (chemical residue analysis confirms caffeinated content)
- **Assessment**: Supported.

## Claim 3: Cylinder jar form shifted from communal to individual around AD 1030

- **Statement**: Beginning about AD 1030, when Gallup Black-on-white became the dominant decorative style, potters began crafting narrower cylinder jars that could be held in one hand (average orifice diameter 9.8 cm). Some were fashioned in sets of two to four vessels with identical shapes and lug configurations.
- **Page**: 394
- **Type**: Empirical finding
- **Evidence cited**: Orifice diameter measurements (Figure 4); identification of paired/matched sets (Figure 5)
- **Depends on**: Sample representativeness; correct association of style with chronology
- **Notes**: The shift to paired sets indicates shared social etiquette around consumption. Sets of drinking cups also characterize prehispanic Bolivia (Anderson 2009:186) and Late Bronze Age Greece (Stockhammer 2012:19; Wright 2004:99-100).
- **Evidence strength**: Strong (quantitative measurement data; cross-cultural parallels)
- **Assessment**: Supported. The metric data clearly document the size shift.

## Claim 4: Roughly 200 cylinder jars found in Chaco Canyon, with approximately 175 from Pueblo Bonito

- **Statement**: All but five of the roughly 200 cylinder vessels with known proveniences were recovered in Chaco Canyon, and around 175 come from Pueblo Bonito. None were recovered from kivas.
- **Page**: 397; Table 1
- **Type**: Empirical finding
- **Evidence cited**: Table 1 listing cylinder jar proveniences by site and room; Crown 2016b for comprehensive vessel catalog
- **Depends on**: Completeness of provenience records
- **Notes**: Room 28 alone contained 112 cylinder jars. 54% show moderate to heavy external use-wear, and at least 23% show reslipping/repainting layers (Crown and Wills 2003).
- **Evidence strength**: Strong (comprehensive inventory)
- **Assessment**: Supported.

## Claim 5: Cylinder jars were concentrated at Pueblo Bonito and not widely emulated or traded

- **Statement**: As distinctive as cylinder jars were, they were neither widely traded prestige items nor widely emulated or copied. Almost all were kept at Pueblo Bonito. There must have been control over their creation and use.
- **Page**: 399
- **Type**: Empirical finding / interpretation
- **Evidence cited**: Geographic distribution data (Table 1); contrast with Maya practices where elites gifted cylinder jars to guests (Reents-Budet 2006:222)
- **Depends on**: Completeness of the archaeological record at outlier sites
- **Notes**: In contrast to Maya practices, outsiders brought cylinder jars to Pueblo Bonito, where they remained. Some intrusive jars include Chuska White Ware and Showlow Red.
- **Evidence strength**: Moderate to strong (distribution data are clear; control interpretation is inferred)
- **Assessment**: Supported for the distribution pattern. The control interpretation is plausible but not directly demonstrated.

## Claim 6: Cylinder jars were corporate property, not individual possessions

- **Statement**: The discard patterns of cylinder jars suggest that they were not the property of individuals or households. Instead, they likely belonged to a corporate group that stored the vessels in Room 28.
- **Page**: 398
- **Type**: Interpretation
- **Evidence cited**: Cylinder jars rarely used in mortuary practice (only one associated with an individual burial, an adult female in Room 33); most from Room 28 (112 vessels) or ceremonial/performative deposits; 99 jars placed on a shelf, sprinkled with turquoise and shell beads, then deliberately burned in Room 28
- **Depends on**: Representative sampling; correct interpretation of depositional context
- **Notes**: Room 28 had become an underground room with a stairway to the West Court by the time it was used for storing cylinder jars
- **Evidence strength**: Moderate (consistent pattern but interpretation relies on absence of individual association)
- **Assessment**: Plausible. The corporate-property interpretation is more consistent with the depositional data than an individual-ownership model.

## Claim 7: Drinking vessel forms evolved from widely shared regional forms to Pueblo Bonito-specific forms

- **Statement**: From simple vessel forms widely shared over a large region, Chacoans adopted more narrowly regional forms, followed by a shape concentrated in use at Pueblo Bonito and finally a shape associated with northern groups.
- **Page**: 402
- **Type**: Empirical finding
- **Evidence cited**: Sequence documented in Figure 1: decorated tecomates/seed jars (pre-AD 700), gourd jars (AD 650+), pitchers (AD 700+), cylinder jars (AD 900-1100), mugs (AD 1050+); vessel form-chronology associations
- **Depends on**: Ceramic chronological framework
- **Notes**: The trajectory moves from shared/regional forms to exclusive/local forms and back to shared forms (mugs), paralleling broader sociopolitical trajectories
- **Evidence strength**: Strong (well-documented ceramic sequence)
- **Assessment**: Supported.

## Claim 8: The cylinder jar shape was designed for public display and heat retention

- **Statement**: The cylinder jar shape was likely made for public display and to be recognized at a distance. Experimental work comparing heat retention in cylinder jars, jars, and bowls of identical volume confirms that the cylinder form has superior heat retention.
- **Page**: 397; Figure 6
- **Type**: Empirical finding
- **Evidence cited**: Heat retention experiment (Figure 6): 1,000 cc vessels of three shapes tested with 237 cc boiling water; cylinder jar retained heat significantly longer. Association with sandstone disc jar lids suggests preference for hot drinks.
- **Depends on**: Experimental validity (reported by Jill Jordan)
- **Notes**: 86% of cylinder vessels have lugs (small loops or knobs) of various shapes and numbers, but most show no wear and are too fragile for attachment, suggesting decorative/symbolic function
- **Evidence strength**: Moderate (experiment confirms heat retention; public display is inferred from form)
- **Assessment**: Supported for heat retention. Public display function is plausible but inferred.

## Claim 9: The dramatic termination of cylinder jars around AD 1100 indicates the end of competitive ritual events

- **Statement**: The concentrated termination of the cylinder jars indicates the end of their ritual use and perhaps the end of the competitive events in which they were used or the emigration or even expulsion of the group that used them.
- **Page**: 402
- **Type**: Interpretation
- **Evidence cited**: Destruction of 102 vessels in Room 28 (the most extreme cylinder jar termination event); reexcavation showing this occurred around AD 1100; independent evidence for the Room 28 destruction event; two post-AD 1100 vessels at Pueblo Bonito (Chaco-McElmo B/W and Nava B/W) as latest types
- **Depends on**: Chronological precision of the termination event; correct identification of Room 28 destruction as intentional
- **Notes**: The termination was not emulated elsewhere; the cylinder jar form was almost completely repudiated in the region. Only one known post-destruction cylinder jar exists (Tusayan Polychrome, AD 1125-1290, from northeastern Arizona).
- **Evidence strength**: Strong (the termination event is well documented)
- **Assessment**: Supported for termination. The interpretation of competitive dynamics is plausible.

## Claim 10: Drinking customs helped establish hierarchy at Chaco

- **Statement**: The cylinder jars may have helped establish hierarchy in Chaco, because one group had control over their use and the knowledge and power associated with it. The cylinder jars' destruction and the return to individually owned drinking vessels may signal the end of that particular hierarchy.
- **Page**: 402
- **Type**: Interpretation
- **Evidence cited**: Mills 2004:240, 247 for hierarchy linked to control of ritual knowledge; concentration of jars at Pueblo Bonito; corporate ownership pattern; the timing of intensification coinciding with peak construction
- **Depends on**: The corporate ownership interpretation (Claim 6); the hierarchy interpretation
- **Notes**: "It is perhaps no coincidence that the intensification of drinking activity occurred in Chaco when construction events were at their height" (p. 402, citing Lekson 1984)
- **Evidence strength**: Moderate (consistent pattern, but the causal link between drinking and hierarchy is not directly testable)
- **Assessment**: Plausible. The correlation between drinking intensification and construction activity is consistent with the hierarchy interpretation but does not exclude alternatives.

## Claim 11: Pitchers were the primary individual drinking vessel throughout the Bonito phase

- **Statement**: By the AD 800s, the pitcher was likely the primary drinking vessel for individuals in Chaco. Pitchers were the most common drinking vessel form throughout the sequence.
- **Page**: 393
- **Type**: Empirical finding
- **Evidence cited**: Chaco mortuary data (Figure 2; Akins 1986): pitchers are common burial accompaniments with both male and female adults, and more common in great house burials than small site burials; Toll and McKenna 1997 ceramic analysis
- **Depends on**: Representative burial sample; correct vessel-function identification
- **Notes**: Novice potters made pitchers more than any other form. Most pitchers were Cibola White Ware, a possibly local product.
- **Evidence strength**: Strong (large sample from mortuary and domestic contexts)
- **Assessment**: Supported.

## Claim 12: Exotic drink ingredients (cacao and holly) were the highest-status drinks at Chaco

- **Statement**: Exotic drink ingredients in Chaco likely included both cacao and holly. Brought from a distance, these were probably the highest-status drinks in Chaco Canyon after around AD 900.
- **Page**: 400
- **Type**: Empirical finding / interpretation
- **Evidence cited**: Crown and Hurst 2009 for cacao residue in cylinder jars; Crown et al. 2015 for ritual drinks in the pre-Hispanic US Southwest and Mexican Northwest; Washburn et al. 2011, 2013 for widespread cacao consumption
- **Depends on**: Residue identification accuracy; correct provenance of ingredients
- **Notes**: "Prestige drinks are economic commodities that are easily divisible, cannot be stored for long periods and are not intended for accumulation, but for consumption" (Vencl 1994:313)
- **Evidence strength**: Strong for presence of cacao/holly; moderate for "highest-status" ranking
- **Assessment**: Supported.

## Claim 13: Consumption of caffeinated drinks continued after cylinder jar termination

- **Statement**: The consumption of caffeinated drinks did not cease with the termination of the cylinder jars. Five mugs from Pueblo Bonito, and mugs at other Chacoan sites, have cacao residue.
- **Page**: 400
- **Type**: Empirical finding
- **Evidence cited**: Crown et al. 2015 for residue analysis of mugs
- **Depends on**: Residue identification accuracy
- **Notes**: The persistence of cacao consumption in different vessels (mugs replacing cylinder jars) suggests the drink ingredient retained value even after the associated ritual form was destroyed
- **Evidence strength**: Moderate (small sample of tested mugs)
- **Assessment**: Supported but based on limited sample.

## Claim 14: Drinking vessel diversity indicates competitive social differentiation

- **Statement**: An increase in the number of distinct drinking vessel forms is an indication of intensified drinking activity, particularly drinking in groups, and likely indicates varied recipes or rituals as well.
- **Page**: 388
- **Type**: Theoretical claim (with cross-cultural support)
- **Evidence cited**: Anderson 2009; Joyce and Henderson 2010:158; Smith et al. 2003; cross-cultural pattern documented in Ancient Greece, Mesoamerica, and the Andes
- **Depends on**: Cross-cultural analogy validity
- **Notes**: In complex societies, the number of distinct drinking vessel forms may decrease when produced by specialists (Hamilakis 1999:48-49). At Chaco, vessel diversity increased and then collapsed.
- **Evidence strength**: Moderate (cross-cultural pattern is well documented; Chaco application is analogical)
- **Assessment**: Plausible. The cross-cultural evidence is consistent but the Chaco-specific mechanism is not directly tested.

## Summary Assessment

Crown 2018 provides a detailed reconstruction of drinking practices at Pueblo Bonito spanning approximately 700 years (AD 500-1200). The paper's central contribution is documenting how drinking vessel forms and associated rituals served as arenas for social differentiation and competitive display. For the costly signaling model, the key contributions are: (1) cylinder jars as corporate-owned ritual objects whose production, use, and destruction were controlled by a specific group at Pueblo Bonito, making them quality-dependent signaling instruments; (2) the temporal correlation between drinking ritual intensification and peak construction activity, suggesting coordinated deployment of multiple signaling channels; (3) the abrupt termination of cylinder jars around AD 1100, which provides a precise chronological marker for the collapse of one signaling system; (4) cacao and holly as imported, caffeinated drink ingredients representing individual-level exotic goods signals (Layer 1); and (5) the shift from communal to individual vessel forms around AD 1030, paralleling the intensification of social competition during the Classic Bonito phase.
