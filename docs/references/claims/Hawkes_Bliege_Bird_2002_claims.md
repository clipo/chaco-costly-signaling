## Claims

### Claim 1

**Statement**: The handicap principle (Zahavi 1975, 1977) makes "waste" a common outcome of signal selection because the cost of a signal guarantees its honesty. "The capacity to bear the cost reveals the show-off's hidden qualities."
**Page**: p. 58
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Zahavi 1975, 1977; Grafen 1990; Johnstone 1995, 1997
**Depends on**: Formal signaling theory
**Notes**: Foundational statement of the handicap principle, directly relevant to the project's Layer 1 (individual signaling equilibrium).
**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: Standard characterization of the handicap principle. Well-established in the theoretical literature.

---

### Claim 2

**Statement**: A critical distinction exists between displays that provide "little but information" and those that "provide benefits to the audience in addition to information." In the latter case (altruistic signaling), "the show-off provides something besides information about a hidden quality to the audience."
**Page**: p. 58
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Zahavi 1995, 2003
**Depends on**: Zahavi's theoretical framework
**Notes**: This distinction between wasteful and altruistic signals is fundamental to the project. Great house construction at Chaco could be altruistic signaling: it provides ceremonial space, storage, and community facilities in addition to signaling the group's capacity. This connects to Layer 3 (cooperation networks).
**Evidence strength**: Strong (theoretical distinction from formal models)
**Assessment**: Well-grounded distinction with significant implications for archaeological applications.

---

### Claim 3

**Statement**: "Collective-action problems do not arise in handicap models because it is mutually beneficial to both show-off and audience to have the information about the show-off's qualities revealed. Show-offs obtain differential treatment only by paying the signal cost; signal recipients obtain information about a signaler's quality only by attending to the signal."
**Page**: p. 61
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Theoretical argument
**Depends on**: Formal signaling theory
**Notes**: This is a key theoretical insight for the project. It explains why costly signaling can sustain cooperation without reciprocity, resolving the free-rider problem. Applied to Chaco, it means great house construction need not be explained by reciprocal exchange or coercion.
**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: Theoretically rigorous. This is the central advantage of the signaling framework over reciprocity-based models.

---

### Claim 4

**Statement**: Among forager societies, meat of large game animals functions like a public good because "if supplied by one, consumption benefits will be claimed by many." Hunters do not control meat distributions and "no quantitative studies of food sharing have found consumers repaying the hunter by returning shares of meat to him."
**Page**: pp. 59-60
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Kaplan & Hill 1985, Hawkes et al. 2001, Bliege Bird & Bird 1997, multiple ethnographic studies
**Depends on**: Quantitative ethnographic data from Ache, Hadza, Meriam
**Notes**: The public-goods nature of hunted meat parallels the public-goods nature of monumental architecture at Chaco. Great houses provided ceremonial space, storage, and community identity that could not be privately consumed.
**Evidence strength**: Strong (based on quantitative ethnographic data)
**Assessment**: Well-supported by multiple independent datasets.

---

### Claim 5

**Statement**: "Men rated as better hunters had much higher fertility" among the Ache, and better Ache hunters "were more often named by women as lovers and as secondary fathers of more children." Similar patterns hold for Hadza hunters (better hunters have younger wives, surviving children faster) and Meriam turtle hunters (higher age-specific reproductive success).
**Page**: pp. 61-62
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Hill & Hurtado 1996 (Ache), Kaplan & Hill 1985 (Ache), Blurton Jones et al. 1997 (Hadza), Hawkes et al. 2001 (Hadza), Smith & Bliege Bird 2000 (Meriam)
**Depends on**: Demographic and behavioral data from forager societies
**Notes**: Demonstrates that costly signaling through resource provisioning generates measurable fitness benefits for signalers. This is the empirical validation of Layer 1 (individual signaling equilibrium). If applied to Chaco, the prediction is that great house builders should have received social advantages.
**Evidence strength**: Strong (multiple independent datasets)
**Assessment**: Among the strongest empirical support for costly signaling in humans.

---

### Claim 6

**Statement**: Among both the Hadza and the Meriam, "those households who supply meat in greater quantities or who supply it more frequently do not seem to be rewarded for their generosity with more meat supplied by others or more frequent receipts." This directly contradicts the reciprocal altruism explanation for food sharing.
**Page**: p. 62 (Box 2)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Hawkes et al. 2001 (Hadza), Bliege Bird & Bird 1997 (Meriam)
**Depends on**: Quantitative sharing data with correlation analyses
**Notes**: The Hadza data show R=0.216 (p=0.3899) for kg supplied vs. kg received; Meriam data show R=0.102 (p=0.4429). Neither relationship is significant. This eliminates reciprocity as the explanation for sharing and supports the signaling alternative.
**Evidence strength**: Strong (quantitative data with statistical tests)
**Assessment**: Important negative result that strengthens the signaling interpretation.

---

### Claim 7

**Statement**: "Social prestige functions like a peacock's tail or the song of a songbird. It attracts collaborators and deters rivals." Quoting Zahavi (1995), the authors argue that the benefits of costly signaling include both attracting allies and deterring competitors.
**Page**: p. 61
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Zahavi 1995
**Depends on**: Zahavi's handicap principle applied to social behavior
**Notes**: This dual function (attraction + deterrence) maps directly onto the project's Layer 2 (intergroup assessment and conflict deterrence) and Layer 3 (cooperation networks). Great houses attract pilgrims/allies (Layer 3) while deterring competitor groups (Layer 2).
**Evidence strength**: Moderate (theoretical claim supported by analogy)
**Assessment**: Compelling analogy. The dual-function claim is central to the project.

---

### Claim 8

**Statement**: "Audience sensitivity (receiver bias) affects the nature of displays because signals must be detected effectively by appropriate recipients." Signals designed to acquire social standing in a group "should be directed to the group at large" and signalers "gain a larger share by providing more of what the viewers want to see or consume than the competition provides."
**Page**: p. 65
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Ryan 1990, Endler 1992, 1993; Guilford & Dawkins 1993
**Depends on**: Sensory ecology of signal reception
**Notes**: This receiver-bias argument explains why monuments and public goods are favored signal media: they reach the widest audience. "Broadcast effectiveness" helps explain why Chaco great houses, visible across the canyon, were effective signals.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: Well-grounded in sensory ecology literature.

---

### Claim 9

**Statement**: Competition among signalers intensifies work effort. "Better Ache hunters spend even more time hunting. The same appears to be true for the !Kung and Hadza as well." Arabian babblers "compete among themselves for the opportunity to spend time as group sentinels." When productive activities are effective displays, "increased productivity can be the outcome of status competition."
**Page**: p. 64 (Box 3)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Hill & Hawkes 1983 (Ache), Lee 1979 (!Kung), Hawkes et al. 2001 (Hadza), Bliege Bird et al. 2001 (Meriam), Carlisle & Zahavi 1986 (babblers)
**Depends on**: Cross-species behavioral data
**Notes**: This competitive escalation dynamic is relevant to understanding the rapid intensification of great house construction at Chaco during the Classic Bonito period (AD 1020-1130). The model predicts escalation when inter-group competition intensifies.
**Evidence strength**: Strong (multiple independent cases)
**Assessment**: Consistent pattern across species and societies.

---

### Claim 10

**Statement**: Language expands the audience for costly signals beyond those physically present. "Among people, the story of a hunter's success spreads to a wide audience, though few, if any, of its members actually saw him capture the prey." However, "talk is cheap," so honesty is maintained because "it is not a hunter himself who touts his own exploits" and reputations depend on the stories told by many independent observers.
**Page**: p. 64
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Lee 1968, 1979 (ethnographic accounts of !Kung hunter self-effacement)
**Depends on**: Ethnographic observations
**Notes**: For the Chaco application, this implies that the audience for great house signaling extended well beyond those who could physically see the construction. Road networks, pilgrimage, and trade connections could all transmit information about construction investment.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: Plausible extension of signaling theory to human cultural contexts.

---

### Claim 11

**Statement**: "While it is just as costly for signalers to give an altruistic signal as an equivalently wasteful display, it may not be equally beneficial. Some observers may be more interested in 'altruistic' signals than 'wasteful' ones because such signals provide more than simply information, because they provide different sorts of information, or both."
**Page**: p. 64
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Hawkes 2001, Smith & Bliege Bird (n.d.)
**Depends on**: Theoretical argument about signal form selection
**Notes**: This argument for the superiority of altruistic over wasteful signals explains why monument construction (which provides direct benefits) would be favored over pure conspicuous waste. At Chaco, great houses provided functional community facilities in addition to their signaling value.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: Theoretically compelling but empirically difficult to distinguish from alternative explanations.

---

### Claim 12

**Statement**: The show-off hypothesis "does not imply that men contribute little to subsistence. On the contrary, the showoff hypothesis and costly signaling can help explain how individuals seeking competitive advantages can increase their own standing and so earn preferential treatment by acting in ways that supply highly valued benefits to others."
**Page**: p. 65
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: General conclusion
**Depends on**: Theoretical framework developed in the paper
**Notes**: This resolves the apparent paradox of wasteful signaling producing socially beneficial outcomes. Applied to Chaco, it means that individually motivated signaling (great house construction) can generate system-level benefits (conflict deterrence, cooperation networks) without requiring collective decision-making.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate (theoretical conclusion)
**Assessment**: This is the core insight linking individual incentives to collective outcomes, which is the project's central argument.

---

## Summary Assessment

Hawkes & Bliege Bird 2002 provides the theoretical foundation for understanding costly signaling as a mechanism that links individual competitive behavior to collective outcomes. The paper establishes three key points for the project: (1) costly signaling resolves collective action problems without reciprocity; (2) altruistic signals (which provide direct benefits to audiences) are favored over purely wasteful displays; (3) signaling simultaneously attracts allies and deters rivals. The ethnographic evidence from Ache, Hadza, and Meriam societies demonstrates that signalers receive measurable fitness benefits, validating the individual-rationality assumption in Layer 1. The distinction between information-only and altruistic signals directly informs the interpretation of Chaco great houses as providing both information and direct community benefits.
