## Claims

### Claim 1

**Statement**: A new low-frequency reconstruction of US Southwest summer temperatures from 3000 BC to present, based on pollen records from 29 southwestern US fossil pollen sites, shows generally warmer temperatures until the first century AD. From then until AD 1000, temperatures were generally cooler, though a warming trend continuing to present begins ca. AD 400, interrupted by cooling ca. AD 900 and 1400.
**Page**: pp. 455-456 (abstract), pp. 458-459
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Gillreath-Brown et al. 2024 (companion paper); modern analog technique (MAT) from Overpeck et al. 1985; ~2000 pollen samples
**Depends on**: Age-depth models for pollen cores; modern analog matching with temperature/precipitation data
**Notes**: The reconstruction is specifically for July temperatures, using loess smoothing (alpha = 0.15). It is sensitive to multi-decadal and centennial variability rather than annual/decadal variation captured by tree rings.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: The pollen-based reconstruction fills an important gap: tree-ring records are insensitive to low-frequency temperature change. The methodology (MAT) is well-established in paleoclimatology.

---

### Claim 2

**Statement**: The difference between the new SWUS temperature reconstruction and the Moberg et al. (2005) Northern Hemisphere reconstruction indexes the mean state of ENSO. When the SWUS is cooler than hemispheric norms, this indicates El Nino-like conditions (cool/wet in the SWUS); when warmer, La Nina-like conditions (warm/dry).
**Page**: pp. 460-462 (Figure 3)
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: ENSO teleconnection literature (Freund et al. 2019; Sandweiss et al. 2020; Heyer et al. 2017; Jimenez-Moreno et al. 2021); comparison of two temperature reconstructions
**Depends on**: The assumption that ENSO effects on SWUS temperature are larger than on hemispheric temperature; Moberg et al. 2005 reconstruction
**Notes**: The proposed "ENSO Index" (Figure 3) shows: El Nino-like conditions dominated much of 2nd millennium BC and 1st millennium AD; La Nina-like conditions dominated 800 BC to AD 100 and AD 1200 to ca. 1900.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: The logic is plausible but the ENSO Index is constructed from the difference of two reconstructions with their own uncertainties. Proxy records of ENSO from Peru (Moy et al. 2002; Rein et al. 2005) provide some independent support but are not perfectly concordant.

### Claim 3

**Statement**: El Nino-like conditions (cool/wet) dominated the first millennium AD up until around AD 1100, providing the climatic framework that underwrote high maize production, the regional Neolithic Demographic Transition, and the emergence of the Chacoan polity.
**Page**: pp. 469-471
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: ENSO Index (Figure 3); population growth data (Dean et al. 1994; Hill et al. 2004; Kohler et al. 2008; Kohler and Reese 2014); NDT literature (Ortman et al. 2016; Wilshusen 2018)
**Depends on**: Claims 1, 2; population and settlement data
**Notes**: The argument is that El Nino brings wetter winters and cooler summers to the SWUS, both favorable for maize. The near-neutral to El Nino-dominated ENSO state from AD 1 to AD 1100 provided over a millennium of generally favorable conditions.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: The correlation between El Nino-like conditions and demographic/political expansion is compelling at the centennial scale. However, the causal mechanism (El Nino favorable for maize) is well-established from modern observations. This is directly relevant to the environmental uncertainty parameter sigma in the model.

### Claim 4

**Statement**: The Chacoan regional system "depended on surpluses in many portions of the SWUS that favored population growth and development of leadership in numerous local societies. In turn this made possible the flow of maize surpluses/taxes (and flows of labor as well) forming a large network of ceremonial, political, and economic dependencies."
**Page**: p. 470
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Crabtree et al. 2017 model; general Chaco literature
**Depends on**: Chaco as a tributary system dependent on surplus extraction
**Notes**: This framing of Chaco as dependent on surplus flows from the periphery is directly relevant to the model's Layer 2 (intergroup assessment) and Layer 3 (cooperation networks). The "flow of maize surpluses/taxes" represents one possible benefit channel for monument signaling.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: The surplus-flow interpretation is widely held but debated. Alternative models (pilgrimage, reciprocal exchange) would change the implications for signaling theory.

### Claim 5

**Statement**: High-frequency drought in the mid-1100s (Sebastian 1992) accompanying a diminution of El Nino-like conditions may have contributed to the decline of the Chaco Canyon center itself. The ENSO Index reached its most extreme values in the AD 1000s as the system was expanding most rapidly.
**Page**: p. 470
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Sebastian 1992; ENSO Index (Figure 3)
**Depends on**: Claims 2, 3
**Notes**: The coincidence of peak ENSO Index (most El Nino-like) with Chaco's maximum expansion, and the shift toward La Nina-like conditions with Chaco's decline, is consistent with the model's prediction that signaling systems collapse when environmental conditions shift beyond the system's buffering capacity.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: The temporal correlation is suggestive but multiple factors contributed to Chaco's decline. The ENSO shift adds a low-frequency climate dimension that complements the well-documented high-frequency drought (megadrought of the 1130s-1150s).

### Claim 6

**Statement**: Around AD 1200, ENSO dynamics entered a brief La Nina-like phase, the first such period in the SWUS in more than a millennium. Dean (1996:38-44) called attention to an extraordinary breakdown in a long-stable pattern of spatial relationships in precipitation between AD 1250 and 1450.
**Page**: p. 470
**Type**: empirical/interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Dean 1996; ENSO Index (Figures 2, 3)
**Depends on**: Claim 2; Dean's tree-ring network analysis
**Notes**: The breakdown in spatial precipitation patterns corresponds to a fundamental reorganization of the climate regime. For networks calibrated to one mode of climate variability, such a shift would be destabilizing, consistent with Gauthier 2021's finding that social adaptations to one mode of variability are fragile to changes in that variability's nature.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (Dean's empirical finding is well-documented from 27 tree-ring chronologies)
**Assessment**: This provides a climate-regime-shift mechanism for the 13th-century crisis that goes beyond simple drought. The regime shift implies that exchange networks structured by previous climate correlations would lose their risk-buffering value.

### Claim 7

**Statement**: The social chaos of the 1200s, including high population movement, frequent conflict, and eventual depopulation of large portions of the Southwest, is connected to the cessation of the El Nino pattern. The depopulation was due to an interaction of demographic, environmental, and social factors; the cessation of El Nino-like conditions is proposed as an additional contributing cause.
**Page**: pp. 470-471
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Kohler et al. 2014; Lipe 1995, 2010; Schwindt et al. 2016; Bocinsky et al. 2016
**Depends on**: Claims 2, 5, 6
**Notes**: The authors propose adding the ENSO regime shift to the list of contributing causes for the 13th-century depopulation, alongside the well-documented high-frequency droughts and social dynamics.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: The argument that a low-frequency climate shift contributed to systemic instability is plausible and adds a dimension not captured by tree-ring-based drought reconstructions alone. For the model, this suggests that sigma (environmental uncertainty) may have changed not just in magnitude but in kind during the late 1200s.

### Claim 8

**Statement**: Maize reached the SWUS at the site of Las Capas, Arizona, as early as the middle of the fourth millennium BC, significantly earlier than previously recognized. The average rate of spread from Oaxaca northward was approximately 0.001 degrees latitude per year (r-squared = 0.01; p > F = 0.72), though the relationship is not significant.
**Page**: pp. 463-465 (Figure 5)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Mabry 2008 (Las Capas); Blake et al. 2017 (Ancient Maize Map); radiocarbon dates (IntCal20 calibration)
**Depends on**: Radiocarbon dating of maize macrofossils
**Notes**: The rate of spread is better quantified when restricted to the northern frontier of maize: 0.003 degrees/year when restricted to the northern frontier (r-squared = 0.76; p > F < 0.001) and 0.002 degrees/year for the SWUS only (r-squared = 0.95; p > F < 0.001).

**Evidence strength**: Strong (based on radiocarbon-dated maize macrofossils)
**Assessment**: Provides baseline data on when maize agriculture became possible at different latitudes, relevant to understanding the agricultural context of Chaco.

### Claim 9

**Statement**: Low-frequency climate processes set the baseline from which high-frequency variability departs. L-f processes impart "great inertia" to cultural systems. A decadal drought has more severe effects on farming if it takes place during a l-f hot/dry period than during a cold/wet period.
**Page**: pp. 462-463
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Dean 1988b, 1996
**Depends on**: Conceptual framework from Dean
**Notes**: This "waves and tides" analogy is important for the model: the sigma parameter should reflect both high-frequency (annual/decadal) and low-frequency (centennial) variability. A drought during a La Nina-dominated period is worse than the same drought during an El Nino-dominated period because the baseline is already unfavorable.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (conceptual framework, not empirically quantified in this paper)
**Assessment**: The framework has important implications for parameterizing environmental uncertainty in the model. Sigma should be conditioned on the low-frequency baseline state.

### Claim 10

**Statement**: Population growth rates in the prehispanic Southwest peaked in the first millennium AD, a period deemed a Neolithic Demographic Transition (NDT). For the Southwest, Lesure et al. (2021) distinguished a low-growth phase of the NDT from 1200 BC to AD 400, followed by a high-growth phase from AD 500 to 1300.
**Page**: p. 469
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Dean et al. 1994; Hill et al. 2004; Kohler et al. 2008; Kohler and Reese 2014; Ortman et al. 2016; Wilshusen 2018; Lesure et al. 2021
**Depends on**: Multiple independent population estimates
**Notes**: The NDT high-growth phase (AD 500-1300) corresponds closely to the period of El Nino-like conditions, and the subsequent population decline corresponds to the shift to La Nina-like conditions.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (multiple independent studies converge on this pattern)
**Assessment**: Provides the demographic context for Chaco: the florescence occurred during the late stage of rapid population growth, when carrying capacity constraints were beginning to bind.

### Claim 11

**Statement**: The hierarchical society at Chaco Canyon was already established in the ninth century (Plog and Heitman 2010). According to the Crabtree et al. (2017) model, the expansion of the Chacoan regional system depended on surpluses that favored population growth and leadership development in local societies.
**Page**: pp. 469-470
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Plog and Heitman 2010; Crabtree et al. 2017
**Depends on**: Archaeological evidence for ninth-century hierarchy; Crabtree et al. agent-based model
**Notes**: The ninth-century dating of Chacoan hierarchy is important: it places the initial development of the signaling system well before the peak of construction investment (1020-1110 CE), suggesting a long period of system development before peak signaling.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: The Crabtree et al. model is one interpretation among several. The chronology of hierarchy establishment is relevant to understanding when the signaling equilibrium first formed.

### Claim 12

**Statement**: By AD 1 or earlier, El Nino-like conditions became dominant in the SWUS. These generally cool/wet conditions would adhere until around AD 1100. In the first millennium AD and up until the near-neutral AD 1100-1300 period, El Nino-like conditions provided the climatic framework underwriting high maize production.
**Page**: p. 471
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Conroy et al. 2008; ENSO Index (Figure 3)
**Depends on**: Claims 1, 2
**Notes**: This establishes a ~1100-year window of favorable conditions preceding the 13th-century crisis. The model should account for the possibility that the signaling system evolved during and was calibrated to this long favorable period.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: The long-duration favorability claim rests on the ENSO Index reconstruction, which has substantial uncertainty at the centennial scale.

## Claim Type Definitions

- **Empirical**: Direct observation or measurement reported by the author
- **Interpretive**: Inference from empirical data; not directly observable
- **Speculative**: Acknowledged by the author as tentative, or basis unclear
- **Methodological**: Claim about how to conduct or interpret research

## Evidence Strength Ratings

- **Strong**: Claim is well-supported by the evidence presented
- **Moderate**: Claim is partially supported; some evidence but gaps remain
- **Weak**: Claim is poorly supported by the evidence presented
- **Assertion without evidence**: No evidence cited; claim is stated as fact without support

## Summary Assessment

Gillreath-Brown & Kohler 2024 provides a new low-frequency temperature reconstruction for the US Southwest and proposes an ENSO Index derived from differences between this reconstruction and Northern Hemisphere temperature norms. The paper connects long-term ENSO state variability to the major demographic and political developments in the Southwest, including the Neolithic Demographic Transition, the rise and fall of the Chacoan system, and the 13th-century depopulation. For the signaling model, the paper's most important contributions are: (1) establishing that the Chaco florescence occurred during a long period of El Nino-dominated (favorable) conditions, (2) linking the system's decline to a shift toward La Nina-like conditions, and (3) providing a framework (low-frequency baseline plus high-frequency variation) for understanding how environmental uncertainty (sigma) should be parameterized. The paper does not address signaling theory but provides essential paleoclimate context for calibrating the model's environmental parameters.
