## Claims

### Claim 1

**Statement**: Buffering against food scarcity uses selected aspects of variability to dampen the effects of others: exploiting resource heterogeneity through diversification, spatial variability through mobility and exchange, and temporal variability through storage.
**Page**: p. 123
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: Synthesis of volume contributions
**Depends on**: Volume case studies
**Notes**: Foundational taxonomy of four buffering mechanisms. Directly parallel to the model's conceptualization of how Chaco communities managed environmental uncertainty.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (well-established framework)
**Assessment**: This four-part taxonomy (mobility, diversification, storage, exchange) is a standard reference point in risk-buffering literature. For the Chaco model, exchange and storage are the most relevant mechanisms, with great house construction potentially serving as both a signaling device and a coordination point for exchange-based buffering.

### Claim 2

**Statement**: Storage embraces not only the direct preservation of foods on an intermediate timescale, but also short-term storage of time through specialized technology and indirect storage in the form of livestock, valuable craft goods, and currencies over progressively longer periods.
**Page**: p. 123
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: O'Shea (this volume); Jongman and Dekker (this volume); Rowley-Conwy and Zvelebil (this volume)
**Depends on**: Volume case studies
**Notes**: The concept of "indirect storage" through valuables is directly relevant to exotic goods at Chaco. Turquoise, macaws, and other exotic items could function as a form of social storage, convertible into obligations and alliances during times of need.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (well-supported conceptually)
**Assessment**: The indirect storage concept provides an alternative framing for exotic goods accumulation at Chaco: rather than (or in addition to) costly signals, exotic goods may represent stored social capital convertible during crises. The two framings are not mutually exclusive; signaling and social storage can be complementary functions.

### Claim 3

**Statement**: Compatible buffering mechanisms tend to operate at the same levels of scale, while antagonistic mechanisms tend to be separated at differing hierarchical levels. This separation of incompatible buffering mechanisms is an important aspect of hierarchised risk-management strategies.
**Page**: p. 124
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: Volume case studies
**Depends on**: Comparative analysis across volume contributions
**Notes**: The concept of hierarchized buffering strategies maps onto the model's multi-scale structure: individual-level mechanisms (diversification, household storage) operate at one scale, while group-level mechanisms (monument-based signaling, inter-community exchange) operate at a higher scale.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: Directly supports the nested signaling model. Low-level (household) and high-level (great house/community) mechanisms at Chaco are structurally separated, consistent with the prediction that incompatible mechanisms are hierarchically segregated.

### Claim 4

**Statement**: When the selective forces acting on high-level decision makers are different from those on producers, a structural conflict can exist between high- and low-level mechanisms, as when centres are largely insulated from shortages, or in the case of a parasitic elite. Upper-level mechanisms may exacerbate shortage at the local level.
**Page**: p. 124
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: Jongman and Dekker (this volume)
**Depends on**: Historical case studies
**Notes**: Relevant to debates about Chaco elite exploitation. If great house elites were insulated from shortages while diverting 35% of community productivity to construction, the high-level buffering mechanism (signaling) could undermine household-level provisioning.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (well-supported conceptually)
**Assessment**: This identifies a potential failure mode for the Chaco system: the signaling mechanism (monument construction) diverts resources from subsistence, and if elites are insulated from the consequences, the system may persist past the point where it benefits the broader population. This structural conflict could accelerate collapse under sustained drought.

### Claim 5

**Statement**: Dependence on storage and exchange is often associated with, and reinforced by, increasing aggregation of population, creating conditions favourable for the growth of warfare, intensified regional interaction, and social stratification. The linking of durable valuables to buffering in "social storage" provides a platform for the unequal accumulation of wealth, status, and power.
**Page**: p. 125
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: Halstead (this volume); Minc and Smith (this volume); O'Shea (this volume); Rowley-Conwy and Zvelebil (this volume)
**Depends on**: Comparative analysis
**Notes**: Describes a pathway from risk-buffering to social complexity that closely parallels the Chaco trajectory: population aggregation at great houses, intensified regional interaction through the outlier network, social stratification reflected in differential burial treatment and access to exotic goods.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: The "social storage" concept, where durable valuables serve both as risk-buffering instruments and as platforms for status accumulation, provides a compelling framework for interpreting turquoise and other exotic goods at Chaco. The dual function (risk buffer + status marker) is consistent with the model's individual-level signaling channel.

### Claim 6

**Statement**: In complex societies, conflict arising from markedly unequal entitlement to food was often a source of political change. The ability of local elites to mount hazardous expeditions overseas to dispose of or acquire surplus food may have been a vital ingredient in cementing inequalities of wealth and power.
**Page**: p. 125
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: Jongman and Dekker (this volume); Forbes (this volume); Garnsey and Morris (this volume)
**Depends on**: Historical case studies (Rome, Holland, pre-classical Greece)
**Notes**: The parallel to Chaco long-distance exchange is striking: elite control over exotic goods acquisition (turquoise from Cerrillos, macaws from Mesoamerica) could function similarly to the Mediterranean food trade in cementing social inequality.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (historical parallels, not formal model)
**Assessment**: Provides cross-cultural support for the mechanism by which control over long-distance exchange cements elite status, relevant to the individual signaling layer of the model.

### Claim 7

**Statement**: Much of the "normal surplus" occasionally deployed as a buffer against scarcity was usually committed to the production and consumption of non-subsistence commodities. This created an incentive for continued overproduction, even when buffering seemed unnecessary, but also obscured the risk-buffering role of the redistributive agency or market.
**Page**: p. 125
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: Bronze Age redistributive economies (Halstead; Wright, Redding and Pollock); historic market economies (Forbes; Garnsey and Morris; Jongman and Dekker)
**Depends on**: Comparative analysis
**Notes**: This dynamic closely parallels the Chaco construction boom: surplus labor directed to monument construction during favorable periods may have served latent risk-buffering functions (signaling cooperative capacity, maintaining exchange networks) while appearing to serve primarily ritual or political purposes.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: Directly relevant to the model's mechanism. Signaling investment (C=0.35 productivity diversion) represents surplus committed to non-subsistence activity that simultaneously maintains the cooperation networks providing crisis buffering. The "obscured" risk-buffering function explains why monument construction is debated as ritual vs. economic activity.

### Claim 8

**Statement**: Buffering mechanisms adopted as solutions to short-term or occasional instability have often instigated radical and sustained change. Once firmly established, these institutions may assume a dynamism of their own.
**Page**: p. 125
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: Emergence of "free trade" ideal from corn supply markets (Jongman and Dekker)
**Depends on**: Historical case studies
**Notes**: Describes institutional path dependence in risk-buffering systems. At Chaco, great house construction that began as a cooperative risk-buffering strategy may have developed its own institutional momentum, persisting beyond the conditions that initially favored it.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: Captures the positive feedback dynamics of the signaling model: once monument construction becomes institutionalized, it generates its own logic (status competition, labor coordination, pilgrimage attraction) that perpetuates the system even when environmental conditions change.

### Claim 9

**Statement**: Variability occurs on a series of more or less discrete scales and buffering takes the form of various scale-specific transformations of four basic types of response. Because of this scalar correspondence between variability and effective response, this framework provides a simple but useful tool for identifying and investigating buffering behaviour.
**Page**: p. 125
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: Volume synthesis
**Depends on**: Cross-case comparison
**Notes**: The scale-matching principle (buffering must match the scale of variability) is fundamental to the model's multi-scale design: household storage buffers seasonal shortfalls, community exchange buffers interannual variability, and regional networks buffer multi-year droughts.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: Provides theoretical justification for the model's three-layer structure. Each layer addresses a different scale of environmental variability, and the signaling mechanisms at each scale must match the temporal and spatial structure of the risk being buffered.

---

### Claim 10

**Statement**: The effects of environmental perturbation are not homogeneous over space. Regional buffering networks, linking often very different cultures and economies in supra-cultural buffering systems, represent the middle ground between individual societies and world systems.
**Page**: p. 126
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: Multiple volume contributions
**Depends on**: Cross-case comparison
**Notes**: Directly supports the Chaco outlier network as a supra-community buffering system. The spatial heterogeneity of environmental perturbation is what makes inter-community exchange networks valuable for crisis buffering.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: The concept of "supra-cultural buffering systems" maps directly onto the Chaco regional system connecting canyon great houses with outlier communities across environmentally diverse zones of the San Juan Basin.
