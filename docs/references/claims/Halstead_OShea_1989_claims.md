## Claims

### Claim 1

**Statement**: The basic structure of variability, including its frequency, duration, spatial scale, severity, and regularity, can often be predicted quite accurately for any given situation. It follows that the basic structure of an effective coping mechanism in the same context can also be specified.
**Page**: p. 1
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: General argument
**Depends on**: None (foundational premise)
**Notes**: This claim underpins the entire analytical framework: if variability structure is knowable, then the required buffering response can be predicted. For Chaco, the known structure of PDSI variability (temporal, spatial, intensity) should predict the form and scale of buffering strategies.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (foundational premise, widely adopted)
**Assessment**: Directly supports the model's approach of parameterizing environmental uncertainty (sigma) and deriving predicted buffering behavior. The claim that buffering structure can be "independently specified" from variability structure is precisely what the formal model attempts.

---

### Claim 2

**Statement**: Any source of variability exhibits three diagnostic characteristics: temporal structure (timescale and duration), spatial structure (size and homogeneity of affected area), and relative intensity (severity and variation in severity). These characteristics determine the scope and severity of the problem with which a society must contend.
**Page**: p. 3
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: General framework
**Depends on**: None (definitional)
**Notes**: This three-dimensional characterization of variability (temporal, spatial, intensity) maps onto the model's parameterization of environmental uncertainty. Sigma in the model primarily captures intensity, but temporal autocorrelation and spatial correlation also enter through the cooperation network value function lambda(sigma).

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: Provides the conceptual foundation for the model's environmental parameterization. The three dimensions should all be captured: sigma (intensity), autocorrelation (temporal structure), and spatial correlation (spatial structure affecting network buffering value).

---

### Claim 3

**Statement**: Four basic categories of buffering mechanism exist: mobility, diversification, physical storage, and exchange. All four exploit favourable aspects of the temporal and spatial structure of variability to mitigate the risk of scarcity. Mobility and diversification use local abundance to counter local scarcity, while storage balances seasons of plenty against lean seasons. Exchange secures a stable food supply by playing off temporal variability against spatial variability.
**Page**: pp. 3-4
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: General framework
**Depends on**: None (definitional)
**Notes**: The four-mechanism taxonomy. Exchange is the most relevant to the signaling model: it requires spatial heterogeneity in variability (different areas affected at different times) and social infrastructure to facilitate transactions, which is what the signaling system provides.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (canonical framework)
**Assessment**: Exchange as buffering requires exactly the conditions the signaling model addresses: (1) spatial heterogeneity in drought (documented by Gauthier 2021), (2) social infrastructure for inter-community transactions (provided by the great house system and outlier network), and (3) trust mechanisms to ensure reciprocity (provided by costly signaling of cooperative intent).

### Claim 4

**Statement**: Two categories of response may be mutually incompatible: storage seriously prejudices mobility and vice versa. Most societies deploy an array of different strategies in a hierarchy of responses equated with both the scale of the producing and consuming units and with the magnitude of the resource failure encountered.
**Page**: p. 4
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: General framework; Bushmen hxaro example
**Depends on**: None (general observation)
**Notes**: The incompatibility between storage and mobility is critical for understanding Chaco. Great house construction represents massive investment in immovable infrastructure, committing communities to a storage-and-exchange strategy and foreclosing mobility as a primary response.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: The incompatibility principle explains why Chaco communities were locked into signaling-based strategies once they invested in great house construction. The sunk cost of monuments created path dependence, making exchange (Layer 3) increasingly important and mobility increasingly difficult as an alternative.

---

### Claim 5

**Statement**: Societies face a trade-off between security, as reflected in the ability to withstand increasingly severe and rare fluctuations, and efficiency in terms of normal subsistence activities and of avoiding investment in precautions that will only rarely be used.
**Page**: p. 4
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: General framework
**Depends on**: None
**Notes**: This security-efficiency trade-off is formalized in the model as the cost parameter C: diverting 35% of productivity to signaling (construction) increases crisis-buffering capacity but reduces normal productivity.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (foundational concept)
**Assessment**: The C=0.35 parameter represents Chaco's resolution of this trade-off. The model predicts that this trade-off shifts as environmental uncertainty (sigma) changes: higher sigma favors greater investment in security (higher C), while lower sigma favors efficiency (lower C).

### Claim 6

**Statement**: High-level coping mechanisms are more powerful in terms of the scale of shortage which they can buffer, but by virtue of their size and relative rarity with which they are activated, they can be both costly in the energy invested in their maintenance, and unreliable, in that they may depend on distant social relations, may entail reversal of cultural norms, and may fail through long periods of disuse.
**Page**: p. 4
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: General framework
**Depends on**: None
**Notes**: Describes the maintenance problem for high-level buffering mechanisms. The signaling model addresses this by embedding the maintenance of cooperation networks within regular signaling activities (ongoing construction, periodic ceremonies, exchange of exotic goods), which keep the network active during non-crisis periods.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: The signaling interpretation of great house construction resolves the maintenance problem identified here: construction is not an "emergency" mechanism activated only during crises but a continuous signaling activity that maintains the cooperation network. This is precisely Halstead and O'Shea's prescription that communities should "embed" emergency mechanisms as regular cultural practices.

### Claim 7

**Statement**: There is strong selective pressure for communities to embed "emergency" buffering mechanisms as regular aspects of the cultural repertoire. For example, surpluses may be earmarked for feasting at a particular annual ceremony, thus ensuring their continued production through a long run of good years. However, this embedding may undermine the latent function if the surplus cannot be redirected in time of need, and high-level mechanisms may be embedded to the point where they are irreversibly transformed.
**Page**: p. 4
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: Garnsey and Morris (this volume); Jongman and Dekker (this volume)
**Depends on**: Comparative analysis
**Notes**: The embedding argument is central to interpreting Chaco ritual and construction activity. Regular ceremonies and construction episodes "embed" the cooperation network maintenance within normal cultural practice, but may become so ritualized that surplus cannot be redirected during actual crises.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: This captures a critical dynamic in the Chaco model. Great house construction and ceremonial activity embed network maintenance in regular practice, but the institutional momentum of these activities may prevent flexible reallocation of resources during the megadrought of the 1130s-1150s, contributing to system collapse rather than adaptive response.

---

### Claim 8

**Statement**: One likely outcome of the embedding process is the radical transformation of society, as perhaps in the appropriation of risk-related surplus for the maintenance of an elite. Associated changes in other aspects of social or economic behaviour are likely to unleash new sources of risk, leading either to the development of new coping mechanisms and potentially another cycle of cultural change, or to the undermining of existing coping mechanisms, resulting in catastrophic rather than transformational change.
**Page**: p. 6
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: General argument
**Depends on**: Volume case studies
**Notes**: Describes two alternative trajectories: transformational (new mechanisms emerge) or catastrophic (existing mechanisms fail). Chaco experienced the catastrophic trajectory: the megadrought undermined the signaling-based buffering system, and no replacement mechanism emerged within the canyon.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: The transformational vs. catastrophic distinction maps onto the model's prediction that collapse occurs when sustained environmental stress exceeds the buffering capacity of the signaling system and the cost of signaling exceeds its benefits (C > B(lambda) at extreme sigma).

### Claim 9

**Statement**: Environmental variability poses a set of problems that must be addressed by every society, regardless of time, place, social organisation, or cognised view of the universe. It is the universality of the problems posed by environmental variation and the relatively limited ways in which human groups can respond to such unpredictability that gives this approach its analytical power.
**Page**: p. 126 [from conclusion chapter, but the principle is stated in the introduction as well, p. 6]
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: Volume synthesis
**Depends on**: Cross-cultural comparison
**Notes**: The universality claim justifies cross-cultural comparison of risk-buffering strategies. The same analytical framework applies to Chaco Canyon, the Maya lowlands (Neiman 1997), and other cases where environmental uncertainty drives cultural responses.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: Supports the comparative dimension of the project, where Chaco, Maya, and other cases are analyzed within the same signaling/buffering framework.

### Claim 10

**Statement**: The structure of variability can often be established even in cases where the average is unknown. The relative structure of variability in ancient crop yields from a particular area may be established by extrapolation from modern data without estimating any absolute mean value. The structure of variability may be more significant than average conditions for understanding human society.
**Page**: p. 6
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: General observation
**Depends on**: None
**Notes**: This methodological principle justifies the model's focus on sigma (variability structure) rather than mean productivity. Tree-ring-based PDSI reconstructions provide the relative structure of climate variability even without precise estimates of absolute agricultural yields.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: Provides methodological justification for parameterizing the model using variance structure (sigma, autocorrelation) from PDSI data rather than attempting to estimate absolute yields.

### Claim 11

**Statement**: Maize yields from southeastern Iowa (1925-1945) are characterized by relatively high temporal variability (considerable year-to-year variation) and at the same time by a very low level of spatial variability (little variation from place to place). Given such a pattern, the most likely buffering mechanisms would be local storage and diversification, while the lack of spatial variability would seem to rule out exchange.
**Page**: p. 5 (with Figure 1.1)
**Type**: empirical/methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: USDA county-level maize yield data, 1925-1945
**Depends on**: Agricultural statistics
**Notes**: Provides a worked example of how variability structure predicts buffering strategy. The inverse case applies to the Southwest, where higher spatial variability (different drought patterns in different areas, per Gauthier 2021) makes exchange a viable and valuable buffering strategy.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (empirical illustration)
**Assessment**: The contrast with the Southwest is instructive. Chaco Canyon is located at the intersection of multiple climate regimes (oceanic vs. continental moisture sources), creating the spatial variability that makes exchange-based buffering valuable, unlike the spatially homogeneous Iowa case.

### Claim 12

**Statement**: When native Americans cultivated maize in the same Iowa region using small, optimally located, and widely separated fields, the factors producing variation in yield were dramatically different. Smaller-scale effects such as soil type, micro-climate, insects, fire, and hail increased in importance, spatial variability was probably greater, and exchange may have been a useful option.
**Page**: p. 5
**Type**: theoretical/methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Comparison of modern vs. prehistoric agriculture
**Depends on**: Agricultural analogy
**Notes**: Demonstrates that the effective variability structure depends on the agricultural system, not just climate. At Chaco, dispersed floodwater farming (Wills 2017) would have experienced higher spatial variability than modern irrigated farming, making exchange more valuable.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (analogy, not direct evidence)
**Assessment**: Reinforces Wills 2017's argument that macro-scale climate reconstructions understate the actual micro-scale variability experienced by Chaco farmers using dispersed field systems.
