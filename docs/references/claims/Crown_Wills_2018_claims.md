## Claims

### Claim 1

**Statement**: Pueblo Bonito's inhabitants "settled into a never-ending project of construction, remodelling, renewal and destruction." The authors propose that the site confounds interpretation because it was never considered finished by its builders; the history of the great house "may never be fully understood because its builders never considered it to be finished."
**Page**: p. 890
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Crown & Wills 2003 (earlier evidence for frequent renewal); 14 years of new excavation and analysis by the authors
**Depends on**: Stratigraphic, architectural, and dating evidence presented throughout the article
**Notes**: This is the paper's central thesis, framed explicitly in the abstract and introduction.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (supported by multiple lines of evidence presented in the paper)
**Assessment**: Directly supports the signal depreciation prediction (delta = 0.10 in the model). If monuments require continuous reinvestment to maintain their signal value, the archaeological record should show exactly what Crown & Wills document: perpetual remodeling rather than a single construction-and-use sequence. The "never-ending project" characterization matches the model's prediction that signal maintenance is ongoing, not a one-time cost.

### Claim 2

**Statement**: Pueblo Bonito encompasses approximately 600 masonry rooms up to four storeys in height, as well as around 37 semi-subterranean masonry-lined structures (kivas). It is "amongst the largest and most completely excavated archaeological sites in the U.S. Southwest."
**Page**: p. 892
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: General architectural description; Lekson 1984
**Depends on**: Excavation records from Hyde Exploring Expedition (1896-1899) and National Geographic Society (1921-1927)
**Notes**: These are standard figures for Pueblo Bonito's scale. The "approximately 600" room count and "around 37" kiva count represent the final configuration, not the initial construction.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (well-established archaeological data)
**Assessment**: The scale of the structure provides the baseline for estimating total construction cost (C parameter). The four-storey height and 600-room count establish Pueblo Bonito as the peak investment in the Chaco signaling system.

### Claim 3

**Statement**: Pueblo Bonito was built on a location occupied during the Basketmaker III Period (c. AD 450-750). Basketmaker III-Period ceramics are present in small amounts at Pueblo Bonito, and Judd (1964) encountered two pithouses under the West Court approximately 4 m below the current surface, which "appear to be Basketmaker III Period in date."
**Page**: pp. 892-893
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Judd 1964; Wills & Windes 1989; Wills et al. 2012; Windes 2014; radiocarbon-dated layers of mixed fluvial and cultural deposits
**Depends on**: Radiocarbon dating; stratigraphic analysis; ceramic chronology
**Notes**: The deeply buried Basketmaker III pithouses (4 m below surface) demonstrate that the site had a long occupation history before great house construction began. The authors note that flood deposits between the late eighth and ninth centuries buried earlier structures.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (stratigraphic and radiocarbon evidence)
**Assessment**: The pre-existing settlement indicates that Pueblo Bonito's location was chosen based on earlier occupation patterns, not selected de novo for signal visibility. This is relevant to the model because it suggests the signal location was constrained by prior settlement history rather than optimized for broadcast effectiveness (contrast with Dungan et al. 2018's visibility findings for outlier great houses).

### Claim 4

**Statement**: Tree-ring dates indicate a hiatus in building activity at Pueblo Bonito between c. AD 975 and 1040. "People may have abandoned part or all of the site during this interval." Existing rooms were "left unmaintained and then partly collapsed before new construction in the A.D. 1040s" (citing Windes & Ford 1996: 301). "Remodelling was such a continuous process at Pueblo Bonito that the absence of dates in this interval is striking."
**Page**: p. 899
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Windes & Ford 1996: 301; tree-ring dating evidence
**Depends on**: Dendrochronological analysis of construction timbers
**Notes**: The hiatus spans approximately 65 years and coincides with the transition between what is conventionally called the Early Bonito Phase and the Classic Bonito Phase. During this gap, rooms were left unmaintained and collapsed, demonstrating the consequences of ceasing signal maintenance.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (tree-ring dating provides annual resolution)
**Assessment**: This hiatus is critical evidence for signal depreciation. When construction/maintenance investment ceased for approximately 65 years, rooms collapsed. This demonstrates empirically that without continuous reinvestment, the monument degraded, precisely the dynamic captured by the depreciation rate delta. The subsequent resumption of construction in the AD 1040s required building on top of accumulated debris and collapsed structures, effectively increasing the cost of signal renewal.

### Claim 5

**Statement**: The eleventh century was "a time of intensified building activities and remodelling at Pueblo Bonito." Tree-ring dates suggest construction of at least 80 new rooms at the rear of the pueblo from AD 1040-1050, another 24 rooms between AD 1050 and 1070, and "perhaps another 160-170 rooms between AD 1070 and 1100" (citing Windes & Ford 1996: 302-303).
**Page**: p. 899
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Windes & Ford 1996: 302-303; tree-ring dates
**Depends on**: Dendrochronological analysis
**Notes**: The construction rate accelerated dramatically: 80 rooms in the first decade (1040-1050), 24 in the next two decades (1050-1070), and then 160-170 in the following three decades (1070-1100). This totals approximately 264-274 new rooms in 60 years, more than doubling the building's size.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (tree-ring dated construction events)
**Assessment**: The rapid escalation of construction investment after the hiatus aligns with two model predictions: (1) signal depreciation during the hiatus required major reinvestment to restore signal value; (2) the Classic Bonito Phase construction boom represents competitive signaling escalation, potentially driven by the emergence of rival great houses and the need to reassert dominance after the gap. The acceleration from 80 rooms/decade to 160-170 rooms in 30 years suggests increasing returns to signaling investment during favorable conditions.

### Claim 6

**Statement**: Remodeling activities in the northern area included "the replacement of Room 28's north wall, the foundation clay for which sits on the floor level of the earlier room. The wall exhibits Type II masonry, a style believed to characterise the time between AD 1020 and 1060." New upper-storey Room 28b was constructed c. AD 1071 with Type IV masonry and was fitted with "two side-by-side T-shaped doorways" that "may have been important for ceremonial processions into the West Court."
**Page**: pp. 899-900
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Lekson 1984: 19 (Type IV masonry dating); Crown 2018 (ceremonial function); Judd 1954: 27 (stairway from West Court)
**Depends on**: Masonry typology; dendrochronological dating; architectural analysis
**Notes**: The replacement of walls with newer masonry styles while earlier floors remained in use demonstrates layered renovation rather than demolition and rebuilding. A stairway connected the West Court down to the now-underground lower Room 28, allowing continued access to the older rooms.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (stratigraphic and architectural evidence with dendro dates)
**Assessment**: This detailed remodeling evidence demonstrates that signal renovation involved not just maintenance but functional transformation. Rooms shifted from habitation to storage to ceremonial use over time, with new architectural features (T-shaped doorways, stairways) added to enhance ritual function. The continuous layering of new construction over old, rather than demolition and replacement, made the building an archaeological palimpsest, with each renovation adding to rather than replacing the signal.

### Claim 7

**Statement**: Around AD 1100, the north-west door between Rooms 28 and 32 was sealed with masonry. Within Room 28, "173 ceramic vessels, including 112 cylinder jars, were placed on shelving, in groups blocking the doors, and on the upper room's floor. This constitutes the largest group of whole vessels recovered from any single room in the region." A fire was set that "partially vitrified the sand floor and burned until the upper room collapsed and smothered the fire. Similar fires destroyed the adjacent rooms along the northern arc of the West Court."
**Page**: p. 901
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Mills 2008: 103; Mills & Ferguson 2008; Crown 2018 (termination rituals)
**Depends on**: Archaeological excavation records (Pepper 1920; Judd 1964)
**Notes**: The fires are interpreted as "probably part of termination or retirement rituals, which closed the rooms from further use and removed the power from objects accorded animacy as spiritual beings." The 112 cylinder jars are the same vessels associated with cacao consumption (Crown & Hurst 2009; Crown 2012). This event sealed a substantial investment in both architecture and exotic goods.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (well-documented excavation context with large artifact assemblage)
**Assessment**: The termination ritual represents deliberate destruction of signal value, both architectural (sealing and burning rooms) and material (entombing 173 ceramic vessels). This is relevant to the model in two ways: (1) it demonstrates that signal depreciation could be intentional as well as natural, suggesting that delta captures both physical decay and ritual retirement; (2) the destruction of 112 cylinder jars (associated with cacao consumption, itself a costly exotic good) shows that signal termination involved sacrificing accumulated signaling investments, consistent with the theoretical expectation that signal retirement requires costly demonstrations of the ability to absorb loss.

### Claim 8

**Statement**: Between c. AD 850 and 925, the inhabitants of Pueblo Bonito constructed masonry rooms using "diverse construction methods," including "large sandstone blocks, with little evidence of mortar; smaller, tabular pieces of sandstone laid closely without defined rows; jacal (wattle-and-daub); and stone courses widely spaced by large quantities of mud." The "diverse wall-construction techniques indicate a lack of a unified architectural style in this early period," which "may suggest construction by individual households using their own building methods."
**Page**: pp. 897-898
**Type**: empirical/interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Pepper 1920 (Room 28a description); Judd 1964; masonry style variation in early rooms
**Depends on**: Architectural analysis of wall construction techniques
**Notes**: Exhausted grinding stones incorporated into masonry walls "support the idea of an extensive local settlement prior to the construction of these rooms" (p. 897). The lack of unified style contrasts with later, standardized masonry types (Types I-IV from Lekson 1984).

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (architectural observation, interpretive inference about household-level construction)
**Assessment**: The early household-level construction with diverse methods suggests that initial monument building was decentralized, with individual households contributing labor using their own techniques. This is relevant to the signaling model's initialization: the earliest phase of monument construction may have been a collective action problem solved by individual contributions rather than a centrally organized project. The shift toward standardized masonry types in later phases would then represent the emergence of coordinated signaling investment.

### Claim 9

**Statement**: "By the mid tenth century, this northern portion of Pueblo Bonito had approximately 25 ground-floor rooms. Burials in Rooms 33 and 56 show that these rooms were eventually used as burial crypts." Initially, rooms "appear to have served as domestic spaces, including as use for habitation, cooking and storage." The authors' excavations in Room 28 "revealed several shallow, amorphous pits dug into the sand floor that served as thermal features."
**Page**: p. 899
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Excavation data from Room 28; burial records from Rooms 33 and 56
**Depends on**: Stratigraphic analysis; archaeological features
**Notes**: The functional transformation from habitation/cooking/storage to burial crypts documents a shift in how rooms were used over the building's life. Formal hearths are described as "relatively rare in Pueblo Bonito rooms" (citing Windes 1984; Bernardini 1999), with informal thermal features potentially confounding population estimates based on hearth counts.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (direct excavation evidence)
**Assessment**: The functional shift from domestic to ritual/mortuary use within the same architectural space documents signal transformation over time. Rooms that began as habitation spaces became burial crypts for high-status individuals (Room 33 contained the richest burials in the canyon). This transformation is relevant to the model because it shows that the signal value of the structure was not static: rooms acquired new ritual significance through use history, meaning signal depreciation (delta) must account for both physical decay and the potential for functional revaluation.

### Claim 10

**Statement**: When construction resumed in the AD 1000s, the surface of the West Court "had risen so much that it was approximately level with the roofs of the first-storey rooms." Judd (1964: 60) attributed this to wind-blown sand, but "sand also piled up against the rear rooms, and new rooms were built on the accumulated sand" (citing Lekson 1984: 133). Stein et al. (2003: 50) argued that "occupants of Pueblo Bonito raised the surface level artificially."
**Page**: p. 899
**Type**: empirical/interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Judd 1964: 60; Lekson 1984: 133; Stein et al. 2003: 50
**Depends on**: Stratigraphic and geological analysis of deposits
**Notes**: Whether the surface rise was natural (wind-blown sand, flood deposits) or artificial (intentional filling), the effect was the same: new construction had to build on top of elevated surfaces, burying earlier rooms and effectively increasing construction costs. The debate about intentionality is unresolved.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (the surface rise is well-documented; its cause is debated)
**Assessment**: The surface accumulation between construction phases created a compounding cost effect: each renovation cycle required more labor because builders had to work on top of accumulated deposits. Whether natural or artificial, this rising surface illustrates a mechanism by which signal maintenance costs increase over time, consistent with the model's prediction that depreciation and renewal create escalating investment demands.

### Claim 11

**Statement**: The earliest rooms at Pueblo Bonito may have included "tub rooms," an architectural form "which may be restricted to the San Juan Basin" (citing Windes 1993). These had "square to rounded masonry walls with floors that included a central, bathtub-shaped depression 200-400mm deep, surrounded by shelves 150-250mm wide." Pueblo Bonito rooms believed to date to the late AD 800s or early 900s have possible tub rooms underlying them.
**Page**: p. 896
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Windes 1993; 2003; Pepper 1920: 126-28
**Depends on**: Architectural analysis of sub-floor features
**Notes**: Two sub-floor features in Room 28a were found at depths of 1.83 m and 2.59 m below the ceiling, with shelf projections visible on the lower floor, "supporting the idea that this was a tub room." The earliest rooms "must date to the AD 800s, and probably the early 800s" based on ceramics from adjacent surfaces.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (sub-floor features partially excavated; interpretation based on architectural form comparison)
**Assessment**: The tub room evidence suggests the earliest settlement may have been larger and more architecturally developed than previously assumed. If initial construction dates to the early AD 800s, the total duration of the monument construction project exceeds 300 years, extending the timeframe over which signal depreciation and renewal cycles operated.

### Claim 12

**Statement**: After the termination fires (c. AD 1100), "new plastered floors were placed over the debris, and a new partition wall was placed between Rooms 28 and 55." The presence of ceramics in the lower northern rooms "suggest continued occupation of Pueblo Bonito into the AD 1200s, perhaps by migrants from the Mesa Verde area to the north." Fewer construction events "indicate a focus on renovating ritual structures and the late construction of semi-subterranean houses scattered along the edge of the plazas" (citing Windes & Ford 1996: 303).
**Page**: p. 901
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Ceramic evidence; Windes & Ford 1996: 303; Wills 2009
**Depends on**: Ceramic typology; architectural stratigraphy
**Notes**: Post-1100 occupation included reuse of lower rooms (possibly as a turkey pen), suggesting a significant decline in the status and function of the structure. The shift from monumental construction to renovation of ritual structures and construction of small semi-subterranean houses represents a fundamental change in investment patterns.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (ceramic dating of late occupation; post-abandonment reuse evidence is less well-documented)
**Assessment**: The post-florescence occupation pattern documents signal degradation in real time. The shift from monumental construction to renovation of ritual structures and small houses represents a collapse of the signaling system: the monument was no longer receiving the investment needed to maintain its signal value. The possible reuse of Room 28b as a turkey pen represents the terminal stage of signal depreciation, where a formerly high-status space is repurposed for subsistence activities. This matches the model's prediction that signal value approaches zero when reinvestment ceases.

### Claim 13

**Statement**: "The poorly documented and, in some cases, unethical excavations at Pueblo Bonito compound issues associated with interpreting any complex construction and use history." One archaeologist in 1897 "hired nine men and a wagon, and went to Chaco Canyon" to make "a 'typical collection in three weeks'" (citing Moorehead 1906: 33). "This group tore down the upper-storey walls of four rooms to reach the lower rooms with intact roofs. They plundered burials, scattering the human skeletal remains across three rooms" (citing Marden 2011: 205). "It has taken over a century to document the extent of this one looting episode."
**Page**: p. 901
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Moorehead 1906: 33; Marden 2011: 205
**Depends on**: Archival records and excavation documentation
**Notes**: This excavation history is important context for all archaeological interpretations of Pueblo Bonito. The authors argue that "it may be impossible to overcome completely the interpretive limitations resulting from poorly executed and documented past excavations."

**Evidence strength**: Strong (well-documented archival evidence)
**Assessment**: While not directly relevant to the signaling model's parameters, this caveat is methodologically important. The interpretive challenges caused by early uncontrolled excavations mean that some aspects of Pueblo Bonito's construction sequence, room function, and material assemblages are permanently unrecoverable. Any model calibrated to Pueblo Bonito data must acknowledge these limitations.

### Claim 14

**Statement**: Construction at Pueblo Bonito "was one of near-constant architectural change, interrupted only once by a probable temporary abandonment in the late AD 900s." The authors conclude that "Pueblo Bonito may always have been experienced as a work in progress. We suspect this dynamism reflects the volatility of rapid socio-political transformation in early farming societies."
**Page**: p. 901
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Synthesis of all evidence presented in the paper
**Depends on**: Full stratigraphic, architectural, and chronological evidence
**Notes**: This conclusion reinforces the central thesis. The "work in progress" characterization emphasizes that the great house was never in a stable, completed state. The only interruption was the late AD 900s hiatus (Claim 4), during which rooms collapsed from lack of maintenance.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (well-supported by the cumulative evidence)
**Assessment**: The "near-constant architectural change" conclusion directly validates the model's signal depreciation and reinvestment dynamics. A monument that was always "a work in progress" is exactly what the model predicts when delta > 0: continuous reinvestment is required to maintain signal value, and the signal is never "finished" because depreciation is ongoing. The link to "volatility of rapid socio-political transformation" suggests the authors view the construction dynamics as reflecting social and political processes rather than purely functional needs, aligning with the signaling interpretation.

## Summary Assessment

Crown & Wills 2018 provides the most detailed account of Pueblo Bonito's construction sequence as a continuous, dynamic process of building, remodeling, destruction, and renewal. The paper's central finding, that the great house was "a never-ending project" interrupted only once by a ~65-year hiatus during which rooms collapsed, directly supports the signal depreciation prediction (delta = 0.10) in the costly signaling model. The evidence shows: (1) continuous renovation from the AD 800s through the early AD 1100s, with only one hiatus; (2) during the hiatus, rooms degraded and collapsed, demonstrating the physical reality of signal depreciation; (3) the post-hiatus construction boom (264-274 new rooms in 60 years) represents the cost of signal renewal after a period of neglect; (4) deliberate termination rituals (c. AD 1100) including the sealing and burning of rooms with 173 ceramic vessels demonstrate intentional signal retirement; (5) post-florescence reuse of formerly high-status spaces for mundane purposes (turkey pen) illustrates terminal signal depreciation. The paper also documents the transition from decentralized, household-level construction in the early phases to standardized, coordinated building in the Classic Bonito Phase, suggesting a parallel transition from individual to collective signaling investment.
