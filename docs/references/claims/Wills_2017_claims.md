## Claims

### Claim 1

**Statement**: "Agricultural intensification is widely assumed by archaeologists to have been a catalytic factor in the development of social complexity in Chaco Canyon, New Mexico, between ca. AD 850 and 1200. In this perspective, water control technology resulted in surplus food production which was controlled by elites to finance the construction of massive buildings ('great houses'). However, recent fieldwork and archival research has been unable to substantiate this view of technological innovation promoting surplus agricultural production."

**Page**: p. 369

**Type**: interpretive

**Evidence cited by author**: Recent UNM fieldwork; archival records

**Depends on**: Assessment of the full archaeological record of water control features

**Notes**: This is the central argument of the paper. Wills directly challenges the consensus that agricultural intensification through water control drove great house construction.

**Evidence strength**: Strong

**Assessment**: Well-supported by the detailed review that follows. The paper methodically examines each category of water control feature and finds the evidence far weaker than commonly assumed.

---

### Claim 2

**Statement**: "There is no known archaeological evidence for earlier water control and thus arguments for agricultural intensification during the Bonito Phase infer a shift from nonintensive farming methods to capital investment in hardened production features such as dams, headgates, canals, and formal fields."

**Page**: p. 374

**Type**: empirical

**Evidence cited by author**: Archaeological record; Sebastian 1992

**Depends on**: Completeness of the archaeological survey record

**Notes**: This establishes that water control features appear to be an innovation of the Bonito Phase, not inherited from earlier periods.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate

**Assessment**: The absence of evidence is not conclusive proof, but Wills notes the extensive survey history of the canyon.

---

### Claim 3

**Statement**: Estimated field areas represented "a tiny (ca. 2 percent) portion of potential arable lands in the canyon" and "experimental field studies in which maize was cultivated with runoff directed through excavated prehistoric water control features into likely field areas did not show obvious improvements in productivity over less formal cultivation methods."

**Page**: p. 371

**Type**: empirical

**Evidence cited by author**: Judge 1989:228; Lagasse et al. 1984; Loose and Lyons 1976; Schelberg 1982; Toll et al. 1985:104-105

**Depends on**: National Park Service Chaco Project studies (1969-1985)

**Notes**: Judge, while Director of the NPS Chaco Project, concluded that water control technology was "neither as widespread nor productive as many researchers had suggested" and that "there is no compelling reason to assume a vast amount of labor involvement in their construction and maintenance."

**Evidence strength**: Strong

**Assessment**: Based on the director of the most comprehensive field study at Chaco. The experimental cultivation results are particularly important because they directly tested the productivity hypothesis.

---

### Claim 4

**Statement**: The evidence for canals fitting Vivian's definition is "ambiguous in the canyon except around Pueblo Bonito." Most previously identified "canals" have been "reinterpreted as surface depressions marking prehistoric or historic roads" and unpublished Park Service surveys "occasionally were unable to relocate reported canals, or reinterpreted mapped canals as roads."

**Page**: pp. 379, 382

**Type**: empirical

**Evidence cited by author**: Vivian 1970:72; Lyons et al. 1976; Roney 1992; UNM fieldwork

**Depends on**: Re-examination of field records and survey data

**Notes**: The diversion channel at Pueblo Bonito, while clearly artificial, shows sedimentology indicating "flashy (brief, high energy) flows rather than slow-moving water typical of irrigation canals." No headgates or agricultural plots are associated with it.

**Evidence strength**: Strong

**Assessment**: The systematic reinterpretation of previously identified canals substantially weakens the case for widespread irrigation infrastructure.

---

### Claim 5

**Statement**: "The Chetro Ketl complex is the only location in Chaco Canyon where an array of grids has been documented and it apparently post-dates most or all of the Bonito Phase." The only confirmed gridded field system postdates the period of great house florescence, with the field area dating after ca. AD 1100 based on its position above the Bonito paleo-channel fill.

**Page**: pp. 382-385

**Type**: empirical

**Evidence cited by author**: Force et al. 2002:24; Loose and Lyons 1976; Vivian 1992:51; Potter 1974; Sturm 2016

**Depends on**: Force et al. 2002 stratigraphic dating; GPR studies by UNM

**Notes**: The soils in the Chetro Ketl field area are sodium-rich clay "considered poor for maize production." Loose and Lyons (1976:146) estimated only about 2% (30 ha) of the canyon was cultivated. Multiple alternative interpretations exist for the grid features, including clay mixing areas for construction or communal ritual/performance area.

**Evidence strength**: Strong

**Assessment**: This is a critical finding. If the only confirmed gridded field system postdates most of the Bonito Phase, it cannot have been the agricultural foundation for great house construction during the Classic Bonito period.

---

### Claim 6

**Statement**: "The construction costs of the 18 identified headgates and any associated intake ditches were inconsequential compared to the enormous amount of material, sophisticated building methods, and communal organization of production sustained over decades to build great houses." The entire corpus of headgate material in Chaco would be "less material than required to construct a single large room in Pueblo Bonito."

**Page**: p. 393

**Type**: empirical

**Evidence cited by author**: Rough estimate of stones at Rincon 4 complex (200-250 sandstone blocks, 30-40 cm long); Judge 1989; Lekson 1984; Neitzel 2003; Wills 2000

**Depends on**: Material inventories from excavated headgate complexes

**Notes**: Headgates were "usually well made but cannot be considered energetically costly." All documented headgates appear to have been built late in the Classic Bonito Phase (after ca. AD 1075) based on geological context and associated ceramics.

**Evidence strength**: Strong

**Assessment**: The contrast between the scale of great house construction and the modest investment in water control features directly undermines the argument that water management was the economic foundation for monumental architecture.

---

### Claim 7

**Statement**: "Arguments that water control features increased agricultural production have yet to be confirmed, while the plausible alternative that technological devices might have been built in an effort to sustain productivity (perhaps unsuccessfully) has not even been considered."

**Page**: p. 393

**Type**: interpretive

**Evidence cited by author**: Summary assessment based on the paper's full review

**Depends on**: The preceding analysis of each category of water control feature

**Notes**: This reverses the usual causal direction: rather than water control enabling surplus production that funded great houses, water control may have been a late, reactive response to declining conditions.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate

**Assessment**: This is a plausible alternative interpretation. The late dating of headgate construction (post-AD 1075) and the Chetro Ketl field system (post-AD 1100) is consistent with reactive rather than proactive investment.

---

### Claim 8

**Statement**: Agricultural productivity in the Chaco Core "was probably sufficient to support most projected population sizes at least at subsistence levels." If one assumes 8-10 km farming catchments around great houses, "all of Chaco Canyon becomes one continuous agricultural zone with a watershed exceeding 11,000 km2."

**Page**: p. 398

**Type**: empirical/interpretive

**Evidence cited by author**: Dorshow 2012; Mathien 2005; Wills and Dorshow 2012; Doyel et al. 1984; Schelberg 1984; Toll 2006; Wills et al. 2014; Windes 2014

**Depends on**: Dorshow 2012 GIS productivity model; ethnographic walking distance data

**Notes**: Hungo Pavi's watershed alone (1,190 hectares) could support between 175 and 250 people. The argument is that dispersed extensive farming rather than intensive irrigated farming could have supported the Chaco population.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate

**Assessment**: The specific productivity estimates depend on the Dorshow 2012 model assumptions. The 8-10 km farming radius is based on ethnographic analogy (Arnold 1985; Doolittle 1990) and is plausible but not directly verified for Chaco.

### Claim 9

**Statement**: Stone and Downum (1999) proposed that great house communities drew in new members "through the granting of access to agricultural areas and protection of those rights," and the concentration of population around great houses "was a self-reinforcing sociopolitical process wherein population aggregates formed through recruitment of new members, which in turn attracted more immigrants."

**Page**: p. 394

**Type**: interpretive (citing Stone and Downum 1999)

**Evidence cited by author**: Stone and Downum 1999; ethnographic Pueblo parallels

**Depends on**: Stone and Downum 1999 model; archaeological settlement pattern data

**Notes**: Wills endorses this model as superior to surplus-extraction models because "the development of great house corporate groups can be understood without coerced or expropriated household surpluses." This recruitment-based model requires labor surpluses (from recruited members) rather than food surpluses.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate

**Assessment**: This is a verbal model that fits the data but is not formally tested. It is directly relevant to the costly signaling framework: great houses as signals could function by attracting labor/members rather than extracting surplus.

---

### Claim 10

**Statement**: "Financing great house construction by extracting labor surpluses through recruitment to corporately owned territories is a more efficient and less costly explanation than extracting both food and labor surpluses from households occupying a difficult farming environment."

**Page**: p. 399

**Type**: interpretive

**Evidence cited by author**: Greif 2006; North 1990; Kantner and Vaughn 2012; Van Dyke 2007

**Depends on**: The preceding arguments about labor vs. food surplus models

**Notes**: Wills argues that labor recruitment removes the "circular argument" of surplus-extraction models where "surpluses are not observed but rather inferred from energy investments, which are explained by surpluses."

**Evidence strength**: Moderate

**Assessment**: The logical critique of circular reasoning in surplus models is strong. The alternative (labor recruitment) is plausible but also lacks direct empirical confirmation.

### Claim 11

**Statement**: "90 percent or more of great house architecture by mass (or size) in the San Juan Basin occurs in Chaco Canyon proper and/or within 20 km of Pueblo Bonito, unequivocally a materialization of massive amounts of labor consumption, if not population packing."

**Page**: p. 395

**Type**: empirical

**Evidence cited by author**: Cordell 1982; Mills 2002; Van Dyke 2007; Wills 2000; Windes et al. 2000

**Depends on**: Archaeological site data from New Mexico ARMS database

**Notes**: This spatial concentration is shown in Figure 11, with relative site size expressed as standard deviations from the mean room count.

**Evidence strength**: Strong

**Assessment**: The spatial concentration of construction investment is a robust finding that constrains any model of the Chaco political economy. It indicates that the signaling investment was overwhelmingly concentrated in the canyon core.

### Claim 12

**Statement**: "It is unlikely to be an accident that all canyon floor great houses in Chaco are positioned in front of large pour-off points" where natural pools at the base of cliffs could provide "potentially tens of thousands of liters" of impounded water.

**Page**: p. 392

**Type**: empirical/interpretive

**Evidence cited by author**: Observation from field survey; canyon geomorphology

**Depends on**: Verification of great house positioning relative to pour-off points

**Notes**: This is significant because it suggests great house placement was related to water access for construction (mortar, plaster, mud floors) rather than irrigation agriculture. "Seasonally ponded water was essential to the massive construction work that took place at Pueblo Bonito in AD 1000s."

**Evidence strength**: Moderate

**Assessment**: The observation about great house positioning relative to pour-off points is interesting but not systematically quantified. The connection to construction water needs rather than agricultural water is a novel interpretation.

---

### Claim 13

**Statement**: "Chaco farming was based on mixtures of the risk-minimizing strategies documented ethnographically in the northern Southwest and that it would have taken a significant amount of social change to move production into a more risk-seeking mode." Pre-Bonito Phase agriculture was "almost certainly heavily reliant on runoff production" using dispersed floodwater farming methods.

**Page**: pp. 375, 377

**Type**: interpretive

**Evidence cited by author**: Bocinsky et al. 2016; Bryan 1929; ethnographic record of Pueblo farming; Sandor et al. 2002, 2007

**Depends on**: Ethnographic analogy; archaeological evidence for field locations

**Notes**: A single household could "prepare and cultivate about 2 ha of fields" including "arrays of rectangular plots irrigated with shallow, temporary ditches." Crop losses in floodwater systems are mitigated through dispersed plantings.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate

**Assessment**: The characterization of pre-Bonito farming is well-supported by ethnographic analogy and consistent with the archaeological evidence. The claim that intensification would require "significant social change" is interpretive.

---

### Claim 14

**Statement**: "Matrilocality is useful in recruiting men and their labor through marriage, both pulling them into new social networks and splitting them away from (at least in part) their natal communities." In the context of immigration, "matrilineal systems can create powerful positive reinforcing dynamics that accelerate recruitment."

**Page**: p. 397

**Type**: interpretive

**Evidence cited by author**: Adler 1994; Fox 1967; Steward 1937; Ware 2014; Kennett et al. 2017; Ware and Blinman 2002; Wills 2000

**Depends on**: Ethnographic evidence for Pueblo matrilineal/matrilocal kinship; Kennett et al. 2017 DNA evidence

**Notes**: References Kennett et al. 2017 for genetic evidence of at least one matriline in the Pueblo Bonito burial population. Ware and Blinman (2002) argue matrilocality creates opportunities for sodality formation which may provide labor for construction.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate

**Assessment**: The connection between matrilineal kinship and labor recruitment is theoretically sound and supported by ethnographic analogy, though the archaeological evidence for matrilocality at Chaco is indirect.

---

## Summary Assessment

Wills 2017 is a thorough, revisionist assessment of the water management evidence at Chaco Canyon. The paper systematically examines canals, gridded fields, headgates, ditches, terraces, reservoirs, check dams, and retention basins, finding the evidence for agricultural intensification through water control much weaker than conventionally assumed. The most consequential findings are: (1) the only confirmed gridded field system postdates the Bonito Phase florescence, (2) water control construction costs were trivial relative to great house construction, and (3) experimental cultivation through excavated water control features showed no obvious productivity improvement. Wills proposes an alternative political economy based on labor recruitment to corporate land-holding groups, rather than surplus extraction through agricultural intensification. This alternative is directly relevant to the costly signaling model because it reframes great houses as instruments for attracting labor (a signaling function) rather than products of extracted food surplus.
