## Claims

### Claim 1

**Statement**: "There was a pinyon juniper woodland (PJW) in Chaco Canyon before 600 BC and reveal a sharp decline in juniper pollen thereafter." Before 600 BC, juniper and pine pollen readings were at 20% and 12% respectively, "which is characteristic of a robust pinyon juniper woodland."
**Page**: p. 6 (Results and discussion, Pollen record)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Pollen data from Operations C-1/C-3 and C-2 (S4 Table in S1 File); Miller and Wigand 1994 [ref 34] for PJW pollen thresholds
**Depends on**: Original pollen analyses from 2013-2015 excavations
**Notes**: This establishes the baseline vegetation state of Chaco Canyon before significant human impact. The presence of a robust PJW is the starting condition against which deforestation is measured.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: Original empirical data from systematic pollen sampling. The 20%/12% juniper/pine figures are consistent with PJW identification criteria from the palynological literature.

---

### Claim 2

**Statement**: "By AD 400, the juniper pollen percent dropped to 8% and then 4% in AD 600. By AD 1100, the juniper pollen dropped to 2%, which likely reflects infrequent juniper trees clinging to the sidewalls of the canyon or possibly ambient pollen blown in from surrounding montane areas."
**Page**: p. 6
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: S5 Table in S1 File; Fig 6
**Depends on**: Original pollen analyses; OSL and radiocarbon dating of strata
**Notes**: This documents a progressive, centuries-long decline in the dominant woodland species. The decline begins well before the Bonito phase, indicating early and sustained human impact on the local environment. By the time of peak great house construction (AD 1075-1100), juniper was essentially gone from the canyon floor.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: Quantitative pollen data with chronological control. The decline trajectory (20% to 8% to 4% to 2%) is clear and unlikely to be an artifact of sampling.

---

### Claim 3

**Statement**: "The time horizon for the precipitous drop in juniper pollen (~500 BC) coincides with the Late Archaic occupation by Ancestral Puebloans, a time when maize (Zea mays) and squash (Cucurbita pepo) cultivation was becoming widespread."
**Page**: p. 8
**Type**: empirical/interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Hayes et al. 1981 [ref 38]; Simmons 1986 [ref 39]; Sebastian 1996 [ref 40]
**Depends on**: Pollen chronology; regional agricultural chronology
**Notes**: The coincidence of juniper decline with agricultural intensification suggests woodland clearing for fields and fuel. This is important for the Chaco model because it means that the resource base was already degraded well before the Bonito phase construction boom, increasing environmental vulnerability.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (the coincidence is documented; the causal connection is inferred)
**Assessment**: The temporal correlation is clear, but the causal attribution to agricultural clearing (vs. climate) is interpretive. The authors strengthen this interpretation in the "Human and climatic drivers of change" section.

### Claim 4

**Statement**: "The pollen record of greasewood, another fuel species favored by the Ancestral Puebloans, runs parallel to the juniper pollen trend. Greasewood pollen dropped down to 1% during the Bonito phase, and the pollen of willow, another useful woody species, completely disappeared during the same period."
**Page**: p. 7
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Toll 1987 [ref 37] for greasewood as fuel species
**Depends on**: Original pollen analyses
**Notes**: The decline of multiple woody species, not just juniper, indicates broad landscape degradation. This reinforces the argument that deforestation was anthropogenic rather than drought-driven (drought would not selectively remove all useful species while leaving non-useful ones).

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: The parallel decline of multiple fuel species strengthens the anthropogenic interpretation.

---

### Claim 5

**Statement**: "Fuel use in an agricultural society places the heaviest burden on wood reserves because fuel is needed daily for cooking otherwise inedible domesticated foods." Samuels and Betancourt (1982) calculated that 8.5 m^3 of fuelwood would have been required to supply a family of five for one year. With 2,100 to 2,700 inhabitants during the Bonito phase over 340 years, "we end up with a figure of over 1 million m^3 of fuelwood needed by the Chaco inhabitants during the Bonito phase."
**Page**: pp. 9, 13
**Type**: empirical (calculation from published estimates)
**Evidence cited by author**: Samuels and Betancourt 1982 [ref 12] for fuel requirements; Lekson 2006 [ref 2], Plog et al. 2017 [ref 83] for population estimates
**Depends on**: Fuelwood consumption rates; population estimates
**Notes**: Over 1 million m^3 of fuelwood over 340 years represents an enormous extraction demand on the local woodland. This is separate from and in addition to construction timber demands. The calculation demonstrates that even without construction, daily fuel needs alone would have severely stressed the PJW.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (individual parameters are well-sourced but the multiplicative calculation assumes constant population and consumption, which are simplifications)
**Assessment**: The calculation provides a useful order-of-magnitude estimate. The key insight is that daily fuel demand far exceeded construction timber demand as a driver of deforestation.

### Claim 6

**Statement**: "At Pueblo Alto, juniper and pinyon together comprised 32% of the overall fuel use. Of the two fuel species, however, juniper appears to have been the most often utilized. For example, at Pueblo Alto, 56% of the firepits and 73% of the warming pits contained juniper wood while 44% and 36% contained pinyon wood, respectively."
**Page**: p. 11
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Toll 1987 [ref 37]
**Depends on**: Macrobotanical analyses at Pueblo Alto
**Notes**: Juniper was preferentially used for fuel while pinyon was more often used for food (pine nuts) and construction. This differential exploitation pattern explains why juniper disappeared from the pollen record faster than pinyon. The 56%/73% vs. 44%/36% split shows clear preferential use of juniper as fuel.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (based on systematic archaeobotanical data from a well-studied great house)
**Assessment**: The data are from Toll's comprehensive analysis of Pueblo Alto plant remains. The differential use pattern is well-documented.

### Claim 7

**Statement**: "Local conifers (including both juniper and pinyon) made up 96% of the construction wood at Chaco Canyon sites during the Basketmaker III period, but thereafter high elevation woods were more commonly used." At Pueblo Alto (AD 1020-1140), "pinyon comprised 31% of the tree ring specimens used for floors and ceilings while juniper contributed 9%, suggesting that juniper was infrequently used for larger and medium-sized beams in the construction process during the Bonito phase."
**Page**: p. 11
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Toll 1985 [ref 59] for 96% figure; Toll 1987 [ref 37] for Pueblo Alto construction wood
**Depends on**: Dendrochronological and macrobotanical analyses
**Notes**: The shift from 96% local conifers in Basketmaker III to predominant use of distant high-elevation woods during the Bonito phase demonstrates local resource exhaustion. This is directly relevant to the construction cost parameter: as local wood was depleted, procurement costs increased substantially because timber had to be transported from the Chuska Mountains (75+ km) and Zuni Mountains.

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: Well-documented shift in timber procurement. The transition correlates with local woodland depletion rather than preference change.

### Claim 8

**Statement**: "Juniper wood for construction was being imported into Chaco Canyon from the Zuni Mountains, 75 km away, as early as AD 850, a sign that local juniper wood was in short supply by that time."
**Page**: pp. 6-7
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Guiterman et al. 2016 [ref 35]
**Depends on**: Dendrochronological sourcing (Guiterman et al. 2016)
**Notes**: This is an important chronological marker. By the very beginning of the Bonito phase (AD 850), local juniper was already insufficient, necessitating long-distance procurement. This means the entire great house construction episode occurred under conditions of local resource scarcity, amplifying the costliness of construction.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (based on Guiterman et al.'s dendrochronological timber sourcing study)
**Assessment**: This is well-established from multiple lines of evidence. The 75 km transport distance for juniper by AD 850 is a key data point for calibrating transport costs in the signaling model.

### Claim 9

**Statement**: The woodland decline was anthropogenic rather than climate-driven. Key evidence: (1) "juniper populations remained intact through the Late Archaic period, a time of 'great aridity,' then declined as the human population of Chaco increased during a time of more mesic conditions"; (2) "areas adjacent to Chaco Canyon have retained their PJW through time" at similar elevations within 100 km; (3) PJW survived extensive aridification events of the Middle Archaic and "likely could have survived the droughts that occurred between AD 200 and 1300."
**Page**: pp. 12-13
**Type**: interpretive (synthesizing multiple empirical observations)
**Evidence cited by author**: Hall 1977 [ref 24] for Archaic aridity; Little 1971 [ref 69], Lentz 1984 [ref 70] for adjacent PJW survival; Drake et al. 2012 [ref 26] for Middle Archaic aridification; Huang et al. 2010 [ref 68] for drought tolerance
**Depends on**: Multiple palynological, packrat midden, and modern vegetation studies
**Notes**: This is the paper's central argument against the competing hypothesis (Wills et al. 2014) that drought caused deforestation. The three-pronged argument (decline during mesic conditions, survival in adjacent areas, survival of prior droughts) is compelling but relies on the assumption that human population and climate can be clearly separated as causal factors.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate to strong (multiple converging lines of evidence, but human-climate interaction is complex)
**Assessment**: The argument is well-constructed. The fact that PJW survived the Middle Archaic aridification but not the Puebloan occupation period is particularly telling. However, the possibility of synergistic effects (human exploitation + drought acting together) cannot be ruled out.

### Claim 10

**Statement**: "Deer and other large mammals used as meat sources at Pueblo Bonito were obtained from distances greater than 40 km away." "If local fuel and meat resources were both depleted and only available at great distances, this would have put tremendous pressure on the inhabitants to obtain essential resources."
**Page**: p. 13
**Type**: empirical/interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Mills 2018 [ref 8]; Grimstead et al. 2019 [ref 82] (based on isotopic and rainfall data)
**Depends on**: Isotopic sourcing studies
**Notes**: The depletion of both timber and game indicates broad ecosystem degradation beyond just woodland loss. This bears on the signaling model's environmental uncertainty parameter: resource scarcity was not just about rainfall variability but about cumulative degradation of the resource base, which would have amplified the effects of drought.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (based on isotopic sourcing)
**Assessment**: The 40 km transport distance for game is a specific, measurable indicator of local resource depletion.

### Claim 11

**Statement**: The period from AD 990 to 1130 "was a time of increasing precipitation." A multi-decadal drought lasted from AD 1130 to 1180, which "coincided with the departure from the canyon by many Chacoan inhabitants." This was followed by the "Great Drought" from AD 1276 to 1299, during which "the remaining inhabitants emigrated from the canyon."
**Page**: p. 4
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Dean et al. 2002 [ref 11]
**Depends on**: Dendroclimatological reconstructions
**Notes**: This provides the climatic chronology framing the study. The Bonito phase florescence (peak construction) coincided with relatively wet conditions, while abandonment coincided with severe drought. This is consistent with the signaling model's prediction: signaling investment is sustainable during moderate environmental conditions but collapses under severe sustained drought.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (standard dendroclimatological evidence)
**Assessment**: These are well-established paleoclimatic findings. The wet-to-dry transition around AD 1130 is one of the most replicated findings in southwestern paleoclimatology.

### Claim 12

**Statement**: "Our observations of increased sedimentation and aggradation in the canyon indicate that there was considerable erosion during the time of occupation by the Ancestral Puebloans." The removal of vegetation "would lead to accelerated erosion."
**Page**: p. 14
**Type**: empirical/interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Scarborough et al. 2018 [ref 19]
**Depends on**: Stratigraphic analyses from excavation profiles
**Notes**: Erosion is a secondary consequence of deforestation that would have further degraded agricultural productivity (loss of topsoil, disruption of water management infrastructure). This adds another dimension to environmental degradation: it is not just resource scarcity but landscape instability that threatened the Chacoan system.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (sedimentation is documented; causal attribution to deforestation-driven erosion is interpretive)
**Assessment**: The observation of increased sedimentation during the occupation period is consistent with the deforestation hypothesis. The connection to water management infrastructure (check dams, canals) is particularly relevant to understanding agricultural vulnerability.

### Claim 13

**Statement**: "The destabilizing impact resulting from recurrent woodland harvesting likely contributed to the environmental unpredictability and difficulty in procuring essential resources suffered by the Ancestral Puebloans prior to their ultimate departure from Chaco Canyon."
**Page**: p. 1 (abstract)
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Synthesis of all evidence presented in the paper
**Depends on**: Claims 1-12
**Notes**: This is the paper's central conclusion. Woodland depletion was not just a consequence of the Chacoan system but a contributor to environmental unpredictability, creating a positive feedback loop: human exploitation degraded the resource base, which increased vulnerability to drought, which ultimately contributed to abandonment. For the signaling model, this suggests that the sigma (environmental uncertainty) parameter was not purely exogenous but was partly endogenous, amplified by the very activities (construction, fuel consumption) that signaling required.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (the conclusion follows logically from the evidence but the feedback mechanism is proposed rather than demonstrated)
**Assessment**: The endogeneity of environmental uncertainty is an important insight for the model. If signaling investment (construction timber, fuel for ceremonies) degrades the resource base and increases sigma, this creates a potential for system collapse even without external climate shocks.

### Claim 14

**Statement**: Charcoal data show juniper charcoal first appearing at 100 BC and disappearing after AD 750. Pinyon charcoal first appears around AD 300 and ends at AD 1100. "Carbonized wood remains of willow, cottonwood and sumac appear as early as AD 300 and are undetected after AD 750."
**Page**: p. 9 (Table 1 discussion)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Table 1 (macrobotanical data from 2013-2015 excavations)
**Depends on**: Original charcoal identification and chronometric dating
**Notes**: The charcoal record provides independent confirmation of the pollen record: juniper use ended by about AD 750, pinyon use continued to AD 1100, and riparian species (willow, cottonwood) disappeared early. The earlier disappearance of juniper charcoal compared to pinyon charcoal is consistent with preferential use of juniper for fuel (Claim 6).

**Evidence strength**: Strong (independent line of evidence corroborating pollen data)
**Assessment**: The convergence of pollen and charcoal evidence strengthens confidence in the deforestation chronology.

## Claim Type Definitions

- **Empirical**: Direct observation or measurement reported by the author
- **Interpretive**: Inference from empirical data; not directly observable
- **Speculative**: Acknowledged by the author as tentative, or basis unclear
- **Methodological**: Claim about how to conduct or interpret research

## Evidence Strength Ratings

- **Strong**: Claim is well-supported by the evidence presented
- **Moderate**: Claim is partially supported; some evidence but gaps remain
- **Weak**: Claim is poorly supported by the evidence presented
- **Assertion without evidence**: No evidence cited; claim is stated as fact without support

## Summary Assessment

Lentz et al. 2021 provides the most detailed palynological and macrobotanical evidence for progressive deforestation of Chaco Canyon's pinyon-juniper woodland over a multi-millennial period of human occupation. The paper's primary contribution is documenting the chronology and magnitude of woodland decline through pollen and charcoal data, with juniper declining from 20% of the pollen assemblage before 600 BC to 2% by AD 1100. The paper argues convincingly that this decline was anthropogenic rather than climate-driven, citing multiple lines of evidence (decline during mesic periods, survival in adjacent areas, drought tolerance of PJW species). For the Chaco signaling model, the key implications are: (1) environmental degradation was cumulative and partly endogenous, driven by fuel consumption and timber extraction; (2) the resource base was already significantly degraded before the Bonito phase construction boom, making signaling investment even costlier; (3) the progressive resource depletion created a positive feedback that amplified environmental uncertainty; and (4) the shift to long-distance timber procurement by AD 850 increased the cost parameter (C) for construction signaling over time. The paper does not address signaling theory, but the environmental data it provides are essential for calibrating the model's environmental parameters and understanding the feedback between signaling investment and resource base degradation.
