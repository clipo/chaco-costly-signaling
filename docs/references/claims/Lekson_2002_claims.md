## Claims

### Claim 1

**Statement**: The two strongest predictors of warfare in non-state societies are "resource unpredictability" (R = 0.631, p < 0.001) and "socialization for mistrust" (R = 0.352, p < 0.025), based on Ember and Ember's (1992) cross-cultural study using standardized coefficients from the Human Relations Area File.
**Page**: p. 611
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Ember and Ember 1992:242, Table 1
**Depends on**: Ember and Ember 1992 cross-cultural regression analysis
**Notes**: These two predictors map onto the Chaco model's environmental (resource unpredictability = sigma parameter) and social (socialization for mistrust = conflict deterrence via Layer 2) components. The finding that resource unpredictability is the strongest predictor supports the model's assumption that environmental uncertainty drives signaling investment, which in turn affects conflict probability.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (standardized regression coefficients from large cross-cultural sample)

---

### Claim 2

**Statement**: Periods of "high temporal variability" in tree-ring departures (i.e., resource unpredictability) occurred four times in the Southwest: 310-380, 750-1000, 1250-1560, and 1730-1825. Dean (1988, 1996) defined these as periods when year-to-year variation in tree-ring indices was highly variable on decadal timescales.
**Page**: pp. 612-613
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Dean 1988, 1996; Dean 1996:43
**Depends on**: Jeffrey Dean's dendroclimatological reconstructions
**Notes**: The 750-1000 period of high temporal variability overlaps with the early development of Chaco (construction begins ~850). The 1000-1250 gap between periods of high variability corresponds closely to Chaco's Middle period peace (Pax Chaco). The 1250-1560 return of high variability corresponds to Late period warfare. This temporal alignment is the paper's most important empirical contribution for the Chaco signaling model.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (based on well-established dendroclimatological data, though periodization boundaries are approximate)

---

### Claim 3

**Statement**: After incorporating both "high temporal variability" and "high seasonal variability" (unique regional disruption of precipitation patterns from 1250 to 1450), the indicated periods of significant resource unpredictability become: 310-380, 750-1000, 1250-1560, and 1730-1825. The 1250-1560 period was caused by both high temporal variability and chaotic seasonal variability simultaneously.
**Page**: pp. 612-613
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Dean 1996:43; Ahlstrom et al. 1995
**Depends on**: Dean's seasonal and temporal variability analyses
**Notes**: The uniqueness of the 1250-1450 seasonal pattern disruption is significant: it was "a 200-year, regional-scale disruption of a climatic pattern that characterized the Southwest for the preceding 550 years and the following 550 years" (Dean 1996:43). This compound unpredictability directly amplified both resource stress and the social disruption that the Chaco signaling system had been managing.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (dendroclimatological data is well-established)

---

### Claim 4

**Statement**: LeBlanc's (1999) chronology of warfare in the Southwest defines three periods: Early (0-900), Middle (900-1150), and Late (1250-Spanish contact). The Middle period from 900 to 1250 was "an era of unprecedented peace," characterized by the absence of warfare and the presence of peace, "punctuated by EP Events" (executions/extreme processing).
**Page**: pp. 613-614
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: LeBlanc 1999:153, 195, 313; Kuckelman et al. 2000; Turner and Turner 1999; White 1992
**Depends on**: LeBlanc's (1999) synthesis of archaeological violence evidence; Lekson extends the Middle period from LeBlanc's original 900-1150 to 900-1250
**Notes**: The Middle period peace (900-1250) corresponds almost exactly to the Chaco florescence and the continuation of the Chacoan political system at Aztec Ruins. This is the core "Pax Chaco" evidence. Lekson extends LeBlanc's Middle period to include the "interim" period of 1150-1250, arguing that peace continued under Aztec. The model's Layer 2 prediction (monument signaling deters intergroup conflict) is supported by this temporal correlation.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (LeBlanc's synthesis draws on extensive archaeological evidence; the temporal boundaries are approximate but the pattern is robust)

---

### Claim 5

**Statement**: The Middle period "EP Events" (extreme processing events) were "a limited but very specific form of violence": groups of people executed, dismembered, mutilated, and sometimes cannibalized. Turner and Turner (1999) note only about 75 possible instances, and re-analysis may reduce this number. These events were "spectacularly brutal" but few in number, and were "sharply concentrated in the Middle period."
**Page**: pp. 614-615
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Turner and Turner 1999; White 1992:52-53; Kuckelman et al. 2000:159; LeBlanc 1999:162-186, 172
**Depends on**: Bioarchaeological and taphonomic analyses of human remains
**Notes**: EP Events are distinct from warfare. Lekson interprets them as "coercive force directed by and perhaps emanating first from Chaco and, later, Aztec Ruins" (p. 618), a form of politically motivated social control. This is relevant to the Chaco model: the signaling system may have been maintained partly through targeted coercion rather than purely through the honest-signal mechanism. The model should account for the possibility that Layer 2 (conflict deterrence) operated through a combination of signaling and coercion.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (well-documented archaeologically, though interpretation of intent is debated)

### Claim 6

**Statement**: The correspondence between periods of resource unpredictability and periods of warfare is "striking": "Violence correlates well with resource unpredictably between 750 and 1600 in the Pueblo Southwest, as do peace and resource predictability."
**Page**: p. 616
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Dean 1988, 1996; LeBlanc 1999; comparison presented in Figure 2
**Depends on**: Alignment of Dean's climatic periodization with LeBlanc's warfare chronology
**Notes**: This is the paper's central empirical finding. The correlation is not precise (there is a ~100-year disjuncture between the end of Early period violence at 900 and the end of high temporal variability at 1000), which Lekson acknowledges. The imprecision is expected given the different resolution of the two chronologies. For the Chaco model, this supports the prediction that environmental uncertainty drives both signaling investment and conflict, with the signaling system mediating the relationship.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (the temporal correlation is broadly supportive but not precise; the comparison involves different chronological methods with different resolutions)

---

### Claim 7

**Statement**: There were "three sequential major regional centers or capitals of the Pueblo Southwest: Chaco (ca. 900-1125), Aztec Ruins (ca. 1110-1275), and Paquime (ca. 1250 to 1450)." Each was "the clear primate settlement of its place and time; each was the center of large but different regions."
**Page**: p. 617
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Lekson 1999 (The Chaco Meridian)
**Depends on**: Lekson's (1999) reconstruction of Southwest political history
**Notes**: This sequential capital model is contested but provides important context for understanding the post-Chaco period. If Aztec represents a continuation of the Chacoan political system (and its signaling framework), then the "collapse" of Chaco was more of a spatial relocation than a system failure. The model should consider whether Layer 2 and Layer 3 mechanisms were transferred to Aztec rather than simply ceasing.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (the Chaco-Aztec connection is well-supported; the Paquime link is more speculative and contested)

---

### Claim 8

**Statement**: "Chaco began as a small-scale political solution to problems of uneven food production within its immediate area (that is, the San Juan Basin)." It "rose as a political solution to raiding and feuding caused (at least in part) by resource unpredictability" and "managed that unpredictability successfully for about a century (that is, from 900 to 1000)."
**Page**: p. 619
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Judge 1979, 1989; Judge et al. 1981; Lekson 1999; Malville 2001
**Depends on**: Judge's (1989) model of Chacoan development; Lekson's (1999) political history
**Notes**: This framing of Chaco as a political response to resource unpredictability aligns directly with the signaling model's core mechanism: environmental uncertainty creates demand for cooperation networks (Layer 3), which are facilitated by signaling (Layers 1-2). The 900-1000 timeframe for Chaco's initial success maps onto the Early Bonito period.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (the general framework is plausible and supported by multiple scholars, but the specific causal mechanism is inferred)

---

### Claim 9

**Statement**: "Following Judge's (1989) chronology, Chaco expanded beyond the ecological limits of the San Juan Basin only after 1000; that is, after the 750-1000 episode of high temporal variability ended." Chaco's political structure developed in the tenth century and "evolved in remarkable ways after the onset of more favorable climatic conditions about 1000."
**Page**: p. 619
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Judge 1989; Dean 1988, 1996
**Depends on**: Chronological alignment of construction data with climate data
**Notes**: This is a crucial observation for the Chaco model. It suggests that the Chaco system was built during a period of high resource unpredictability but expanded during a period of relative climatic stability. The model's sigma parameter would be high during the system's genesis (demanding signaling) and lower during its expansion (when the cooperation networks were paying off). This complicates a simple "signaling responds to current stress" prediction.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (the chronological alignment is supported but the causal interpretation is one of several possibilities)

---

### Claim 10

**Statement**: The disjuncture between the onset of resource unpredictability at 1250 and continued construction at Aztec until 1275 "can be understood as evidence of political failure. The end of regional order does not result in the immediate abandonment of the center." Aztec attempted to "perpetuate Chaco's central political power" but "failed."
**Page**: p. 619
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Lekson 1999; Rapoport 1993
**Depends on**: Lekson's sequential capital model; construction chronology at Aztec Ruins
**Notes**: The lag between environmental deterioration and political collapse is consistent with the signaling model's prediction: the system has inertia (existing cooperation networks, accumulated symbolic capital) that delays collapse even after conditions deteriorate. The model could be parameterized to include this lag effect through the signal depreciation term.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (the temporal sequence is documented; the political interpretation is Lekson's, which is contested)

### Claim 11

**Statement**: LeBlanc's Early period (0-900) was marked by "intermittent but persistent violence: endemic raiding and feuding." The late Early period (700s-900) showed "marked 'increase in the level of warfare during the late Pueblo I period, in the late 700s and 800s'" before violence "abruptly terminated [about 900]."
**Page**: pp. 613-614
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: LeBlanc 1999:145-146, 153
**Depends on**: LeBlanc's (1999) synthesis of violence evidence
**Notes**: The abrupt termination of Early period violence around AD 900 coincides with the onset of Chaco's development. This temporal coincidence supports the model's Layer 2 prediction: the initiation of monument signaling at Chaco deterred intergroup conflict. The "abrupt" nature of the transition is particularly significant, suggesting a threshold effect rather than gradual change.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (based on LeBlanc's comprehensive synthesis; the temporal coincidence is well-documented)

---

### Claim 12

**Statement**: The Late period (1250-1600) was marked by "large-scale warfare: village against village and even multiple-village alliances against single villages or enemy alliances." This "real war" was "unprecedented in southwestern prehistory" and represented a qualitative escalation from Early period raiding and feuding.
**Page**: pp. 615, 620
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: LeBlanc 1999; Haas and Creamer 1997; Upham 1982
**Depends on**: Archaeological evidence of fortified sites, burned villages, mass violence
**Notes**: The Late period escalation to "real war" followed the collapse of the Chacoan political system and the return of resource unpredictability. The model predicts exactly this: when the signaling system collapses, the conflict-deterrence function of Layer 2 disappears, and violence returns. The escalation beyond Early period levels may reflect the additional factor of "socialization for fear" during the Middle period (Claim 13).

**Evidence strength**: Strong (extensive archaeological evidence of Late period warfare)

---

### Claim 13

**Statement**: Middle period EP Events constituted "a dramatic socialization for fear" that, paradoxically, may have contributed to the severity of Late period warfare. "Three and one-half centuries of Middle period EP Events must have produced lasting cultural patterns, internal causes, that exacerbated and escalated warfare in the Late period."
**Page**: pp. 620-621
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Ember and Ember 1992:245, 254; Toren 1996:512
**Depends on**: Ember and Ember's cross-cultural finding that socialization for fear predicts warfare; archaeological evidence of EP Events; the assumption that EP Events constituted socialization
**Notes**: This is Lekson's most speculative but also most original argument. If the Chacoan signaling system relied partly on coercion (EP Events) to maintain peace, then the legacy of that coercion may have amplified post-collapse conflict. This suggests a dark side to the Chaco signaling system that the model should acknowledge: the system may have suppressed violence in the short term while creating conditions for more severe violence after collapse.

**Evidence strength**: Weak (the causal chain from EP Events to socialization for fear to Late period warfare is plausible but highly inferential; the cross-cultural data support the general mechanism but not the specific application)

---

### Claim 14

**Statement**: "Construction at Chaco began about 900 (or even 850), two centuries of Early period intense raiding ended 'abruptly' (LeBlanc's word). With the end of resource unpredictability at 1000, Chaco expands to became a major regional center (ca. 1020; Judge 1989). The coincidence of the end of Early period feuding/raiding, the rise of Chaco, and changes in resource predictability is close, if not precise."
**Page**: p. 617
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: LeBlanc 1999; Judge 1989; Windes and Ford 1996
**Depends on**: Alignment of three independent chronologies (violence, construction, climate)
**Notes**: This three-way alignment is the paper's most important contribution to the Chaco signaling model. It suggests that Chaco emerged as a response to Early period violence and resource unpredictability, and that its success in suppressing conflict coincided with improving climatic conditions. The signaling model can accommodate this: the system was initiated during stress (high sigma), succeeded in reducing conflict (Layer 2), and thrived when conditions improved (lower sigma but persistent cooperation networks from Layer 3).

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (the three-way alignment is suggestive but the chronological imprecision, acknowledged by Lekson, limits the strength of the inference)

## Claim Type Definitions

- **Empirical**: Direct observation or measurement reported by the author
- **Interpretive**: Inference from empirical data; not directly observable
- **Speculative**: Acknowledged by the author as tentative, or basis unclear
- **Methodological**: Claim about how to conduct or interpret research

## Evidence Strength Ratings

- **Strong**: Claim is well-supported by the evidence presented
- **Moderate**: Claim is partially supported; some evidence but gaps remain
- **Weak**: Claim is poorly supported by the evidence presented
- **Assertion without evidence**: No evidence cited; claim is stated as fact without support

## Summary Assessment

Lekson 2002 provides the most systematic alignment of resource unpredictability, violence chronology, and political history for the Pueblo Southwest. The paper's central empirical contribution is the demonstration that periods of warfare correspond to periods of resource unpredictability (Ember and Ember 1992 model), with the Middle period peace (900-1250, "Pax Chaco") corresponding to a gap between periods of high temporal variability. The paper draws on Dean's (1988, 1996) dendroclimatological periodization and LeBlanc's (1999) warfare synthesis to construct a unified chronological framework. For the Chaco signaling model, Lekson 2002 provides essential evidence for the Layer 2 prediction (monument signaling deters conflict), the relationship between environmental uncertainty (sigma) and conflict, and the collapse dynamics when the signaling system fails. The EP Events discussion adds an important complication: the Chacoan peace may have been maintained partly through coercion, not purely through honest signaling.
