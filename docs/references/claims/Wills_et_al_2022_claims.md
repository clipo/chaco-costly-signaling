# Claims Extraction: Wills et al. 2022

**Source:** Wills, W. H., Paulina F. Przystupa, and Katharine Williams. 2022. Communication in the Chaco World: A Consideration of Time and Labor Mobilization. *American Antiquity* 87(3):457-486.

**DOI:** 10.1017/aaq.2022.33

---

## Claim 1

- **Statement:** Great house construction during the Bonito Phase (ca. AD 850-1200) "required massive amounts of building material and efficient mobilization and coordination of large labor pools."
- **Page:** 457 (abstract)
- **Type:** Empirical
- **Evidence cited by author:** General archaeological consensus
- **Depends on:** Lekson 1984, Windes and McKenna 2001
- **Notes:** Framing premise for the entire study.
- **Evidence strength:** Strong; widely accepted in the literature.
- **Assessment:** Uncontested.

---

## Claim 2

- **Statement:** The immense size of the largest great houses (100-600+ rooms, 8,000-24,000 m^2) required coordination of large numbers of people at a scale previously unknown in the Ancestral Pueblo region.
- **Page:** 458
- **Type:** Empirical
- **Evidence cited by author:** Lekson 1984:272, 1999:49
- **Depends on:** Architectural survey data
- **Notes:** Provides specific scale parameters for great house sizes.
- **Evidence strength:** Strong; based on direct architectural measurements.
- **Assessment:** Well-supported by multiple independent studies.

---

## Claim 3

- **Statement:** Between AD 1050 and 1080, there were 11 construction stages requiring 20,000 to 55,000 person hours, and 12 stages requiring 55,000 to 192,000 person hours of labor.
- **Page:** 458
- **Type:** Empirical
- **Evidence cited by author:** Lekson 1984:258-259
- **Depends on:** Lekson's construction stage analysis
- **Notes:** Key quantitative data on labor requirements for great house construction.
- **Evidence strength:** Moderate; estimates from 1984 study, based on reasonable assumptions but not independently verified.
- **Assessment:** These are the standard labor estimates in the Chaco literature. Useful for parameterizing C.

---

## Claim 4

- **Statement:** Lekson (1984:258-260) identified at least 32 major "construction events" at great houses dating between approximately AD 1025 and 1130, with roughly 90% of those hours consumed by creating mortar and erecting walls.
- **Page:** 461
- **Type:** Empirical
- **Evidence cited by author:** Lekson 1984:258-260, 278
- **Depends on:** Lekson's labor analysis
- **Notes:** Specifies that wall construction and mortar production were the dominant labor costs. Mortar required water, which was unpredictable.
- **Evidence strength:** Moderate; standard reference for labor allocation.
- **Assessment:** The 90% figure for mortar/wall labor is important for understanding what drove costs.

---

## Claim 5

- **Statement:** Water made up 30% to 40% of wall and ceiling volume in great houses as mortar and plaster, and water for large-scale construction was mainly available as storm runoff events in late summer and early fall, which are "extremely unpredictable in frequency, size, and location."
- **Page:** 460
- **Type:** Empirical
- **Evidence cited by author:** Vivian 1992; Lekson 1984:278; Love 1977
- **Depends on:** Architectural analysis and hydrological data
- **Notes:** Critical link between construction scheduling and environmental uncertainty. Water availability constrained the construction window.
- **Evidence strength:** Strong for the volumetric estimate; strong for the rainfall unpredictability claim.
- **Assessment:** Directly relevant to the sigma parameter: construction itself was subject to environmental uncertainty, creating scheduling conflicts with agriculture.

---

## Claim 6

- **Statement:** The primary sources of labor for communal building projects were agricultural communities located within one to three hours of the largest buildings in the canyon.
- **Page:** 457 (abstract), 475
- **Type:** Empirical/Interpretive
- **Evidence cited by author:** Least cost path analysis results (this study)
- **Depends on:** LCA methodology, site location data from ARMS
- **Notes:** Central finding of the paper. Defines the labor source area as a 1-3 hour travel-time zone.
- **Evidence strength:** Moderate; based on modeled travel times, not direct evidence of labor origin.
- **Assessment:** Plausible but not demonstrated. The 3-hour threshold is argued rather than empirically validated.

---

## Claim 7

- **Statement:** Most outlying (extra-canyon) great houses in the primary analysis are within two to three hours of one or more great houses located in the Chaco Core for average hikers. Running significantly reduces these times.
- **Page:** 464
- **Type:** Empirical
- **Evidence cited by author:** LCA results (Table 1, Figures 2-3)
- **Depends on:** DEM data, Tobler hiking function
- **Notes:** Quantifies the communication network density. Travel times from Pueblo Bonito: to Kin Bineola 1.99 hrs (walk), 0.78 hrs (run); to Kin Klizhin 3.62 hrs (walk), 1.17 hrs (run); to Greenlee 2.43 hrs (walk).
- **Evidence strength:** Strong for the modeling results; the times are idealized.
- **Assessment:** The travel time data are internally consistent and reproducible (code provided).

---

## Claim 8

- **Statement:** The overall network of time pathways between great houses exhibits an unexpected grid-like structure, with an inner ring of sites within 1-2 hours and an outer ring at roughly 2.5-3.0 hours from the Chaco Core.
- **Page:** 465-466
- **Type:** Empirical
- **Evidence cited by author:** LCA results (Figure 3)
- **Depends on:** Site distribution and topography
- **Notes:** The grid pattern results from outlier settlements located in parallel tributaries at approximately equal distances from the canyon.
- **Evidence strength:** Moderate; pattern is descriptive rather than statistically tested.
- **Assessment:** The ring structure is a novel empirical finding. The 3-hour outer ring aligns with the labor mobilization argument.

---

## Claim 9

- **Statement:** The only documented technological innovation associated with great house construction in Chaco Canyon appears to have been diversion canals at Pueblo Bonito, which protected the great house from flooding and may have impounded runoff near construction sites.
- **Page:** 457
- **Type:** Empirical
- **Evidence cited by author:** Wills 2017; Wills et al. 2021
- **Depends on:** Prior fieldwork
- **Notes:** Argues that great house construction methods were homologous with modern building, just without leveraging technologies for lifting and transporting heavy weights.
- **Evidence strength:** Moderate; absence of evidence for other innovations.
- **Assessment:** Supports the interpretation that construction was labor-intensive rather than technology-intensive.

---

## Claim 10

- **Statement:** Light signals (fire, smoke, reflective materials) were unlikely to have played a significant role in mobilizing construction labor because there are no ethnographic examples from the Puebloan area of conveying messages via signaling other than binary information (presence/absence), and complex construction coordination required face-to-face communication.
- **Page:** 474
- **Type:** Interpretive
- **Evidence cited by author:** Beers 2014 (review of Indigenous signaling); Aguilar and Preucel 2013; Liebmann 2008; Ellis 1991
- **Depends on:** Ethnographic analogy, information theory argument
- **Notes:** Directly argues against intervisibility-based "signaling" interpretation. Great houses outside the lower canyon were often not intervisible.
- **Evidence strength:** Moderate; the ethnographic argument is well-supported, but absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.
- **Assessment:** Important for distinguishing between "signaling" in the communication sense and costly signaling theory. The paper uses "signaling" to mean literal message transmission, not the evolutionary/economic concept.

---

## Claim 11

- **Statement:** The North and South Roads probably did not enhance information flow much (if at all) because they were not the only equivalent time pathways available between the same termini. The roads may be navigation features between destinations rather than transportation corridors.
- **Page:** 475
- **Type:** Interpretive
- **Evidence cited by author:** LCA results (Figure 8); shorter road features interpreted as cosmological rather than transportation
- **Depends on:** Fowler and Stein 1992; Marshall 1997:71; Roney 1992
- **Notes:** Challenges the assumption that Chaco roads functioned as a communication/transportation system.
- **Evidence strength:** Moderate; the LCA evidence is convincing that roads were not the fastest routes.
- **Assessment:** The finding that roads were not optimal transportation routes supports their interpretation as having symbolic/cosmological significance, which is consistent with a signaling framework (roads as display, not infrastructure).

---

## Claim 12

- **Statement:** Assembling work parties for great house construction required diverting labor from agricultural activities in a society characterized by low-level food production, and there are no indications that any portion of the able-bodied population would have been wholly free from agricultural responsibilities.
- **Page:** 461
- **Type:** Interpretive
- **Evidence cited by author:** General assessment; Vivian et al. 2006; Wills 2017
- **Depends on:** Population estimates and subsistence models
- **Notes:** Directly quantifies the opportunity cost of construction labor: every worker diverted to construction was a worker lost to farming during the same critical season.
- **Evidence strength:** Strong; the seasonal overlap between construction and agriculture is well-documented.
- **Assessment:** Key evidence for the C parameter. Construction represented a direct trade-off with subsistence production.

---

## Claim 13

- **Statement:** The construction window for mortar-based wall erection was limited to July through September, when precipitation and warm temperatures overlap. Walls and roofs had to be completed before cold weather onset (below 5C average daily minimum, with lowest daily minimum of -26C).
- **Page:** 461
- **Type:** Empirical
- **Evidence cited by author:** McHenry 1984; Dean and Warren 1983; Wills 2000
- **Depends on:** Material science of earthen mortars; modern climate data
- **Notes:** The construction season is exactly the agricultural growing season, maximizing the scheduling conflict.
- **Evidence strength:** Strong; based on physical properties of mortar materials.
- **Assessment:** Provides the mechanistic basis for why construction and agriculture competed for labor.

---

## Claim 14

- **Statement:** Messaging times between distant outliers and canyon great houses ranged between 3 and 14+ hours. Simple least cost paths between individual outliers and Pueblo Bonito exceed 8 to 14 hours (except for Indian Creek at 3 or more hours), placing these sites at the same messaging range as Aztec and Salmon to the north and Kin Ya'a to the south.
- **Page:** 469
- **Type:** Empirical
- **Evidence cited by author:** LCA secondary analysis (Figures 5-6)
- **Depends on:** DEM data, site locations
- **Notes:** Defines the outer boundary of effective communication. Beyond 3 hours, coordinating construction schedules with unpredictable water availability becomes impractical.
- **Evidence strength:** Moderate; travel times are modeled, not observed.
- **Assessment:** The 3-hour threshold for practical labor coordination is a key empirical constraint on the signaling model's spatial extent.
