## Claims

### Claim 1

**Statement**: Six new great houses were built in Chaco Canyon during the Late Bonito phase (A.D. 1100-1140): Casa Chiquita, Headquarters Site A, Kin Kletso, New Alto, Tsin Kletsin, and Wijiji. These structures employed McElmo-style masonry (compact, symmetrical, pecked sandstone blocks) and were erected in short, self-contained construction events between A.D. 1100 and 1130.
**Page**: pp. 418-419 (Table 1, p. 419)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Lekson 1986:267-269; Vivian 1990:367; Vivian and Mathews 1965; Bannister 1964; Voll and Mayer 1964
**Depends on**: Tree-ring dates for construction events
**Notes**: Table 1 provides detailed data for each structure: Casa Chiquita (34/59 ground floor rooms, 1-3 stories, 4.2 sq m avg room size); Headquarters Site A (39/39 rooms, 1 story, 7-9 sq m); Kin Kletso (57/132 rooms, 2-3 stories, 8.6-9.4 sq m); New Alto (32/58 rooms, 1-2 stories, 2.5-7.1 sq m); Tsin Kletsin (55/74+15 arc rooms, 1-2 stories, 4-5 sq m); Wijiji (104/206 rooms, 2-3 stories, 5.4 sq m).

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: The data are well-documented through dendrochronology and excavation. These structures are directly relevant to the signaling model because they represent a burst of construction investment during a period of environmental and social stress (post-AD 1090 drought).

### Claim 2

**Statement**: Late Bonito great houses were much smaller than Classic Bonito phase great houses and required substantially less labor. McElmo-style buildings represent "Class II" construction requiring 55,000-90,000 person-hours, approximately one-half to one-third as much labor as Classic Bonito phase "Class IV" construction requiring 170,000-192,000 person-hours.
**Page**: p. 423 (citing Lekson 1986:257-269)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Lekson 1986:257-269
**Depends on**: Lekson's labor cost estimates
**Notes**: The standardized McElmo unit template would have "facilitated speedy and straightforward construction of multiple buildings" (p. 423). Beam transport was the most time-consuming activity in great house construction.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (derived from Lekson's estimates, not independently verified)
**Assessment**: The cost differential between Late Bonito and Classic Bonito construction has direct implications for the signaling model. If Late Bonito leaders had less labor capacity (reduced population, waning regional authority), lower-cost construction may represent a shift in the cost parameter C, with leaders maintaining signaling activity at a reduced investment level.

### Claim 3

**Statement**: Late Bonito great house builders were deliberately concerned with the visual impact of their structures on viewers. They employed "illusory tricks to emphasize both mass and height," including unfaced secondary parallel walls along the south sides of Casa Chiquita and Kin Kletso, and crude internal crosswalls that turned the space between walls into small cells (approximately 150 x 50 cm). A similar parallel wall at Kin Bineola served the same purpose.
**Page**: pp. 422-423
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Fowler and Stein 1992:111; Fowler et al. 1987:61-66; Vivian and Mathews 1965:44; Voll and Mayer 1964:7; Marshall et al. 1979:59
**Depends on**: Excavation data from multiple sites
**Notes**: The walls "turned the space between the walls into small 150 x 50 cm cells" found along building exteriors and had "no clear structural or functional purpose" (p. 423). This "mere added mass" was designed to make structures look larger than they were.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (physical evidence from excavations at multiple sites)
**Assessment**: This is direct evidence for signal manipulation, where builders invested in features designed to amplify the perceived signal at lower cost than actual structural expansion. This is relevant to signal honesty: if signals can be inflated through "illusory tricks," this introduces potential for dishonest signaling, which the Spence condition must address.

### Claim 4

**Statement**: Late Bonito great houses were positioned on the landscape to create a patterned, nested series of oppositional relationships. Six Late Bonito great houses form two north-south axes: the Pueblo Alto-Tsin Kletsin axis and a new axis through Casa Rinconada. Four Late Bonito houses are positioned on the canyon floor (Casa Chiquita, Kin Kletso, Headquarters Site A, Wijiji) and two on the canyon rim (Tsin Kletsin, New Alto), creating a vertical dualism of visible/invisible.
**Page**: pp. 424-426
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Stein and Lekson 1992:90; Vivian 1997:12, 14, 15; Fritz 1978; Lekson 1999; Sofaer 1997
**Depends on**: Survey data on site locations and intervisibility
**Notes**: Casa Chiquita, Headquarters Site A, and Wijiji are the only canyon great houses that lack a line-of-sight connection to any other great house. They were "deliberately positioned in cul-de-sacs or around canyon corners, just out of sight of others" (p. 426), creating a "deliberate concern with invisibility."

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: The spatial patterning of Late Bonito great houses supports the interpretation that their placement was communicative rather than purely functional. The visible/invisible dualism is relevant to the signaling model: structures positioned for maximum visibility function as intergroup signals (Layer 2), while those positioned for invisibility may serve primarily intragroup or ritual functions. The distinction between visibility-oriented and concealment-oriented construction provides a test for the model's predictions about signaling audience.

### Claim 5

**Statement**: Classic Bonito phase architecture (A.D. 1020-1100) expressed basic tenets of a Chacoan worldview: directionality, balanced dualism, and the canyon as center place. Horizontal divisions correspond to cardinal directions; vertical divisions include upper and lower worlds. These beliefs are shared across contemporary Pueblo groups (Tewa, Tiwa, Keresan, Zuni, Hopi) who speak different languages and otherwise differ in ritual practice.
**Page**: pp. 416-417
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Ortiz 1965, 1969, 1972; Fox 1967; Cushing 1896, 1966; Ladd 1979; Young 1988; Hieb 1979; Dozier 1960; Swentzell 1992
**Depends on**: Ethnographic analogy from modern Puebloan peoples
**Notes**: The author acknowledges that "we can probably never learn the details of specific ceremonies or ritual practices" (p. 416), but argues that the broad structural principles are archaeologically visible. The persistence of directional and dualistic organization across linguistically diverse Pueblo groups supports deep time-depth for these concepts.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (ethnographic analogy; difficult to verify specific cosmological meanings for prehistoric architecture)
**Assessment**: The claim that Chacoan architecture encoded cosmological meaning is plausible but difficult to test archaeologically. For the signaling model, the key implication is that architectural signals carried conventionalized meaning legible to a broad audience sharing Puebloan cosmological frameworks.

### Claim 6

**Statement**: Late Bonito builders used architecture to reference the Classic Bonito past through alignments and road connections, constituting deliberate construction of "social memory." Chacoan roads and alignments "very likely carried substantial symbolic meaning" and some served as "time bridges" connecting non-contemporaneous sites as symbolic connections of the present with the past.
**Page**: pp. 424-425
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Lekson 1999; Roney 1992; Sofaer et al. 1989; Stein and Lekson 1992; Fowler and Stein 1992:116-118; Van Dyke 2003
**Depends on**: Survey data on road segments and alignments
**Notes**: A road segment leads north from Tsin Kletsin toward Pueblo Alto, stopping at the north edge of South Mesa. New Alto was sited at 357 degrees east of true north from Tsin Kletsin (not due north), creating a new meridian "based on the old, but slightly different" that traverses Casa Rinconada and bisects the space between Pueblo Bonito and Pueblo Alto.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (alignment data are objective, but interpretation of "social memory" requires ethnographic inference)
**Assessment**: The architectural referencing behavior is relevant to signal depreciation in the costly signaling framework. When existing signals lose their communicative force (due to environmental or social change), new signals that reference old ones represent an attempt to restore signal credibility at reduced cost. This maps onto a depreciation mechanism where signaling effectiveness decays and must be renewed.

### Claim 7

**Statement**: Late Bonito great houses lack the enclosed plazas, great kivas, and domestic refuse deposits that characterized Classic Bonito great houses. Late Bonito structures "do not have enclosed plazas, associated great kivas, or (with the exception of Tsin Kletsin) associated refuse." The lack of domestic occupation evidence "might as easily be construed to indicate the buildings were not meant to be used at all."
**Page**: pp. 422-423 (citing Windes 2003:26-28)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Windes 2003:26-28; Lekson 1986:269-272; Vivian 1990:375-376
**Depends on**: Excavation and survey data
**Notes**: Lekson argued McElmo structures were for administration and storage (1986:269-272). Vivian (1990:375-376) refuted this interpretation point by point, arguing they were habitations. Van Dyke suggests they "were meant to convey the ideas inherent in the concept of 'great house'" (p. 423) regardless of actual use.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (absence of evidence from multiple excavated sites)
**Assessment**: If Late Bonito great houses were "not meant to be used" in a domestic sense, they may have functioned primarily as signals rather than as habitation or storage. This supports the interpretation that their construction cost was the signal itself, consistent with Trigger's (1990) energy framework.

### Claim 8

**Statement**: By the early 1100s, Chaco was in competition with Aztec for regional prominence. Construction began at Salmon Ruin in A.D. 1088 and at Aztec during the same decade. By the late 1100s, Aztec had become an important center of the post-Chacoan world. "If Chaco was in competition with Aztec during the early 1100s, Aztec won."
**Page**: p. 426
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Stein and McKenna 1988; Judge 1989:247; Lekson 1999:140-141; Sebastian 1992:135
**Depends on**: Tree-ring dating of Salmon Ruin and Aztec constructions
**Notes**: Some people from the San Juan Basin had shifted allegiance to the Aztec area. The construction of McElmo-style structures in Chaco coincided temporally with construction at Aztec, suggesting the building programs were competitive responses to one another.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (timing correlation does not demonstrate direct competition)
**Assessment**: The Chaco-Aztec competition is directly relevant to the signaling model. If multiple centers are competing for regional followers, monument construction functions as intergroup signaling (Layer 2) where the audience includes potential followers choosing between competing centers. The "loss" to Aztec represents a case where one signaler's investment exceeded another's, with the lower-investment signaler losing audience share.

### Claim 9

**Statement**: The Late Bonito building program was undertaken by "a leadership that had less labor at its disposal" than Classic Bonito leaders. During the Classic Bonito phase, basin residents visited periodically to attend ritual events. By the early 1100s, many people throughout the San Juan Basin had shifted allegiance to Aztec. The Late Bonito building program was an attempt to "generate renewed interest in Chaco as a center place and to restore confidence in the rituals that took place there."
**Page**: pp. 423, 426
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Sebastian 1992:135; Lekson 1999; Wills 2000
**Depends on**: Population estimates and construction labor requirements
**Notes**: The author frames this as a crisis of legitimacy: environmental and social pressures had "shaken confidence in the Chacoan ritual order" (p. 426).

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (inference from indirect evidence)
**Assessment**: This claim is central to the signaling model. Construction investment during a period of declining labor availability and legitimacy represents an attempt to restore signal credibility. The model predicts that signaling investment should increase (or at least be maintained) when the signaler faces competitive pressure, which is precisely what Van Dyke describes for the Late Bonito phase.

### Claim 10

**Statement**: A sharp downturn in agricultural production during the 1090s, together with a decade of extremely low corn production, may have "shaken the faith of the populace in their ritual leadership." Near the end of the Classic Bonito phase, "burial evidence indicates material and nutritional differences between great house and small site inhabitants were at their most extreme."
**Page**: p. 426
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Sebastian 1992:135; Akins 1986, 2003
**Depends on**: Burial analysis from Akins; paleoclimate data
**Notes**: This connects environmental stress to loss of confidence in ritual leaders, providing a mechanism for the legitimacy crisis that prompted Late Bonito construction.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (the burial evidence is empirical; the causal connection between drought and legitimacy loss is inferred)
**Assessment**: The widening inequality during environmental stress is consistent with a signaling model prediction: when environmental conditions worsen, high-quality signalers should increase investment to maintain their signaling advantage, while the gap between signaler and non-signaler widens. The legitimacy crisis mechanism is compatible with but distinct from the model's cost-benefit framework.

### Claim 11

**Statement**: Great house construction in central Chaco Canyon was initiated during the mid-A.D. 800s at three locations: Una Vida, Pueblo Bonito, and Penasco Blanco. Construction escalated dramatically during the Classic Bonito Phase (A.D. 1020-1100), when major additions were made to these three structures plus four new great houses (Hungo Pavi, Chetro Ketl, Pueblo Alto, Pueblo del Arroyo). Twelve standing great houses in Chaco Canyon represent three centuries of construction and social change.
**Page**: p. 415
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Windes 2003; Windes and Ford 1992, 1996; Lekson 1986
**Depends on**: Dendrochronological dating
**Notes**: By the Classic Bonito phase, "over a hundred outliers (Bonito style architecture surrounded by a community of small sites) are found beyond Chaco Canyon" (p. 415; citing Fowler et al. 1987; Kantner and Mahoney 2000; Marshall et al. 1979; Marshall and Sofaer 1988; Powers et al. 1983).

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: These chronological data provide the construction timeline necessary for modeling signaling investment over time. The escalation during Classic Bonito and the subsequent Late Bonito burst are key temporal patterns for the model.

### Claim 12

**Statement**: The building program "failed," lasting only about a generation before Chaco never regained its former regional importance as a ritual center. "The flurry of new construction activity lasted only a generation, perhaps less. In fact, some buildings were never completed," including Roberts Small Pueblo east of Pueblo Bonito and Hillside Ruin east of Pueblo Bonito.
**Page**: p. 427
**Type**: empirical/interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Roberts 1926-27; Stein et al. 2003:55-56; Windes 2003:31
**Depends on**: Excavation and survey data showing incomplete construction
**Notes**: The failure of the Late Bonito building program directly illustrates a signaling model prediction: when the cost-benefit ratio becomes unfavorable (insufficient labor, declining audience, persistent drought), signaling investment ceases.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (incomplete buildings are direct physical evidence)
**Assessment**: Incomplete buildings are particularly telling for the signaling model. The abandonment of construction mid-project suggests the cost-benefit threshold was crossed abruptly, consistent with Barclay et al.'s (2021) prediction that signaling systems can collapse in a single interaction round.

---

## Summary Assessment

Van Dyke 2004 provides critical evidence for the Late Bonito phase (A.D. 1100-1140) that is directly relevant to the signaling model. The paper documents: (1) a burst of construction investment during a period of environmental and social crisis; (2) deliberate architectural strategies to maximize visual impact at reduced cost (illusory tricks, standardized McElmo template); (3) spatial patterning of new great houses that references and reformulates Classic Bonito cosmological themes; (4) competition between Chaco and Aztec for regional followers; and (5) the ultimate failure of the building program within a generation. These observations map onto specific model predictions about signal maintenance during declining returns, signal depreciation and renewal, and the conditions for signaling system collapse. The paper's "social memory" framework is compatible with the signaling interpretation, where architectural references to the past function as a mechanism for restoring signal credibility.
