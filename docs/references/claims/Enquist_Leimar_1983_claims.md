## Claims

### Claim 1: Utility of fighting expressed as U = pV - C
"For most game theory models of fighting behaviour developed the utility (U) for an animal of using a given strategy in a contest situation can be written as U = pV - C where p is the probability of winning, V the value of the resource, and C the cost of fighting." (p. 188)

**Relevance**: Foundational equation for modeling contest outcomes. In the Chaco model, V maps to territory/prestige value, C to conflict costs, and p to probability of prevailing. Monument signals modify p by providing pre-contest information about group quality.

---

### Claim 2: Resource value is subjective and varies among contestants
"The value that the resource represents to an animal as subjective resource value, to indicate that resource value depends on internal factors like the animal's physiological state and information about the environment, and may thus vary among contestants." (p. 188)

**Relevance**: Establishes that the stakes of a contest are not symmetric. In the Chaco framework, the subjective value of contested resources (agricultural land, trade access, ritual authority) would differ across groups based on their current state and needs, making assessment of both opponent quality and opponent motivation important.

---

### Claim 3: ESS prescribes costlier strategies as resource value increases
"A common, although not universal, finding for games with variation in resource value is that an ESS will prescribe more costly strategies when resource value increases. The increased cost may result from increased persistence of the animal and/or from the use of more costly behaviour patterns." (p. 190)

**Relevance**: The general prediction that contest investment scales with resource value. For Chaco groups, this predicts that as resources become more valuable (e.g., during drought when productive land is scarce), groups should invest more heavily in signals of competitive quality, consistent with the prediction of increased construction during periods of environmental stress.

---

### Claim 4: Sequential assessment games proceed through information acquisition
"A sequential assessment game models a contest that proceeds in steps, each step entailing a certain cost or risk for a contestant. Contestants vary in their ability to inflict and avoid costs during a fight, i.e. in their fighting ability. Prior to an interaction, a pair of competing individuals have poor information about their relative fighting ability, but during the interaction they gain more information and use this information to decide whether to give up or to continue fighting." (p. 191)

**Relevance**: Core mechanism for Layer 2. The sequential assessment model provides the formal basis for how groups acquire information about each other through costly interactions. Monument signals function as pre-contest information: they allow groups to assess relative quality before engaging in costly direct interaction, reducing the number of assessment steps required.

---

### Claim 5: Relative fighting ability theta is estimated through noisy observations
"We choose that variable as theta = theta_AB = ln(c_B/c_A). Theta is referred to as the relative fighting ability, and it has the property that theta_AB = -theta_BA. Prior to a fight, contestants only have limited information about the relative fighting ability and this information can be expressed as a prior distribution, beta(theta), of the relative fighting ability." (p. 192)

**Relevance**: The parameter theta formalizes relative quality difference between contestants. In the Chaco model, monument investment provides information about this parameter: a group with a larger great house signals higher quality, allowing potential opponents to update their prior estimate of theta. The noise parameter sigma determines how quickly theta is estimated during sequential assessment.

---

### Claim 6: Assessment noise sigma determines fight duration and cost
"At each step of the fight, A assesses theta_AB and B assesses theta_BA. There is some inaccuracy in these assessments, and the errors of observation for A and B are assumed to be independent and normally distributed with mean zero and standard deviation sigma." (p. 192)

**Relevance**: The observation error sigma is the key parameter linking monument signals to conflict reduction. In the Chaco model, monument construction reduces sigma (assessment noise) by providing clear, observable information about group quality. Lower sigma means fewer costly assessment rounds are needed, reducing the expected cost of intergroup interaction.

---

### Claim 7: Local strategy as switching line determining persistence
"A local strategy S is given by a level (switching point) S_n for each n; if x_n goes below S_n the player gives up at step n." (p. 192)

**Relevance**: The switching line concept formalizes the decision to persist or withdraw from a contest. Monument signals shift the switching lines: when a group observes a rival's large great house, the switching line shifts such that the group is more likely to withdraw before engaging in costly conflict, analogous to the model's prediction that signal observability reduces conflict probability.

---

### Claim 8: Higher-resource-value contestants adopt more persistent strategies
"Animals with higher subjective resource value are more persistent." (p. 193, describing Fig. 1 ESS results)

**Relevance**: Groups with more at stake fight harder. In the Chaco context, groups occupying prime agricultural land or controlling important trade routes would be predicted to invest more in defensive signaling (larger great houses), consistent with the observation that the largest great houses are in Chaco Canyon, the regional center with the highest combined resource value.

---

### Claim 9: Both fight duration and probability of winning increase with resource value
"Considering contests of individuals with a given V against the population, the probability of winning will increase with V. Furthermore, both fight duration and cost increases when an individual's and/or an opponent's V increases." (p. 194)

**Relevance**: The joint increase in fight duration and win probability with resource value means that contests between high-value groups are both more intense and more decisive. This predicts that interactions between major great house communities (e.g., Pueblo Bonito vs. Chetro Ketl) should have been more formalized and costly than interactions between small sites, consistent with the scale of investment in the largest great houses.

---

### Claim 10: Owner-intruder information asymmetry determines contest outcomes
"The owner knows the exact value of the resource, whereas the intruder does not have this information... The owner will become more persistent as the value of the resource increases. This has the effect that the intruder, not having perfect information about the resource, tends to win the 'wrong' fights." (pp. 195-196)

**Relevance**: The information asymmetry model maps onto the Chaco situation where established great house communities (owners) have better information about local resources than newcomers or raiders (intruders). Monument construction communicates the owner's assessment of resource value to potential intruders, reducing the intruder's uncertainty and lowering the probability of costly mismatched contests.

---

### Claim 11: Owner's probability of winning increases with resource value in information-asymmetric contests
"The owner's probability of winning will increase with resource value... the owner will win no more than 55% of the interactions, but the overall utility for an owner is 0.26 (ranging from 0.02 for V = 0.5 to 0.57 for V = 1.5) compared to only 0.10 for intruders." (p. 196)

**Relevance**: Even modest information advantages translate into significant utility differences. For Chaco groups, monument construction serves as an honest signal of resource value, conferring a defensive advantage by communicating the group's commitment to defense. The 0.26 vs. 0.10 utility differential demonstrates that information-based advantages compound across many interactions.

---

### Claim 12: Decreasing assessment noise makes fight outcomes more determined by relative ability
"If the cost of acquiring information about relative fighting ability is decreased, either by decreasing sigma or c, the ESS changes so that the switching lines come closer together in the causal factor space. The effect of this will be that the outcomes of fights will be more determined by relative fighting ability than by asymmetries in subjective resource value." (p. 195)

**Relevance**: When assessment is cheap and accurate (low sigma), outcomes depend on actual quality rather than motivation. Monument signals, by reducing assessment noise, make contest outcomes more predictable and more dependent on actual group quality, which is the mechanism by which signaling reduces costly conflict: groups can assess relative quality without fighting.

---

### Claim 13: Empirical data across 18 species support model predictions
Table 1 (p. 199) compiles empirical data from 18 species showing that when resource value increases: fight duration increases (+), offensive behavior increases (+), and probability of winning for the higher-value contestant increases (+). Species include hermit crabs, spider crabs, crayfish, funnel-web spiders, digger wasps, dung flies, red-spotted newts, iguanas, fulmars, bald eagles, shrews, red deer, house cats, and chimpanzees.

**Relevance**: The broad empirical support across taxa confirms that resource value modulates fighting investment as a general biological principle, not a taxon-specific phenomenon. The cross-species generality strengthens the argument that the same logic applies to human intergroup contests, where group-level "resource value" encompasses territory, trade networks, and social status.

---

### Claim 14: Contests between closely matched opponents are longest and costliest
"For contests where an individual, A, has a greater V than an opponent, B, the longest contests will be those where B is slightly stronger, and contests won by B will tend to be longer than those won by A." (pp. 194-195)

**Relevance**: Close matches produce the most costly interactions. This predicts that the most intense and visible intergroup tensions at Chaco should have occurred between communities of similar size and investment, not between clearly mismatched ones. The prediction is consistent with the observation that the largest great houses are clustered in the canyon core, where matched communities would have faced the highest assessment costs, and with the "Pax Chaco" observation that open conflict was rare where great house signaling was densest.

---

### Claim 15: Increasing resource value variance has similar effect to increasing assessment noise
"Increasing the range of variation of subjective resource value in the population or decreasing the range of variation of fighting ability has a similar effect on the ESS, making the outcomes of fights more determined by asymmetry in subjective resource value than asymmetry in fighting ability." (p. 195)

**Relevance**: When resource variance is high (as during periods of drought with patchy rainfall), contest outcomes become more dependent on how much each party values the resource rather than on actual competitive ability. This creates conditions where signaling (which communicates both quality and motivation) becomes especially important for avoiding costly misassessment.

## Summary Assessment

This paper extends the sequential assessment model from the 1983 paper to incorporate resource value variation. It provides: (1) the formal utility equation U = pV - C that underlies the Layer 2 conflict probability function; (2) the sequential assessment mechanism where contestants gain information about relative quality through costly interaction rounds; (3) the role of assessment noise (sigma) in determining contest duration and cost; (4) predictions about how resource value asymmetries interact with fighting ability in determining outcomes; and (5) empirical validation across 18 species. The model provides the formal theoretical foundation for the claim that monument signaling reduces intergroup conflict by providing pre-contest information about relative group quality, lowering assessment noise (sigma) and thereby reducing the expected number and cost of assessment steps.
