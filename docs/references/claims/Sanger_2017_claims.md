## Relevance Assessment

This paper addresses hunter-gatherer storage practices in the Late Archaic southeastern United States (ca. 4800-3200 cal B.P.) and is **not directly about Chaco Canyon or the American Southwest**. However, several claims are relevant to the project's theoretical framework regarding risk-buffering strategies, the relationship between storage and social complexity, and the materialization of surplus management in archaeological contexts.

---

## Claims

### Claim 1

**Statement**: Hunter-gatherer food storage is considered a "risk reducing mechanism" designed to offset seasonal downturns in resource availability. In areas where critical food resources are storable and subject to high levels of seasonal availability, hunter-gatherers focus on intensified gathering and stockpiling to survive lean times.
**Page**: p. 52
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: Binford 1980; Keeley 1988; Kelly 1995; Testart 1982; Weissner 1982
**Depends on**: Ethnographic and cross-cultural analyses
**Notes**: Establishes the canonical link between storage and risk buffering. Directly relevant to the model's conceptualization of storage as one of four buffering mechanisms (following Halstead & O'Shea 1989).

**Evidence strength**: Strong (well-established framework)
**Assessment**: Confirms the theoretical baseline: storage is a risk-buffering response to temporal variability in food availability. At Chaco, the equivalent mechanism operates through great house rooms functioning as communal storage facilities.

---

### Claim 2

**Statement**: In conjunction with reduced mobility, the presence of stored foods and the ability to amass a surplus may relate to the emergence of elites, class structures, specialization, and inheritability of wealth or status. Population size and density are also thought to increase when stored foods are available.
**Page**: p. 52
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: Testart 1988; Wesson 1999; Keeley 1988; Kuijt 2008
**Depends on**: Cross-cultural analysis
**Notes**: Describes the standard model linking storage to social complexity. Relevant as a comparison case: the Late Archaic shell ring communities may have experienced similar dynamics to early Chaco communities, where surplus management capabilities enabled social differentiation.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (widely cited relationships)
**Assessment**: The storage-to-complexity pathway described here parallels the Chaco trajectory. The key difference is that Chaco developed monument-based signaling (great houses) as the institutionalized form of surplus management, while the shell ring communities used subterranean pit storage.

---

### Claim 3

**Statement**: The societal ramifications of storage practices typically correlate with the scale and centralization of the practice. Large-scale, centralized storage practices often facilitate the development of social complexity and inequality, while more moderate storage practices have a relatively minor impact as they provide for occasional dietary shortfalls yet are too dispersed to be effective tools for elite control.
**Page**: p. 52
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: Testart 1982; Wesson 1999
**Depends on**: Cross-cultural analysis
**Notes**: The scale dependence of storage's social effects is relevant to the model's multi-scale structure: household-level storage buffers individual shortfalls (low social impact), while community-level storage in great house rooms provides a platform for social differentiation (high social impact).

**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: Supports the model's distinction between individual-level and group-level buffering mechanisms. Centralized storage at Chaco great houses would have greater social ramifications than dispersed household storage, consistent with the emergence of signaling dynamics around communal surplus management.

---

### Claim 4

**Statement**: Small-scale and dispersed caching of foods may actually reduce the threat of emergent elitism and instead allow increased levels of autonomy, as families and individuals could provide for themselves with little need or desire for centralized authority.
**Page**: p. 53
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: Ingold 1983; Morgan 2012
**Depends on**: Theoretical argument
**Notes**: The inverse relationship between dispersed storage and elite emergence is relevant as a counterfactual: if Chaco communities had relied on dispersed household storage rather than centralized great house facilities, the conditions for elite signaling would not have developed.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: Provides theoretical contrast for understanding why the Chaco system developed signaling dynamics: centralized surplus management at great houses, rather than dispersed household storage, created the conditions for elite differentiation and competitive signaling.

---

### Claim 5

**Statement**: Excavations at two Late Archaic shell rings on St. Catherines Island, Georgia, revealed dozens of subterranean features interpreted as storage and processing pits for tree nuts. The St. Catherines Shell Ring contained approximately 32 pits within 50 m2 of excavation, with many overlapping. Pits varied between 0.5 and 1.5 m wide and 0.3 and 1.5 m deep.
**Page**: pp. 54-55
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Excavation data from 2006-2011 field seasons
**Depends on**: Primary archaeological data
**Notes**: Empirical data on storage facility dimensions and density. Not directly relevant to Chaco, but provides comparative data on the material correlates of large-scale food storage in non-agricultural societies.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (primary excavation data)
**Assessment**: Comparative interest only. The scale of storage investment at the shell rings (hundreds of pits, each requiring significant labor to excavate) demonstrates that non-agricultural societies can invest substantially in storage infrastructure.

---

### Claim 6

**Statement**: Extrapolations suggest the St. Catherines Shell Ring could encircle roughly 600 pits, with more than 180,000 liters of storage potential. The McQueen Shell Ring could contain almost 500 pits, with roughly 100,000 liters of storage potential.
**Page**: p. 67 (Table 2)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Volumetric calculations using 3D Analyst extension for ArcGIS 10.2; extrapolation from excavated sample
**Depends on**: Excavated pit dimensions, site area measurements
**Notes**: Quantified storage capacity estimates. The scale is impressive for a non-agricultural community, demonstrating that hunter-gatherer societies can organize large-scale food storage.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (extrapolated from sample; 5% and 3% area tested)
**Assessment**: Comparative interest. The quantification methodology (volumetric analysis, caloric capacity estimation) provides a template for quantifying storage at Chaco great houses if similar analysis is undertaken.

---

### Claim 7

**Statement**: The potential caloric value of the largest storage pits, if filled with acorns at 70% capacity, ranges from approximately 325 to 1,930 kcal (acorns) or 85 to 810 kcal (hickory nuts), representing between 35 and 790 subsistence days for a single individual.
**Page**: p. 65 (Table 1)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Caloric values from Barlow and Heck 2002, Semon 2008, Bettinger et al. 1997; subsistence rate from Kelly 1995:102 (2,440 kcal/day)
**Depends on**: Experimental and ethnographic caloric values
**Notes**: Demonstrates the method of translating storage volume to subsistence potential. Could be applied to estimate storage capacity in Chaco great house rooms.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (multiple assumptions about fill rate, resource type)
**Assessment**: Methodological interest. The approach of converting storage volume to caloric capacity and then to subsistence days provides a quantitative framework applicable to Chaco.

### Claim 8

**Statement**: The use of below-ground storage, rather than above-ground facilities, demands explanation. Because above-ground facilities are generally better at preserving foods and are easier to construct, maintain, and access, archaeologists generally view underground food storage as arising from a need to either hide resources or keep track of access.
**Page**: p. 68
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: DeBoer 1988; Wills and Windes 1989
**Depends on**: Cross-cultural analysis
**Notes**: The reference to Wills and Windes 1989, which examines population aggregation and dispersal during the Basketmaker III period in Chaco Canyon, directly connects this storage discussion to the Chaco context.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: The contrast between below-ground (hidden) and above-ground (visible) storage is relevant to the signaling framework. At Chaco, the shift to above-ground, monumental storage rooms in great houses may represent a transition from competitive exclusion (hiding resources) to costly signaling (displaying surplus management capacity).

---

### Claim 9

**Statement**: Stored foods could be consumed during lean seasons, thereby allowing continuous occupation of the rings throughout the year. As foods amassed, perhaps beyond the annual needs of the community, they could be redeployed as resources for larger gatherings. Ethnographic studies show that mast, particularly acorns, can be utilized as foods for large-scale feasts when accumulated over multiple years and deployed using group labor.
**Page**: p. 68
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: McCarthy 1993
**Depends on**: Ethnographic analogy
**Notes**: The pathway from surplus storage to feasting and communal gatherings parallels the proposed function of Chaco great houses as feasting/ceremonial venues supported by stored surplus. The multi-year accumulation pattern is relevant to the signaling model's temporal dynamics.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (ethnographic analogy)
**Assessment**: The surplus-to-feasting pathway provides a cross-cultural parallel for interpreting Chaco great kiva ceremonies and the evidence for cacao consumption (Crown 2018). Surplus management enabling communal gatherings could serve both risk-buffering and signaling functions simultaneously.

---

### Claim 10

**Statement**: The adoption of mast as a significant resource can have dramatic effects on interpersonal relations at a variety of scales, particularly when foods are amassed and stored at significant rates. Studies show that the adoption of mast as a significant resource is often linked to changing settlement and mobility strategies, understandings of group membership, and the creation of more formalized social boundaries.
**Page**: pp. 52, 67
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: Bettinger 2015
**Depends on**: Cross-cultural comparison
**Notes**: Describes how a shift in subsistence strategy (intensified mast exploitation) can trigger social reorganization. The parallel at Chaco is the shift to intensive maize agriculture in a marginal environment, which similarly required new forms of social organization including inter-community cooperation networks.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: The general principle, that a change in the intensity and mode of resource exploitation drives social reorganization, is relevant to understanding the emergence of the Chaco system. The specific pathway differs (mast vs. maize), but the structural dynamics (resource intensification leading to storage, aggregation, and social differentiation) are parallel.
