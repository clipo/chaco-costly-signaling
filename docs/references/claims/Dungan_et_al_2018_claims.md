## Claims

### Claim 1

**Statement**: Great houses were often placed to take advantage of highly visible locations within their local landscapes, while great kivas were not. Approximately half of the 269 great houses fall into the eighth visibility decile or higher, whereas great kivas show a much more even distribution across moderate and lower visibility deciles.
**Page**: pp. 913-914
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Total viewshed analysis of 430 sites (269 great houses, 161 great kivas) using 30m ASTER DEM; Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests against uniform background distribution
**Depends on**: Accuracy of site location data from Chaco Social Networks database (Peeples et al. 2016); DEM resolution; 4km study area radius assumption
**Notes**: The K-S test confirms that great house distribution differs significantly from the background landscape distribution. The great kiva distribution also differs from uniform but is less skewed toward high visibility.
**Evidence strength**: Strong

### Claim 2

**Statement**: The K-S test shows that the great house distribution within visibility deciles differs significantly from the uniform distribution of visibility deciles on the landscape, both when great houses are considered as a single group and when grouped by century of construction (Table 1, Figure 7).
**Page**: p. 914
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: K-S statistics: all great houses Max D = 0.24 (significant at alpha = 0.001); AD 1000-1100 (n = 160) Max D = 0.24; before AD 900 (n = 32) Max D = 0.31
**Depends on**: Correct assignment of construction dates; DEM accuracy
**Notes**: The pattern is strongest for great houses built before AD 900 (Max D = 0.31) and those built AD 1100-1200 (Max D = 0.28), though both periods have smaller sample sizes.
**Evidence strength**: Strong

### Claim 3

**Statement**: The distribution of great houses and great kivas within visibility deciles differs significantly from each other (K-S Max D = 0.16, significant at alpha = 0.001, Table 1). Great houses show a strong preference for high-visibility locations, while great kivas do not.
**Page**: p. 914
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: K-S test comparing great house vs. great kiva distributions
**Depends on**: Same as Claim 1
**Notes**: This distinction between the two types of monumental architecture is the paper's most important finding for the signaling framework.
**Evidence strength**: Strong

### Claim 4

**Statement**: Great house visibility shows relatively little geographic variation across the study area, with the preference for higher visibility deciles present in both Chaco Canyon/central San Juan Basin and at the edges of the study area. Two exceptions are great houses in southeastern Utah and near Lobo Mesa/Red Mesa Valley.
**Page**: pp. 914, 918
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Table 2; Figure 8 (regional visibility decile maps)
**Depends on**: Sufficient sample sizes by region
**Notes**: The geographic consistency suggests that the visibility-seeking behavior was a general property of great house construction, not specific to the canyon core.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate

### Claim 5

**Statement**: Around Lobo Mesa, a subregion stands out as having a higher proportion of great houses in less visible locations, characterized by the early use of great houses in the Red Mesa Valley. Unlike later great houses, these early examples were not frequently placed in high-visibility locations, though later ones (such as the Andrews great house) do appear in high-visibility settings.
**Page**: p. 918
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Figure 8; Van Dyke (2000) on Red Mesa Valley
**Depends on**: Accurate dating of early vs. late great houses in the subregion
**Notes**: This temporal pattern suggests that the emphasis on visibility increased over time, at least in some areas.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate

### Claim 6

**Statement**: Great houses are inherently more visible from a distance than great kivas due to their physical properties. Great houses rise above the surrounding landscape, while great kivas are single, usually roofed rooms whose monumentality was primarily experienced from within. The "legibility" of great houses was frequently enhanced through the choice of setting.
**Page**: pp. 916, 918
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Physical characteristics of both structure types; Moore (1996) on legibility; visibility analysis results
**Depends on**: Empirical findings from Claims 1-3
**Notes**: The distinction between external visibility (great houses) and internal experience (great kivas) maps onto different signaling functions: great houses as signals to distant audiences, great kivas as venues for proximate social interaction.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate

### Claim 7

**Statement**: The concern with "being seen" is a fundamental part of Chaco-style architecture. The overbuilding that defines great houses was frequently enhanced by the selection of highly visible building sites, and great houses were intended to be seen by individuals across the wider landscape rather than only by viewers in the immediate vicinity.
**Page**: p. 918
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Results of the total viewshed analysis; physical properties of great houses; comparison with great kivas
**Depends on**: All empirical claims above
**Notes**: This is the paper's central interpretive conclusion. The phrase "being seen" directly connects to the signaling framework: the visibility of the signal is a precondition for signal function.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate

### Claim 8

**Statement**: The total viewshed method used here generates viewsheds approximately 2000 times faster than legacy ArcGIS tools by using GPU computing (custom C++ algorithm running on NVIDIA GPU). The 5-cell spacing approximation produces negligible differences from true total viewsheds (maximum difference approximately 0.02, 99% of differences below 0.015).
**Page**: pp. 908-909
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Benchmarking against true total viewsheds for 15 study areas (Figure 2)
**Depends on**: Validity of the approximation
**Notes**: This methodological advance makes total viewshed analysis practical for large study areas, but the method's core contribution to the project is the empirical finding, not the computational technique.
**Evidence strength**: Strong

### Claim 9

**Statement**: The complete dataset contains point locations for 430 sites, including 269 great houses (or sites recorded as great houses) and 161 non-great house sites with great kivas, derived from the Chaco Social Networks database (Peeples et al. 2016) and the Chaco Research Archive.
**Page**: p. 910
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Peeples et al. 2016; Chaco Research Archive; Van Dyke et al. 2016b; Mills et al. 2018
**Depends on**: Accuracy of site classification and location data
**Notes**: The sample includes all known great houses and great kivas across the Chaco World, making this the most comprehensive viewshed analysis of Chaco architecture to date.
**Evidence strength**: Strong

### Claim 10

**Statement**: Great kiva visibility in the Middle San Juan may show an unusual emphasis on high visibility, with approximately half of the great kivas falling into the two uppermost visibility deciles, compared to the broader pattern of great kivas being placed without strong concern for visibility.
**Page**: p. 916
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Table 3; Figure 9
**Depends on**: Small sample size for Middle San Juan (n = 19)
**Notes**: If confirmed with larger samples, this subregional variation might indicate different functions for great kivas in different parts of the Chaco World.
**Evidence strength**: Weak

### Claim 11

**Statement**: The visibility pattern of great houses persists through time and is certainly evident by the eleventh-century AD peak of great house construction. The pattern is most obvious for great houses built after ca. AD 1000, which may partly reflect the larger sample size for this period (n = 160).
**Page**: p. 914
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Figure 5 (histograms by construction date); Table 1 (K-S tests by period)
**Depends on**: Construction date assignments
**Notes**: The earlier periods (before AD 900, n = 32; AD 900-1000, n = 30) also show preferences for higher deciles, suggesting the pattern was present from the beginning of great house construction.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate

## Summary Assessment

Dungan et al. 2018 provides the first systematic, large-scale test of whether great houses were intentionally placed to be visible across local landscapes. The finding that great houses were consistently placed in high-visibility locations while great kivas were not is directly relevant to the costly signaling model: signals must be observable to function, and great houses appear to have been engineered for maximum observability. The distinction between external visibility (great houses as landscape signals) and internal experience (great kivas as ceremonial venues) supports the model's differentiation between monument-based intergroup signaling (Layer 2) and the social mechanisms operating within communities. The geographic consistency of the visibility pattern across the Chaco World argues against local explanations and supports a system-wide signaling logic.
