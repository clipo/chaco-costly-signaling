# Claims Extraction: Scarborough et al. 2018

**Source:** Scarborough, Vernon L., Samantha G. Fladd, Nicholas P. Dunning, Stephen Plog, Lewis A. Owen, Christopher Carr, Kenneth B. Tankersley, Jon-Paul McCool, Adam S. Watson, Elizabeth A. Haussner, Brooke Crowley, Katelyn J. Bishop, David L. Lentz, and R. Gwinn Vivian. 2018. Water Uncertainty, Ritual Predictability and Agricultural Canals at Chaco Canyon, New Mexico. *Antiquity* 92(364):870-889.

**DOI:** 10.15184/aqy.2018.114

---

## Claim 1

- **Statement:** Using optically stimulated luminescence (OSL) dating supplemented with AMS radiocarbon dates, the authors located Bonito Phase canal features at the far west end of the canyon and definitively dated one canal network to Chaco's Bonito Phase occupation (AD 800-1130).
- **Page:** 872
- **Type:** Empirical/Methodological
- **Evidence cited by author:** OSL dating of canal sediments; AMS radiocarbon dates
- **Depends on:** OSL and AMS dating methods
- **Notes:** First definitive dating of a Chaco canal system. Prior canal dating was uncertain.
- **Evidence strength:** Strong; multiple independent dating methods converge.
- **Assessment:** Resolves a long-standing question about whether canals were contemporaneous with great house occupation.

---

## Claim 2

- **Statement:** The San Juan Basin has limited annual precipitation of about 200 mm, with yearly fluctuations common and spatial distribution varying greatly. Except for 15 years, the period from AD 990-1115 received higher than average precipitation; this was followed by a significant drought often assumed to be associated with canyon depopulation around AD 1130.
- **Page:** 872
- **Type:** Empirical
- **Evidence cited by author:** Sebastian 1992; Dean and Funkhouser 2002; Vivian et al. 2006
- **Depends on:** Dendroclimatological reconstructions
- **Notes:** Quantifies the precipitation context: 200mm annual average; AD 990-1115 was generally wetter than average.
- **Evidence strength:** Strong; based on well-established tree-ring chronologies.
- **Assessment:** Standard paleoclimate characterization for Chaco.

## Claim 3

- **Statement:** Water-flow coordination required flexible and quick responses from the local population. Pueblo social and political organisation, both past and present, is heavily integrated with ritual practices that help structure responses to the uncertain environment, including organising the labour necessary to maintain water-management infrastructure.
- **Page:** 872
- **Type:** Interpretive
- **Evidence cited by author:** General ethnographic analogy with modern Pueblo societies
- **Depends on:** Ethnohistoric record of Pueblo water management
- **Notes:** Links water management to ritual authority and labor mobilization.
- **Evidence strength:** Moderate; ethnographic analogy is plausible but the specific connection to Bonito Phase social organization is inferred.
- **Assessment:** Central interpretive framework of the paper. The connection between ritual authority and water management provides a mechanism for how environmental uncertainty could drive social complexity.

---

## Claim 4

- **Statement:** The canal system tapped multiple water sources over time. ED-XRF revealed two distinct signatures: the Escavada Wash and Chaco Canyon (the latter comprising the Chaco Wash and the North Mesa rincons). Strontium isotope analysis confirmed the diversion of waters from multiple independent sources.
- **Page:** 880
- **Type:** Empirical
- **Evidence cited by author:** ED-XRF and 87Sr/86Sr analysis of water and sediment samples (OSM 5, 6)
- **Depends on:** Geochemical analysis methods
- **Notes:** Demonstrates sophisticated water management involving multiple source diversions.
- **Evidence strength:** Strong; geochemical sourcing is an established method.
- **Assessment:** The multi-source strategy is consistent with risk-aversion behavior under environmental uncertainty.

---

## Claim 5

- **Statement:** An early canal (Op. C04) at the foot of the North Mesa, dated by OSL to AD 600+/-100, contains evidence of canal deposits dating to the Late Basketmaker period (AD 500-750). This makes the rincons the most probable source for early canals.
- **Page:** 881, 883
- **Type:** Empirical
- **Evidence cited by author:** OSL date from Op. C04; Vivian 1972 assessment
- **Depends on:** OSL dating
- **Notes:** Pushes the beginning of canal irrigation at Chaco back to the Basketmaker III period, well before great house construction.
- **Evidence strength:** Moderate; single OSL date with +/-100 year error.
- **Assessment:** If confirmed, this extends the water management tradition to pre-Bonito Phase, indicating agricultural infrastructure preceded monument construction.

---

## Claim 6

- **Statement:** The canal system at Ops. C01/C03 evolved through several phases: an initial smaller channel (OSL age AD 900+/-100), a larger constructed canal with successive berms (Unit C-4 yielded OSL AD 1000+/-100 and AMS AD 997-1150), and progressively narrowing channels indicating efforts to maintain flow as water availability dropped.
- **Page:** 876
- **Type:** Empirical
- **Evidence cited by author:** Stratigraphic analysis, OSL and AMS dating (OSM 3, 4)
- **Depends on:** Sedimentological interpretation and dating
- **Notes:** The narrowing of the canal over time and construction of berms indicate active management and adaptation to changing water conditions.
- **Evidence strength:** Strong; stratigraphic sequence is well-documented and independently dated.
- **Assessment:** Provides direct evidence of adaptive responses to environmental change during the Bonito Phase.

---

## Claim 7

- **Statement:** The canals were stacked or reused during the ninth and tenth centuries with their elevated, infilled courses possibly re-used during the historical Navajo period. A "highly complex set of risk-aversion strategies was in place that utilised water from multiple independent sources."
- **Page:** 884
- **Type:** Interpretive
- **Evidence cited by author:** Vivian 1990 (stone-slab-lined canal segments and diversion gates)
- **Depends on:** Stratigraphic and dating evidence from this study plus Vivian's earlier work
- **Notes:** The characterization as "risk-aversion" is directly relevant to the signaling model's environmental uncertainty framework.
- **Evidence strength:** Moderate; the "risk-aversion" interpretation is reasonable but other motivations are possible.
- **Assessment:** The multi-source, multi-period canal system is strong evidence of systematic responses to water uncertainty.

---

## Claim 8

- **Statement:** Quick social responses to the dynamic setting were necessary to harness multiple water sources through the canal system below Penasco Blanco, "probably drawing upon labour provided by occupants of the great houses and the numerous small sites throughout the canyon."
- **Page:** 884
- **Type:** Interpretive
- **Evidence cited by author:** General assessment based on labor requirements
- **Depends on:** Proximity of great houses and small sites to canal features
- **Notes:** Links canal maintenance to the same labor pool used for great house construction.
- **Evidence strength:** Weak; the specific labor organization is inferred, not documented.
- **Assessment:** Plausible inference but undemonstrated.

---

## Claim 9

- **Statement:** Ritual practice, economic access and political authority tied to moisture and fertility "almost certainly allowed for the deployment and management of a labour force that responded quickly to environmental change. The centralisation of leadership may, in part, have sustained the Chaco social system by establishing certainty in the face of unpredictable environmental factors."
- **Page:** 885
- **Type:** Interpretive
- **Evidence cited by author:** Parsons 1939; Ortiz 1969:106; ethnographic parallels with modern Pueblo ties between ritual leaders and water management
- **Depends on:** Archaeogenomic evidence for matrilineal dynasty (Kennett et al. 2017); ethnographic analogy
- **Notes:** Directly argues that ritual authority was the mechanism through which labor was mobilized to manage environmental uncertainty.
- **Evidence strength:** Moderate; the ethnographic parallels are strong but the specific application to Chaco is inferred.
- **Assessment:** Consistent with the signaling model's Layer 3, where signaling of ritual/political authority functions to organize cooperation networks for crisis response.

---

## Claim 10

- **Statement:** Material assemblages within or near the Pueblo Bonito burial cluster include turquoise, frog/tadpole effigies, and other materials that are "ethnographically documented symbols of moisture and fertility throughout the Southwest."
- **Page:** 885
- **Type:** Empirical/Interpretive
- **Evidence cited by author:** Neitzel 2003; Crown and Hurst 2009; Watson et al. 2015; Plog 2003, 2011
- **Depends on:** Excavation records and ethnographic interpretation
- **Notes:** Links elite burial goods to water/fertility symbolism, connecting status signaling to environmental management.
- **Evidence strength:** Moderate; the ethnographic connection to moisture/fertility symbolism is well-established; the specific application to Bonito Phase elites is interpretive.
- **Assessment:** Supports the interpretation that elite status at Chaco was partly grounded in perceived ability to manage environmental uncertainty.

---

## Claim 11

- **Statement:** Depositional changes within the canal system could occur "at the scale of minutes to hours," demonstrating the dynamism of the environmental setting and the need for rapid responses.
- **Page:** 872
- **Type:** Empirical
- **Evidence cited by author:** Friedman et al. 2014:626-627
- **Depends on:** Geomorphological analysis of arroyo processes
- **Notes:** The minute-to-hour timescale of depositional change underscores the unpredictability of the hydrological environment.
- **Evidence strength:** Strong; based on analysis of living tree rings in relation to deposited sediment.
- **Assessment:** Provides direct evidence for the environmental stochasticity parameter.
