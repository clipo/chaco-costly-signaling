## Claims

### Claim 1

**Statement**: Costly signaling theory applies not only to the rise of complex societies but also to political collapse and subsequent regeneration of complexity. Signaling behavior is "subject to historical events, such as political collapse."
**Page**: pp. 1-2
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Zahavi 1975; Smith and Bliege Bird 2000; Neiman 1997; Kantner and Vaughn 2012
**Depends on**: Acceptance of CST framework as applicable to complex societies
**Notes**: This is the paper's central theoretical contribution. Most CST applications in archaeology focus on the rise of complexity; Schwarz extends the framework to collapse and regeneration. Directly relevant to the Chaco project's collapse predictions (Layer 2/Layer 3 failure under sustained stress).

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (conceptual extension supported by case study evidence below, but the Maya case differs from Chaco in key respects)

---

### Claim 2

**Statement**: Two forms of cost modification explain signaling dynamics during and after political collapse: "cost shifting" (a decrement to the cost of signal production due to decreased ability to mobilize labor) and "cost masking" (the full expense for production of a costly signal is hidden or not paid for completely by the signaler).
**Page**: pp. 9-11
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Neiman 1997:271 (for cost shifting in monument cessation); Laporte 2002, Maynard Smith and Harper 2003 (for deception in signaling); Andres 2000 (for scavenging of previous productions)
**Depends on**: The continuum model of signaling cost (from handicap principle to deception)
**Notes**: Cost shifting maps onto the Chaco model's signal depreciation and maintenance costs: when elites can no longer mobilize labor, signal production shifts to a lower cost level. Cost masking (appropriating previous constructions) has parallels in Chaco where Late Bonito McElmo-style great houses used illusory architectural features (Van Dyke 2004).

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (concepts are well-defined but operational identification in the archaeological record requires inference)

---

### Claim 3

**Statement**: The collapse of a complex state, in terms of costly signaling, is "a decline in the marginal cost of costly signal production. This is a downward spike in the absolute costliness of signals. But it was not the complete failure, at least in the Maya case, of costly signaling, but a marked departure from the previous growth trajectory."
**Page**: pp. 23-24
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Neiman 1997; Tainter 1988; Webster 2014
**Depends on**: Archaeological evidence for continued (but reduced) monument construction in the Terminal Classic and Postclassic
**Notes**: This finding is relevant to how Chaco's collapse should be modeled. Rather than a binary signal/no-signal outcome, the model should allow for reduced signaling intensity. Schwarz argues signaling continued but in modified form. At Chaco, the Late Bonito McElmo-style great houses (Van Dyke 2004) represent a similar cost-shifted signaling regime.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (well-supported for the Maya case; applicability to Chaco requires separate demonstration)

---

### Claim 4

**Statement**: Maya architectural monuments were "constructed with relatively modest annual labor outlays, as these monuments were built up over time," meaning the amount of architectural cost "does not have to be high on an absolute scale, if the architectural building program is maintained over a long period of time."
**Page**: p. 6
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Abrams 1994:114-119, 127-129
**Depends on**: Abrams's energetics calculations for Maya architecture
**Notes**: Directly relevant to Chaco construction cost parameterization. At Chaco, Crown and Wills (2018) demonstrate a similar pattern: Pueblo Bonito was built incrementally over 250+ years. The annual cost may have been manageable even if the cumulative investment was enormous. This supports modeling C as an annual flow rather than a lump-sum investment.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (Abrams 1994 provides detailed energetic calculations; finding is well-established)

---

### Claim 5

**Statement**: The mobilization of labor to construct monuments was critical to building social power over time. "The organizing and motivating of labor in the construction of architecture among the Maya was perhaps the most important factor in creating a costly outlay," more important than material costs alone.
**Page**: p. 6
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Abrams 1994:114-119, 120-122, 127-129; Mann 1986:119; Trigger 1990; Sanders and Price 1968:55-56
**Depends on**: Comparative evidence from Maya and other complex societies
**Notes**: For the Chaco model, this supports the interpretation that the cost parameter C should reflect labor mobilization capacity rather than raw material costs. Wills et al. (2022) provide labor mobilization data for Chaco that can be interpreted through this lens.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (multiple independent sources converge on this finding)

---

### Claim 6

**Statement**: A triadic model of status communication through architecture involves three roles: the sponsor (who promotes and authorizes production), the builder (architect and labor team), and the receiver (who visits and experiences the building). "The sponsor may mobilize allocative resources (materials and labor) and authoritative resources (canonical principles of design, schema for building, etc.)."
**Page**: pp. 7-8
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Gell 1998; Quinn 2019; Schwarz 2004, 2013b:314; Hall 1966:60-65
**Depends on**: Agency theory framework (Giddens 1984; Gell 1998)
**Notes**: This triadic model provides a more nuanced framework for signal production than the simple signaler-receiver dyad in most CST models. At Chaco, the sponsor-builder-receiver triad maps onto elite sponsors, labor teams (recruited through cooperation networks), and the broader community/pilgrims who observe the construction. The distinction between allocative and authoritative resources is relevant to understanding what Chaco elites controlled.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (model is conceptually clear but empirical validation in specific cases requires inference about intent)

---

### Claim 7

**Statement**: During the Terminal Classic period (AD 800-950), there was a "downward spike in carved monument creation" as "elite competitive advertising faltered." Erection of monuments continued but "at a lower level and with new building and monument designs," representing cost shifting rather than complete cessation of signaling.
**Page**: pp. 10, 14-15
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Neiman 1997:271; Halperin 2017; Rice 1986, 1988; Schwarz 2004, 2009, 2013a-b; Shiratori 2019; Hermes and Quintana 2000:64-66
**Depends on**: Architectural and chronological data from Peten Lakes region sites
**Notes**: The parallel to Chaco is instructive. The Late Bonito period (AD 1100-1140) saw continued great house construction but in smaller, less elaborate McElmo-style forms (Van Dyke 2004), representing a similar cost-shifting dynamic. The key theoretical point is that signaling systems can degrade gradually rather than collapsing suddenly.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (multiple sites and lines of evidence document the architectural transition)

---

### Claim 8

**Statement**: Cost masking in post-collapse Maya contexts took the form of appropriating Classic-period monuments and architectural settings. "The Postclassic builders and their sponsors, the emerging elites in the new era, commandeered these past monumental constructions and settings and so did not have to pay the full cost of building de novo."
**Page**: pp. 15-16
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Chase 1983a; Pugh and Schwarz 1996; Pugh et al. 2012; Rice et al. 1998; Rice and Rice 2015
**Depends on**: Archaeological evidence of Postclassic building atop Classic foundations at multiple sites (Ixlu, Tayasal, Zacpeten)
**Notes**: At Chaco, the post-Chaco reoccupation of great houses (documented in Crown and Wills 2018) and the Aztec Ruins continuation may represent analogous cost masking. The rebuilding atop earlier constructions appropriates the symbolic capital of the earlier system.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (documented at multiple sites with clear stratigraphic evidence)

---

### Claim 9

**Statement**: Imported fineware ceramics (Plumbate, Fine Orange, Fine Gray) constituted costly signals in the Terminal Classic and Early Postclassic period. Plumbate and other finewares constituted "up to 0.58 percent of Terminal Classic-Postclassic ceramic assemblages from seven sites, with ranges from just above zero to around 0.30 percent being the most common."
**Page**: pp. 16-18
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Neff 1984, 2014; Chase 1983; Rice 1987; Table 1 (seven-site comparison); Schwarz 2004
**Depends on**: Ceramic assemblage data from seven Peten Lakes sites
**Notes**: The very low percentages (0.3-0.58%) are significant precisely because they indicate rarity and restricted access, hallmarks of costly signals. At Chaco, exotic goods (turquoise, macaws, cacao) similarly appear in very small absolute quantities but are concentrated in elite contexts (Watson et al. 2015; Mattson 2025). The parallel supports interpreting low-frequency exotics as costly signals rather than utilitarian trade goods.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (quantified assemblage data from multiple sites)

---

### Claim 10

**Statement**: Obsidian functioned as a costly signal of elevated status among the Classic Maya, but its signaling function declined in the Postclassic period. "It appears that a resource that was restricted greatly to the elites and elite control in the Classic period was less restricted, and new strategies for tool procurement were enacted in the Postclassic."
**Page**: pp. 20-22
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Kingsley 2014; Rice 1984; Schwarz 2013a; Schwarz 2004; Meissner 2014; Pierce 2017
**Depends on**: Obsidian-to-chert ratios at Classic vs. Postclassic sites; source provenance data
**Notes**: The decline of obsidian as a costly signal illustrates how signals can lose their power when the cost structure changes (consistent with Quinn 2019). The Maya obsidian case shows how technological and trade route shifts can undermine signaling systems. At Chaco, turquoise may show an analogous pattern if its distribution became less restricted over time, though current evidence (Mattson 2025) suggests elite control was maintained.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (quantitative obsidian/chert ratios and provenance data support the argument)

---

### Claim 11

**Statement**: In the Late Classic period, obsidian-to-chert ratios at major Maya centers were as high as 1.2 (at Seibal), while rural hamlets had ratios as low as 0.03. By the Early Postclassic, ratios across communities converged to between 0.05 and 0.08, and the largest Postclassic community (Zacpeten) had only a 0.19 ratio.
**Page**: pp. 21-22
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Schwarz 2013a: Table 2; Kingsley 2014:205-206: Table 2; Rockmore 2006; Schwarz 2004
**Depends on**: Lithic assemblage quantification from multiple sites
**Notes**: The convergence of obsidian ratios across community sizes in the Postclassic is a quantitative demonstration of signal degradation. When elite and commoner contexts cannot be distinguished by access to the signaling medium, the medium loses its signaling value. This provides a methodological template for testing whether similar convergence occurred with Chaco exotic goods after the florescence.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (quantitative data from multiple sites with clear temporal trend)

### Claim 12

**Statement**: Costly signaling exists along a continuum from honest, costly signals to deceptive, low-cost signals. "There is an inverse relationship between the efficiency of signal creation and signal reliability." The handicap principle "posits that a degree of waste in signaling is inherent in any effective communication."
**Page**: pp. 9-10
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Zahavi and Zahavi 1997:40-60, 229; Laporte 2002
**Depends on**: Zahavi and Zahavi's (1997) handicap principle theory
**Notes**: This continuum model is more nuanced than a binary honest/dishonest signal framework. For the Chaco model, it suggests that signaling quality may have degraded before the system collapsed entirely, with a period of increasingly unreliable signals preceding abandonment. Penn and Szamado (2020) would challenge the handicap framing here, preferring the Spence condition approach.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (the continuum concept is well-supported; the specific invocation of the handicap principle is contested by Penn and Szamado 2020)

---

### Claim 13

**Statement**: Local communities in the Peten Lakes region produced imitation Fine Orange and Plumbate ceramics. These "local similars" are "more of a bricolage" than true imitations: they "portray different iconography from Fine Orange and incorporate more Maya-like glyphs" while referencing the broader Epiclassic symbol system.
**Page**: p. 18
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Chase 1983a; Moriarty 2012, 2013; Rice 1987; Schwarz 2004; Shiratori 2019; Neff 1984, 2014; Christina Halperin personal communication 2021
**Depends on**: Ceramic analysis and comparison of imported vs. local wares
**Notes**: The production of local similars represents cost masking at the community level: elites who could not afford imported finewares produced local versions that referenced the broader status system. At Chaco, small-house communities built Chacoan-style architectural features (great kivas, berms) that may represent similar "bricolage" referencing the great house tradition.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (ceramic analysis supports identification of local vs. imported, but the signaling interpretation requires inference about intent)

---

### Claim 14

**Statement**: The re-expansion of costly signaling margins above a baseline functions as "a principal mechanism for the regeneration of the state. The increased margins allowed elite growth and the creation of new and more obviously honest signals portraying fitness and excellence among resurgent elites."
**Page**: p. 25
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Schwarz 2009, 2013a-b; Rice 2004; Rice and Rice 2018; Halperin and Martin 2020:820-821, 832
**Depends on**: Evidence of architectural monumentality returning in the Late Postclassic
**Notes**: This regeneration mechanism is the inverse of the collapse mechanism. It suggests that signaling systems can restart after a period of cost shifting if new elites emerge who can afford honest signals. For the Chaco project, this may explain the Aztec Ruins phase (post-1110) as an attempted regeneration of the Chacoan signaling system, albeit in a new geographic center.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (the Peten Lakes evidence supports regeneration, but the causal role of signaling re-expansion is inferred rather than demonstrated)

## Claim Type Definitions

- **Empirical**: Direct observation or measurement reported by the author
- **Interpretive**: Inference from empirical data; not directly observable
- **Speculative**: Acknowledged by the author as tentative, or basis unclear
- **Methodological**: Claim about how to conduct or interpret research

## Evidence Strength Ratings

- **Strong**: Claim is well-supported by the evidence presented
- **Moderate**: Claim is partially supported; some evidence but gaps remain
- **Weak**: Claim is poorly supported by the evidence presented
- **Assertion without evidence**: No evidence cited; claim is stated as fact without support

## Summary Assessment

Schwarz 2023 makes a significant theoretical contribution by extending costly signaling theory to political collapse and societal regeneration, introducing the concepts of cost shifting and cost masking as mechanisms that explain how signaling behavior transforms rather than simply ceasing during periods of crisis. The Maya case study from the Peten Lakes provides empirical grounding through three material culture domains (architecture, ceramics, obsidian), each showing distinct trajectories during the Classic-Postclassic transition. The paper's strongest empirical contributions are the quantitative obsidian-to-chert ratios showing convergence across community types in the Postclassic (Claims 10-11) and the fineware percentage data (Claim 9). For the Chaco project, the paper's primary value lies in its theoretical framework for modeling signaling behavior during and after collapse, and its demonstration that signaling systems degrade through cost modification rather than binary failure.
