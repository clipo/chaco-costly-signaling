## Claims

### Claim 1

**Statement**: Great houses functioned as costly signals at two levels for aspiring leaders: to attract/retain group members in order to construct and maintain a competitive signal, and to signal competitive qualities to competing leaders.
**Page**: p. 49 (Table 3.1)
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: Neiman 1997:270; Kantner 1996
**Depends on**: CST framework as applied to group-level signals; Kantner's model of aspiring leaders and kin-based factions
**Notes**: This two-level signaling structure (internal audience: potential group members; external audience: rival leaders) directly parallels our Layer 1 (individual signaling) and Layer 2 (intergroup assessment) distinction. However, Safi does not formalize the model or derive equilibrium conditions.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate (verbal model with archaeological correlates specified in Table 3.1)
**Assessment**: The dual-audience structure is well-argued and supported by a detailed table of archaeological correlates. The framework is more explicitly multi-level than Kantner & Vaughn 2012 but lacks formal specification.

### Claim 2

**Statement**: There are three potential responses by a viewer to a competitive signal: (1) the viewer accurately interprets the signal and invests in the signaler; (2) the viewer recognizes the signal as superior and decreases their own competitive activities; (3) the viewer increases their own signal to exceed the one observed.
**Page**: p. 48
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: Neiman 1997:270
**Depends on**: Neiman's competitive signaling model for Maya elites
**Notes**: Response (2) maps directly onto our Layer 2 conflict deterrence mechanism, where weaker groups recognize their inferiority and avoid conflict. Response (3) implies an escalation dynamic (arms race in signaling intensity).
**Evidence strength**: Moderate (theoretical prediction derived from Neiman)
**Assessment**: These three responses are logical consequences of honest costly signaling and align with our model's predictions, though Safi does not formalize the conditions under which each response obtains.

### Claim 3

**Statement**: Weaker elites or groups can recognize their inferior competitive abilities based on the extravagant costs that go into competitive signals and potentially avoid conflicts they cannot win; followers are able to align themselves with those most likely to provide them with resource benefits and protection.
**Page**: p. 48
**Type**: theoretical
**Evidence cited by author**: Aranyosi 1999:357
**Depends on**: Honest signaling of competitive ability through construction investment
**Notes**: This is the conflict deterrence function of our Layer 2. Safi explicitly connects monument investment to conflict avoidance, though she frames this as a secondary benefit of the signaling interaction rather than modeling it as a distinct layer.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate (theoretical argument with ethnographic reference)
**Assessment**: Supports our Layer 2 formulation but does not test it independently.

### Claim 4

**Statement**: Investment in monumental architecture is one means by which costly signaling theory can be evaluated within an archaeological context. Monumental architecture is defined by "scale and elaboration [that] exceed the requirements of any practical functions that a building is intended to perform" (Trigger 1990:119).
**Page**: p. 45
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Trigger 1990:119; Lupo 2007:168; Bliege Bird and Smith 2005a:236
**Depends on**: Material correlates of signaling behavior can be identified archaeologically
**Notes**: This definition of monumental architecture as exceeding practical requirements is the standard CST criterion for identifying costly signals. Safi operationalizes this through Chacoan architectural features (core-and-veneer walls, banded masonry, high-elevation timbers, over-tall rooms).
**Evidence strength**: Strong (well-established theoretical framework)
**Assessment**: Appropriate operationalization consistent with the broader CST literature.

### Claim 5

**Statement**: Non-canyon great houses are similar to but much smaller than great houses in Chaco Canyon (with the exception of Salmon Ruins and Aztec). Differences in local power and competitive ability should result in visible distinctions in the quality of great house construction, the structure's size or scale of construction events, and in the amount and quality of resources used during great house events.
**Page**: pp. 45-46
**Type**: empirical prediction
**Evidence cited by author**: General observation from Chaco literature
**Depends on**: Quality-dependence of signal investment
**Notes**: This prediction maps onto the Spence condition in our model: higher-quality signalers (larger, more resource-rich groups) invest more in signals, and this variation is visible in the archaeological record. The prediction is key to testing CST against alternative Chaco models.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate (testable prediction)
**Assessment**: Prediction is well-formed and directly testable with the data Safi collected.

### Claim 6

**Statement**: Environmental uncertainty is a less likely explanation for the construction of great houses overall. Many episodes of construction at Chaco Canyon are within intervals of environmental productivity while lulls in construction appear to coincide with environmental downturns. Agricultural productivity increases during the time period in which southern Cibola great houses are constructed.
**Page**: p. 56
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Dean 1992
**Depends on**: Paleoclimate and construction chronology data
**Notes**: This finding contradicts a simple bet-hedging model where signaling increases during environmental stress. Instead, it suggests construction occurs when groups have the surplus to invest, which is more consistent with our model's prediction that signaling intensity depends on the interaction between environmental uncertainty (sigma) and available resources. Safi explicitly rejects environmental instability as a "causal mechanism" for great house construction.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate (based on regional chronological patterns but not statistically tested)
**Assessment**: This claim is important because it distinguishes Safi's CST model from bet-hedging accounts. However, the relationship between construction timing and environmental conditions may be more nuanced than presented; our model predicts lambda(sigma) makes signaling more valuable under uncertainty, but construction still requires sufficient resources.

### Claim 7

**Statement**: Safi derives 17 data expectations to distinguish costly signaling from three alternative models (Chacoan outpost, Chaco emulation, Chaco as ideological system) for outlier great houses. Of these 17 expectations, 11 were supported at the scale of all three great house communities, while 3 were supported for individual great houses.
**Page**: pp. 60-61 (Table 3.2); p. 228
**Type**: empirical/methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Original analysis
**Depends on**: Comparative evaluation of model expectations against data from Largo Gap, Cox Ranch Pueblo, and Cerro Pomo
**Notes**: Table 3.2 is a critical contribution: it specifies which expectations are shared between models and which uniquely distinguish costly signaling. Expectations unique to costly signaling include: prominent landscape placement (expectation 7), unique networks of exchange at households not replicated at great house (expectation 17), and unique patterns of obsidian procurement at each great house (expectation 16).
**Evidence strength**: Moderate (systematic comparison, but some expectations are not uniquely diagnostic)
**Assessment**: The systematic comparison of model predictions is a strength, but the fact that many expectations are shared across models limits the discriminating power. This highlights the need for formal models with quantitative predictions, which is where our approach advances beyond Safi.

### Claim 8

**Statement**: Three southern Cibola great houses (Largo Gap, Cox Ranch Pueblo, Cerro Pomo) were examined as case studies. Largo Gap has approximately 22 rooms in a D-shaped plan with bounded plaza and blocked-in kiva. Cox Ranch Pueblo has approximately 44 rooms in a rectangular structure with bounded plaza and internal kiva. Cerro Pomo has approximately 43 rooms in a rectangular plan with external kiva.
**Page**: pp. 170, 229 (Table 6.14)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Duff 2005; Fowler et al. 1987; original excavation data
**Depends on**: Field survey and excavation
**Notes**: These three sites are much smaller than Chaco Canyon great houses (Pueblo Bonito had 350+ rooms). All three incorporate Chacoan architectural features to varying degrees: Type II-style masonry, compound walls, over-tall rooms, core-and-veneer walls (Cox Ranch), non-local high-elevation timbers.
**Evidence strength**: Strong (based on direct excavation and survey)
**Assessment**: Provides concrete data on outlier great house scale relative to canyon great houses.

### Claim 9

**Statement**: Momentary population estimates for the three southern Cibola communities range from approximately 73-168 people for Cerro Pomo, 131-273 for Largo Gap, and 106-237 for Cox Ranch Pueblo, depending on assumed occupation span (20, 30, or 40 years). None of the three great house groups was reproductively viable on its own if 475 people are necessary; all would have been viable using a minimum threshold of 75-100 people.
**Page**: p. 162 (Table 6.2)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Mahoney 2000:23; Wobst 1974, 1975
**Depends on**: Room count estimates; average room use-life assumptions; demographic viability thresholds
**Notes**: This population data is directly relevant to our model's carrying capacity parameter. The sub-viability of individual communities supports the prediction that signaling facilitates inter-community alliances necessary for demographic viability (Layer 3 cooperation networks). The aggregate population of three communities (310-678) approaches the 475-person threshold.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate (population estimates depend on assumptions about room use-life)
**Assessment**: The population estimates support the argument that individual communities needed external connections, but the wide range of estimates introduces substantial uncertainty.

### Claim 10

**Statement**: Great houses did not control non-local trade. Non-local ceramics were not restricted to great house contexts and were disproportionately identified in great house communities rather than at the great houses themselves (two at Cerro Pomo, four at Cox Ranch Pueblo, and nine at Largo Gap). Individual households, rather than great house occupants, managed their own long-distance exchange and obsidian procurement.
**Page**: pp. 223, 227, 257
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: INAA compositional analysis; XRF obsidian sourcing; Powers et al. 1983
**Depends on**: Compositional analysis of ceramics and obsidian from three communities
**Notes**: This finding is critical for our model. It contradicts a centralized redistribution model and supports our Layer 3 prediction that individual households maintain independent exchange networks. However, it also complicates a simple costly signaling model where great house leaders broker exchange relationships. Safi interprets this as evidence that exchange networks were drawn on by individual household affiliations to support a group-level costly signal (data expectation 17).
**Evidence strength**: Strong (based on compositional analysis: INAA for ceramics, XRF for obsidian)
**Assessment**: Well-supported finding with significant implications for how exchange relates to signaling. Supports decentralized network model rather than elite-controlled redistribution.

### Claim 11

**Statement**: All three southern Cibola great house communities display two manufacturing traditions (Mogollon brown ware and Pueblo gray ware), indicating multi-ethnic settlement. Both traditions are present at all great houses and community sites.
**Page**: pp. 153-154
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Duff 2015; Duff and Nauman 2010
**Depends on**: Technological analysis of utility wares (coil count, indentation, thickness)
**Notes**: Multi-ethnic composition has implications for our model: it suggests that great houses attracted diverse populations, consistent with the prediction that signals attract followers from different ethnic backgrounds. This is consistent with our Layer 1 prediction that signaling attracts members to corporate groups.
**Evidence strength**: Strong (based on technological analysis of manufacturing practices)
**Assessment**: Supports the costly signaling prediction that great houses attract diverse populations.

### Claim 12

**Statement**: Ceramic use at the great house is distinct from household sites: great houses have higher proportional use of smudged brown and red wares relative to the white ware bowls and jars that dominate community site assemblages. These visually distinct wares may have marked great house activities as separate from everyday activities, and their use may have been an important component of a costly signal at Largo Gap.
**Page**: pp. 151, 154-155
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Correspondence analysis of decorated wares (Figure 5.9)
**Depends on**: Ceramic analysis at Largo Gap and associated community
**Notes**: This finding supports the interpretation that great houses served as locations for special activities (feasting, ritual) that were distinct from domestic activities. The use of visually distinctive wares in these contexts supports the credibility-enhancing display function of great houses.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate (clear statistical pattern, but functional interpretation is less certain)
**Assessment**: Suggestive of differential access to or use of specific wares at great houses, consistent with CST but not uniquely diagnostic.

### Claim 13

**Statement**: There is no correlation between great house size (number of rooms) and number of macro-regional exchange partners. A Spearman's rank correlation found the relationship between number of rooms and number of exchange regions is weakly positive but not significant (rs=0.10, p=0.71). With only 14 great houses in the sample and after removing cases with only one production zone identified, R2 improves to 0.43 (p=0.11) but remains non-significant with n=7.
**Page**: pp. 51-52 (Figure 3.1)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Kantner et al. 2000; Neitzel et al. 2002
**Depends on**: Compositional analysis of ceramics from 14 great houses
**Notes**: This is an attempt to test the costly signaling prediction that larger signals (bigger great houses) attract more exchange partners. The failure to find significance may reflect small sample size rather than absence of a relationship. Safi acknowledges this and notes the result is "suggestive" for Chimney Rock. For our model, this suggests the room-count metric may be too coarse to capture signal strength; architectural quality, landscape placement, and associated rituals may be more relevant.
**Evidence strength**: Weak (small sample, non-significant results)
**Assessment**: The test is well-conceived but lacks statistical power. The weak result does not falsify CST, but it also does not provide positive support for the prediction that signal investment correlates with exchange network size.

### Claim 14

**Statement**: Costly signaling currently better accounts for the distribution and use of Chaco-style great houses than does a Chaco-directed model. The Chaco-directed model is the least supported of the four models tested. A costly signaling model of great house construction can better account for the construction and use of these features than a simple model of general emulation or directed construction.
**Page**: pp. 261-262, 271
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Comparison of multiple lines of evidence against four model expectations
**Depends on**: Data from southern Cibola, Middle San Juan, and Red Mesa Valley case studies
**Notes**: This is Safi's overarching conclusion. She evaluates CST against three alternatives: Chaco outpost (hierarchical control), Chaco emulation, and Chaco as ideological system. CST fares best, but she acknowledges it cannot be definitively distinguished from a modified ideological model.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate (systematic comparison but many expectations shared between models)
**Assessment**: The conclusion is reasonable given the evidence but reflects the difficulty of discriminating between models with overlapping predictions using archaeological data alone. Our formal modeling approach can sharpen these predictions.

### Claim 15

**Statement**: Great house architectural variability seems to correlate with when each was constructed relative to other great houses in the general area, with an increase in Chacoan architectural characteristics built into later structures or added during remodeling events of existing structures. Remodeling events may represent a mechanism for increasing the signal's credibility.
**Page**: pp. 169, 271
**Type**: empirical/interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Observations from Bluff, Cox Ranch Pueblo, Middle San Juan structures
**Depends on**: Comparative architectural data from multiple great houses
**Notes**: This temporal pattern of increasing architectural elaboration is consistent with a signaling arms race: as competition intensifies, groups invest more in signal quality (adding Chacoan features during remodeling). This maps onto our model's prediction that signaling investment increases with the number of competing groups (greater competitive pressure).
**Evidence strength**: Moderate (observed pattern, but not quantitatively tested)
**Assessment**: Suggestive pattern that is consistent with CST predictions. The remodeling-as-signal-escalation interpretation is compelling but would benefit from formal modeling.

### Claim 16

**Statement**: Each southern Cibola great house community engaged in exchange with communities 45-90 km away in multiple directions. Approximately the same number of non-local core sherds was identified at each great house (11 at Cerro Pomo and 15 each at Cox Ranch Pueblo and Largo Gap). All three great houses have a variety of unique obsidian sources represented, with 3-6 unique sources at each great house.
**Page**: pp. 223-227, 229 (Table 6.14)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: INAA and XRF analysis
**Depends on**: Compositional analysis of ceramics and obsidian
**Notes**: The fact that each great house has unique exchange networks and obsidian procurement patterns supports both the costly signaling model (each leader maintains distinct exchange relationships as a signal of individual quality) and our Layer 3 prediction (signaling builds cooperation networks). The lack of overlap between great house exchange partners supports competition rather than coordination.
**Evidence strength**: Strong (based on compositional sourcing data)
**Assessment**: Well-supported empirical finding that distinguishes between competing models.

### Claim 17

**Statement**: Obtaining non-local obsidian from multiple sources was symbolic and a component of a costly signaling display. All three great houses have a variety of unique sources represented. The use of multiple obsidian sources at each great house seems unnecessary to daily practice.
**Page**: pp. 227-228
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: XRF analysis (168 obsidian samples)
**Depends on**: XRF obsidian sourcing data from Largo Gap, Cox Ranch Pueblo, and Cerro Pomo
**Notes**: This interpretation connects non-local resource procurement to costly signaling: the diversity of obsidian sources at great houses (beyond what is needed for practical purposes) represents a costly display of the range of external connections maintained by great house leaders.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate (pattern is consistent with CST, but alternative explanations exist)
**Assessment**: The multiple-source interpretation is plausible but would be strengthened by quantitative comparison with household-level obsidian diversity.

### Claim 18

**Statement**: Costly signals could function to attract new contributing group members (followers) during the costly signaling process enacted among competing leaders. Low population estimates for several great house communities suggest exogamous mate selection was necessary.
**Page**: pp. 48, 162-163
**Type**: theoretical/empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Aranyosi 1999:357; Mahoney 2000; Wobst 1974, 1975
**Depends on**: Population estimates and reproductive viability thresholds
**Notes**: This directly corresponds to our Layer 3 prediction that signaling attracts members and builds cooperation networks. The demographic necessity of exogamy provides a concrete fitness benefit to both signalers (attracting mates and followers) and receivers (gaining access to resources and mates), satisfying the mutual benefit criterion of CST.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate (demographic argument is logical but population estimates are uncertain)
**Assessment**: A persuasive argument connecting demographic constraints to the functional benefit of signaling for attracting community members.

### Claim 19

**Statement**: The presence of both "Chacoan" and "Chaco-like" structures within the same communities (e.g., Holmes, Morris 39, Morris 41 in the Middle San Juan) emphasizes that a costly signaling model of great house construction can better account for the construction and use of these features than a simple model of general emulation or directed construction, because there is no conflict between who initiated construction and the extent to which the structure served as a costly signal.
**Page**: p. 262
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Reed 2011, 2014
**Depends on**: Architectural data from Middle San Juan great houses
**Notes**: Safi argues that poor emulation (visible in low-quality materials, smaller structures, lack of veneers) represents a weak costly signal rather than failed emulation. The quality of the signal varies as a direct consequence of the ability to gather resources and organize labor. This is consistent with our quality-dependent investment framework.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate (logical argument but based on limited comparative data)
**Assessment**: The argument that "poor emulation" is actually a weak costly signal is insightful and compatible with our model's Spence condition, where investment is quality-dependent.

### Claim 20

**Statement**: Southern Cibola great houses were built relatively contemporaneously after community formation. Correspondence analysis of ceramic assemblages shows temporal overlap between all three great houses and their communities. This contemporaneity is a necessary condition for costly signaling competition between communities.
**Page**: pp. 165-169
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Correspondence analysis of ceramic types (Figures 6.5, 6.6)
**Depends on**: Ceramic seriation and correspondence analysis
**Notes**: The contemporaneity of multiple great houses in close proximity (11-30 km) is a necessary condition for inter-community competition. Cox Ranch Pueblo may have developed slightly later than Largo Gap and Cerro Pomo.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate (correspondence analysis provides relative chronology, not absolute dates)
**Assessment**: Important supporting evidence for the competitive framework. Without contemporaneity, the CST model of inter-group competition would not apply.
