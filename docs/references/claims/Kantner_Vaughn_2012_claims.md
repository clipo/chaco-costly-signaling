## Claims

### Claim 1

**Statement**: "Monumental centers are costly signals of the authority and influence of competing centers' leadership, which can include the leaders' influence over supernatural forces."
**Page**: p. 66 (abstract)
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Boone 1998, Neiman 1997, Atran and Henrich 2010, Blanton 1998
**Depends on**: Costly signaling theory framework (Zahavi 1975, Bliege Bird and Smith 2005)
**Notes**: This is one of two signaling channels proposed. The center signals leader quality/status.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: Agree. This maps directly onto the mls-monuments model's Layer 2 (intergroup assessment) and partially onto Layer 1 (individual signaling for leader status). However, the claim remains at the verbal/conceptual level without formal modeling.

### Claim 2

**Statement**: "The pilgrimage itself... serves as a costly signal of the pilgrims' commitment to the religious system and the beliefs and values associated with it; this in turn facilitates cooperation and other prosocial behaviors among pilgrims who otherwise might be strangers."
**Page**: p. 66 (abstract)
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Sosis and Bressler 2003, Sosis and Alcorta 2003, Norenzayan and Shariff 2008
**Depends on**: Costly signaling theory; evidence that pilgrimage is genuinely costly
**Notes**: This is the second signaling channel. The act of pilgrimage signals pilgrim quality (commitment/adherence). This maps onto a cooperation-facilitating mechanism consistent with Layer 3 of the mls-monuments model.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: Agree with the theoretical logic. The claim that pilgrimage facilitates cooperation among strangers is well-grounded in CST. However, the archaeological evidence for this specific mechanism at Chaco is indirect.

### Claim 3

**Statement**: For a behavior to qualify as a costly signal, four criteria must be met: "(1) there is within-group variance in some unobservable attribute; (2) observers can benefit from reliable information about this variance; (3) higher-quality signalers can benefit from accurately broadcasting this information, but lower-quality signalers have the potential to achieve benefits at the expense of recipients through deception; and (4) the cost or benefit to the signaler of sending the signal is correlated with the signaler's quality and thus honestly signals their qualities."
**Page**: p. 70
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Boone 1998, p. 7; Smith and Bliege Bird 2003
**Depends on**: None (definitional)
**Notes**: These four criteria are standard CST criteria. Criterion 4 corresponds to the Spence condition in the mls-monuments model. The authors note that most archaeological applications focus on competitive prowess as the hidden quality, but "it is not necessary that the hidden quality play a potential role in competitive interactions; it can also factor into prosocial behavior" (p. 70).

**Evidence strength**: Strong (as definitional criteria drawn from established theory)
**Assessment**: Agree. These criteria are well-established. The extension to prosocial contexts is important and aligns with the mls-monuments model's Layer 3.

### Claim 4

**Statement**: The pilgrimage center and the pilgrimage act "can therefore be thought of as two different costly signals, one signaling status and the other signaling reputation, that synergistically fuel one another."
**Page**: p. 73 (Table 1 discussion)
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Conceptual argument from CST framework
**Depends on**: Claims 1 and 2
**Notes**: This dual-signal framework is a key contribution. The center signals leader status (competition among leaders for adherents), while the pilgrimage act signals pilgrim commitment (cooperation among strangers). The two are interdependent: more impressive centers attract more pilgrims, and more pilgrims validate the center's leaders. This maps onto a feedback loop but is not formally modeled.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (logically coherent but no formal demonstration that the synergy is stable)
**Assessment**: Agree with the conceptual framework. The mls-monuments model formalizes a similar feedback through the lambda-sigma loop, though the mechanism differs (environmental uncertainty rather than religious authority).

### Claim 5

**Statement**: Contexts for emergence of pilgrimage centers as costly signals include: competition among religious leaders for adherents; association of religious leadership with supernatural influence over environment, subsistence, and societal success; and climatic unpredictability that promotes desire among adherents to engage with successful religious leaders.
**Page**: p. 73 (Table 1)
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Conceptual framework; Allen 1992
**Depends on**: CST framework
**Notes**: The third condition (climatic unpredictability) is directly relevant to the bet-hedging/environmental uncertainty component of the mls-monuments model. This is the closest the paper comes to the environmental uncertainty dimension, but it frames uncertainty as driving demand for religious authority rather than as driving signaling investment per se.

**Evidence strength**: Weak (conditions listed without systematic evidence that they are necessary or sufficient)
**Assessment**: The conditions are plausible but presented as a verbal framework without formal derivation or empirical testing. The environmental uncertainty condition aligns with the mls-monuments model's predictions.

### Claim 6

**Statement**: "The intensity of construction during this period clearly exceeded the labor available from canyon residents," while "many of the materials used in the buildings came from dozens of kilometers away, including an estimate that more than 200,000 timbers had to be hand-carried across the San Juan Basin from distant mountains."
**Page**: p. 75
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Lekson 1984, Metcalf 2003, Windes and McKenna 2001
**Depends on**: Dendrochronological and architectural analyses
**Notes**: Key empirical claim establishing construction as genuinely costly. The labor exceeded local capacity, requiring mobilization from beyond the canyon. This is consistent with the model's C = 0.35 parameter (labor diversion exceeding what a single community can absorb). The 200,000 timber figure from Windes and McKenna 2001.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (based on well-documented architectural and dendrochronological evidence)
**Assessment**: Agree. This is well-established empirical evidence.

### Claim 7

**Statement**: "At Chaco's height, over 50% of ceramics and 30% of lithics were non-local, almost all from beyond the canyon, and the evidence is fairly clear that very few of these imported items ever left the canyon."
**Page**: p. 75
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Cameron 2001, Toll 2006
**Depends on**: Ceramic and lithic sourcing studies
**Notes**: One-directional flow of goods into the canyon supports the pilgrimage model and is consistent with the signaling interpretation: items flowed in as part of the costly signaling/pilgrimage system but were consumed or deposited at the center rather than redistributed.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (based on compositional analyses)
**Assessment**: Agree. The unidirectional flow pattern is important for evaluating the "redistribution" model versus the "consumption/signaling" model.

### Claim 8

**Statement**: "High-investment burials in exclusive locations in the great house of Pueblo Bonito suggest that leaders of the pilgrimage center enjoyed high levels of status. These individuals were interred with rare ceremonial items ranging from conch shell trumpets to decorated staves, items that are ethnographically identified as representative of religious leadership, as well as unique vessels perhaps used for drinking imported cacao."
**Page**: p. 75
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Akins 2003, Crown and Hurst 2009
**Depends on**: Burial excavation data
**Notes**: Direct evidence for individual-level status signaling through exotic goods. Maps onto Layer 1 of the mls-monuments model (individual signaling equilibrium). The burial assemblages demonstrate differential access to status-signaling materials.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (based on documented burial assemblages)
**Assessment**: Agree. This is well-documented evidence for elite status differentiation.

### Claim 9

**Statement**: "Their status as powerful priests was likely sustained by the enormous costs of the monumental architecture of Chaco, the spectacle of feasts and kiva ceremonies that occurred there, the importation of large quantities of exotic materials ranging from turquoise to live macaws, the lengthy causeways reinforcing Chaco's centrality, and the carefully elaborated astronomical observatories."
**Page**: p. 75
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Multiple sources cited throughout the Chaco section
**Depends on**: Claims 6, 7, 8
**Notes**: The claim that these costly investments *sustained* leader status is interpretive. The evidence shows the costs existed and that leaders had high status, but the causal mechanism (costs sustaining status through signaling) is inferred from the CST framework.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (evidence exists for both costly investments and high-status leaders; the causal connection is inferred)
**Assessment**: Agree with the interpretation. The mls-monuments model provides the formal mechanism for why these costs would sustain status (the Spence condition).

### Claim 10

**Statement**: The pilgrimage era at Chaco (Classic Bonito, AD 1020-1130) was characterized by "greater average rainfall" but with "comparatively lower autocorrelation and higher standard deviation values," indicating "continuing uncertainty about rainfall, and thus farming success, from year to year."
**Page**: pp. 76-77 (Table 2 and text)
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Force et al. 2002, Grissino-Mayer 1996, Grissino-Mayer et al. 1997
**Depends on**: Dendroclimatological reconstructions
**Notes**: Key empirical data. Table 2 provides precipitation statistics: Classic Bonito (AD 1020-1130) had mean precipitation 15.09 in. (s=1.94, r1=0.275), while Early Bonito (AD 850-1020) had mean 14.31 in. (s=1.59, r1=0.305). The higher standard deviation and lower autocorrelation during the pilgrimage era support the model's prediction that signaling intensifies under environmental uncertainty (higher sigma). However, the authors interpret uncertainty as driving demand for religious leadership rather than driving signaling investment directly.

**Evidence strength**: Strong (quantitative paleoclimate data)
**Assessment**: Agree with the data. The interpretation that uncertainty drove demand for religious leaders is plausible but is only one of several possible mechanisms. The mls-monuments model provides an alternative mechanism: uncertainty drives signaling investment through the lambda(sigma) feedback.

### Claim 11

**Statement**: "The development of an influential pilgrimage center at Chaco Canyon is correlated with widespread evidence of prosocial, cooperative behavior, at least in the beginning. Lekson 2002, LeBlanc 1999, for example, note that the evidence for violent interaction that is so common before and especially after the Chaco era was largely absent during Chaco's height; Lekson refers to the pattern as a 'Pax Chaco.'"
**Page**: p. 77
**Type**: empirical/interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Lekson 2002, LeBlanc 1999, Kantner 1999, Kohler and Kramer Turner 2006, Kohler et al. 2009
**Depends on**: Bioarchaeological evidence for violence patterns
**Notes**: Directly relevant to the mls-monuments model's Layer 2 prediction: monument signaling reduces intergroup conflict. The temporal pattern (low violence during florescence, high violence before and after) is exactly what the model predicts. However, the authors note some forms of violence did occur during the Chaco era, "especially in the years prior to Chaco's collapse" and "in areas outside of Chaco's most direct influence."

**Evidence strength**: Moderate (the pattern is documented but the evidence is mixed, with some violence during the Chaco era)
**Assessment**: The general pattern supports the model, but the nuances (violence at margins, violence increasing before collapse) need careful treatment.

### Claim 12

**Statement**: "Sharing of locally produced commodities with neighbors was commonplace, but that exchange over longer distances actually decreased during the Chaco era. This decrease, however, seems not to be related to reduced interest in trade goods, but rather to increased difficulty of access to long-distance exchange networks at the same time that socioeconomic inequities were emerging."
**Page**: p. 77
**Type**: empirical/interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Kantner and his colleagues 2000, Kantner 2010b
**Depends on**: Exchange network analyses
**Notes**: This is a nuanced finding. Local sharing increased but long-distance exchange became more restricted. The interpretation is that access to long-distance networks became a status marker (socioeconomic inequity), consistent with exotic goods as individual-level signals in the model. The restriction of long-distance exchange to elites supports the signaling interpretation: only high-quality agents could afford the cost.

**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: The empirical finding is important. The restricted long-distance exchange aligns with the model's prediction that signaling costs are quality-dependent (Spence condition).

### Claim 13

**Statement**: "If, however, conditions are so consistently bad that the entreaties of all religious leaders are seen as ineffectual, we might expect pilgrimage centers and pilgrimage activity to eventually end."
**Page**: p. 73
**Type**: speculative
**Evidence cited by author**: Conceptual argument
**Depends on**: The assumption that pilgrimage center legitimacy depends on perceived supernatural efficacy
**Notes**: This prediction aligns with Chaco's collapse during the mid-1100s megadrought. It is also consistent with the mls-monuments model's prediction: sustained environmental stress beyond the system's buffering capacity causes the signaling system to collapse. However, the mechanism differs: Kantner & Vaughn invoke loss of perceived supernatural efficacy, while the model invokes the economic impossibility of maintaining signaling investment.

**Evidence strength**: Weak (speculative prediction, not tested)
**Assessment**: The prediction is plausible and consistent with the Chaco evidence, but the causal mechanism (loss of perceived efficacy vs. economic impossibility) needs empirical discrimination.

## Claim Type Definitions

- **Empirical**: Direct observation or measurement reported by the author
- **Interpretive**: Inference from empirical data; not directly observable
- **Speculative**: Acknowledged by the author as tentative, or basis unclear
- **Methodological**: Claim about how to conduct or interpret research

## Evidence Strength Ratings

- **Strong**: Claim is well-supported by the evidence presented
- **Moderate**: Claim is partially supported; some evidence but gaps remain
- **Weak**: Claim is poorly supported by the evidence presented
- **Assertion without evidence**: No evidence cited; claim is stated as fact without support

## Summary Assessment

Kantner & Vaughn 2012 is the only published application of costly signaling theory directly to Chaco Canyon. The paper makes two key contributions: (1) framing pilgrimage centers as costly signals of leader status and (2) framing the pilgrimage act as a costly signal of pilgrim commitment, with the two functioning synergistically. The theoretical framework is well-grounded in CST but remains verbal rather than formal. The paper provides useful empirical data, particularly the precipitation statistics in Table 2 (showing higher variability during the pilgrimage era) and the pattern of reduced violence during the Chaco florescence. The main limitation for the mls-monuments project is that Kantner & Vaughn treat signaling primarily through a religious/supernatural lens rather than addressing the economic and environmental mechanisms that the three-layer model formalizes. They do not model group-level signaling (monument as intergroup deterrent) or the cooperation-network-building function of monument investment. The environmental uncertainty dimension appears only as a driver of demand for religious authority, not as a direct driver of signaling investment through the lambda(sigma) feedback loop.
