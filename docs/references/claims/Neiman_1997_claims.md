## Claims

### Claim 1

**Statement**: Conspicuous consumption is "wasteful" in a Darwinian sense because "it represents fitness costs that, unlike the time and energy expended on foraging, have no apparent compensatory fitness benefit." Maya monuments (altars, stairs, stelae) "represent expenditures of resources to assemble and supervise the individuals who quarried, transported, assembled, and carved them."
**Page**: pp. 269-270
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Veblen 1899, Zipf 1949, Trigger 1990
**Depends on**: Darwinian fitness framework applied to cultural behavior
**Notes**: This is the foundational argument for interpreting monumental architecture as costly signaling. The characterization of monument costs as fitness costs (diverted from maintenance, mates, offspring) maps directly onto the project's C parameter (labor diversion cost).
**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: The framing is theoretically sound. The claim that monument costs represent genuine fitness costs is plausible but would require demographic evidence to confirm.

---

### Claim 2

**Statement**: "Investment in waste does not confer a direct selective advantage on the individual making it; where does the advantage lie?" Zahavi's answer: "Waste is a signal that conveys novel information about some hidden quality of the sender, who incurred a fitness cost to send the signal, to a receiver." Selective advantage accrues because "the signal causes the receiver to act in a way that benefits the sender."
**Page**: p. 270
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Zahavi 1975, 1987; Grafen 1990a, 1990b; Kirkpatrick 1986
**Depends on**: Zahavi's handicap principle and Grafen's formalization
**Notes**: Core theoretical mechanism for Layer 1 of the project model. The "hidden quality" in the Chaco case would be competitive ability/resource control capacity of the corporate group.
**Evidence strength**: Strong (based on formal mathematical models)
**Assessment**: Well-established theoretical foundation.

---

### Claim 3

**Statement**: In the Darwinian theory of wasteful advertising, "advertising is on average honest. Individuals of greater competitive ability or quality advertise more and receivers correctly interpret the message." Additionally, "advertising is costly. Its costs scale with the amount of advertising." The cost is what guarantees honesty.
**Page**: p. 270
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Derived from Zahavi-Grafen framework
**Depends on**: Formal signaling theory
**Notes**: These two properties (honesty and costliness) are the defining features that must be demonstrated for any CST application. For Chaco, they predict that great house size/investment should correlate with actual group capacity.
**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: Direct derivation from formal models.

---

### Claim 4

**Statement**: The amount of wasteful advertising in a group of competitors is a function of three factors: "(1) the competitive ability of the worst advertiser, (2) the variance in competitive ability in the group, and (3) the payoff to a given investment in advertising." Greater variance in competitive ability "leads to more advertising, because there is more variation in political quality to distinguish." The payoff increases when "the signal reaches a larger audience" and when a larger proportion of the audience is non-kin.
**Page**: pp. 270-271
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Theoretical derivation, Hamilton 1964 (kin selection)
**Depends on**: Formal model
**Notes**: This three-factor model provides specific predictions for the Chaco case: (1) advertising should increase as group differentiation increases; (2) advertising should increase with larger, more heterogeneous audiences (e.g., pilgrimage populations); (3) payoffs to advertising decline when kin comprise a larger share of the audience.
**Evidence strength**: Strong (formal model)
**Assessment**: These predictions are testable at Chaco. The pilgrimage model (large non-kin audience) predicts higher advertising investment than a purely residential model.

---

### Claim 5

**Statement**: Maya monuments were "engineered to be impossible to counterfeit." The costs of supporting a literate elite, skilled carvers, and the labor for construction "guaranteed the signal sent by the monuments was reliable." Some elite members "were personally engaged in wasteful activities" such as having sculptors' names incised on stelae and participating in self-inflicted bloodletting rituals.
**Page**: p. 271
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Stuart 1988, 1993; Schele & Miller 1986
**Depends on**: Epigraphic and iconographic evidence from Maya monuments
**Notes**: The anti-counterfeiting argument strengthens the signaling interpretation. For Chaco, the analogous argument is that great house construction required specialized knowledge (masonry techniques, timber procurement logistics, astronomical alignment) that could not be easily faked.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate (archaeological/epigraphic evidence)
**Assessment**: Plausible but relies on interpreting archaeological evidence through a signaling lens.

### Claim 6

**Statement**: From a sample of 69 Maya sites, terminal monument dates range from AD 731 (Tortuguero) to AD 909 (Tonina), spanning 178 years. The large-scale trend in cessation of monument construction, estimated using loess regression, shows expected terminal dates varying from c. AD 760 to 900 across the region.
**Page**: pp. 273-277, Table 15.1
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Bove 1981, Morley et al. 1983, Houston n.d., Mathews & Willey 1991, and others (see Table 15.1)
**Depends on**: Compilation of terminal monument dates from multiple sources
**Notes**: The empirical dataset of 69 sites provides the basis for Neiman's spatial analysis. The 178-year span of monument cessation indicates a gradual process, not a sudden collapse.
**Evidence strength**: Strong (primary data compilation)
**Assessment**: Rigorous data collection, though dependent on accuracy of monument dating.

---

### Claim 7

**Statement**: A "surprisingly strong" relationship exists between terminal monument dates and mean annual rainfall. The rank correlation between rainfall and fitted terminal dates is r = -0.69 (Spearman's r). "Areas with the highest annual rainfall (> 2500 mm) have the earliest expected terminal dates," while areas on the dry margins have the latest. "Permutation-based testing reveals the probability of observing effects of this size by chance alone is vanishingly small."
**Page**: pp. 279-281, Figure 15.5
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Rice 1993 (rainfall data), statistical analysis by Neiman
**Depends on**: Spatial correlation between rainfall and monument dates
**Notes**: This is the paper's most important empirical finding. The relationship between environmental conditions (rainfall) and the cessation of costly signaling (monument construction) directly parallels the project's prediction that signaling investment at Chaco should correlate with environmental conditions (PDSI). However, the direction is opposite to what the project might predict: at Chaco, construction increases during drought (negative correlation), while Maya cessation occurs first in wet areas.
**Evidence strength**: Strong (quantitative spatial analysis with permutation testing)
**Assessment**: Methodologically rigorous. The ecological disaster interpretation is well-supported.

### Claim 8

**Statement**: The variogram analysis of terminal monument date residuals shows that "the semivariance increases past the total variance, reaching a maximum at a distance of roughly 65 km. Thereafter it declines until it reaches a value that is roughly equal to the total residual variance, at about a distance of about 150 km." This "hole effect" pattern indicates that "pairs of sites that are 65 km apart have terminal dates that are less similar than pairs of sites chosen at random."
**Page**: pp. 280-283, Figure 15.6
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Geostatistical analysis by Neiman; Davis 1986, Isaaks & Srivastava 1989
**Depends on**: Variogram analysis of spatial data
**Notes**: The 65 km distance at the semivariance peak provides an estimate of the spatial scale of political competition. The "hole effect" is interpreted as evidence of competitor movement between catchments during the collapse, which increased advertising at recipient sites while hastening cessation at donor sites.
**Evidence strength**: Strong (quantitative geostatistical analysis)
**Assessment**: Innovative use of geostatistics. The interpretation (competitor movement) is plausible but not the only possible explanation for the observed pattern.

---

### Claim 9

**Statement**: "Our best estimate -- 50 km -- of the mean size of political catchments from the variogram fits comfortably with" the estimate of polity size derived from Peter Mathews' epigraphic analysis of emblem glyphs, where "the mean nearest-neighbor distance between 32 Late Classic sites with identified emblem glyphs is 52 km." This agreement between independent lines of evidence supports the signaling interpretation.
**Page**: pp. 283-284
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Mathews 1991, Houston 1993
**Depends on**: Independent convergence of variogram and epigraphic analyses
**Notes**: The convergence of two independent methods on the same spatial scale (~50 km) strengthens the interpretation. For the Chaco model, this suggests looking for spatial patterning in construction investment over distances consistent with the known extent of great house catchments (~60-80 km to outlier communities).
**Evidence strength**: Strong (independent convergence)
**Assessment**: Compelling methodological approach. The agreement is notable.

### Claim 10

**Statement**: The cessation of monument construction across the region was ultimately caused by "an ecological disaster in which erosion played a leading role." Evidence includes anthropogenic environmental alteration recovered from lake sediment cores showing "dramatic increases in the amount of sediment and in silica concentrations, offering convincing evidence for soil erosion on a massive and destructive scale" from BC 1000 to AD 900.
**Page**: pp. 280-281, 284
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Hodell et al. 1995 (Lake Chichancanab), Rice 1993, Deevey et al. 1979, Brenner et al. 1990
**Depends on**: Paleoenvironmental data from sediment cores
**Notes**: The ecological disaster model for Maya collapse provides a direct parallel to the Chaco case, where prolonged drought (megadrought beginning c. AD 1130) is associated with the cessation of great house construction and canyon abandonment. Both cases show environmental degradation preceding the end of monumental investment.
**Evidence strength**: Strong (multiple independent paleoenvironmental proxies)
**Assessment**: Well-supported by diverse evidence. The parallel with Chaco is direct.

### Claim 11

**Statement**: "Theory forges the links from which we can model the causal chain that connects monuments to the conditions and mechanisms responsible for them. An explicit mechanistic theoretical framework facilitates model construction -- the valid deduction of precise empirical expectations that should hold if our initial ideas about the mechanisms and the historical contexts in which they operated are correct."
**Page**: p. 285
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: General argument for mechanistic theory
**Depends on**: Philosophy of science
**Notes**: This argument for explicit mechanistic theory as opposed to "covert, common-sense assumptions about human nature" directly supports the project's approach of building a formal three-layer model with quantitative predictions.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate (methodological argument)
**Assessment**: A persuasive argument for the type of model-building the project undertakes.

### Claim 12

**Statement**: Under the ecological disaster scenario, "the cessation of monument construction was the predictable outcome of collapse in the range of political quality and the number of competitors." The collapse process involved both "a reduction in the range of political quality among competitors, caused by inability of individuals to produce or extract from others enough food to feed and maintain large cohorts of political allies" and "a reduction in population size and thus the number of political competitors."
**Page**: pp. 279-280
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Theoretical derivation from the wasteful advertising model
**Depends on**: The three-factor model of advertising intensity (Claim 4)
**Notes**: This prediction of how collapse proceeds (shrinking quality variance + shrinking audience) provides specific expectations for the Chaco case. The model predicts that as drought reduced agricultural productivity, both the range of group capacities and the number of competing groups would decline, reducing the payoff to signaling investment.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate (theoretical prediction)
**Assessment**: Logical derivation from the model. Testable against Chaco construction chronology data.

---

### Claim 13

**Statement**: The Maya collapse was "not a palimpsest of two downward trends in an ongoing political cycle" but rather "a single time and space-transgressive event." The loess-fitted surface shows "expected dates for crossing the monument-construction threshold varying widely across the region from c. AD 760 to 900," indicating that "the deterministic component behind the shrinkage in political-quality range and audience size... is a time-transgressive process operating in different places in the region at times separated by nearly a century and a half."
**Page**: pp. 277, 284
**Type**: empirical
**Evidence cited by author**: Loess regression analysis, Figure 15.3
**Depends on**: Spatial-temporal analysis
**Notes**: The time-transgressive nature of the Maya collapse, driven by ecological factors, provides a structural comparison for understanding Chaco's more rapid collapse (c. AD 1130-1150). The difference in timescale may reflect differences in the spatial extent of the system and the nature of the ecological shock (gradual soil erosion vs. rapid drought onset).
**Evidence strength**: Strong (based on quantitative spatial analysis)
**Assessment**: Well-supported empirical finding.

## Summary Assessment

Neiman 1997 is the foundational application of costly signaling theory to monumental architecture in archaeology. It provides: (1) a formal model of "wasteful advertising" deriving monument construction from competitive political signaling; (2) specific predictions about how advertising intensity responds to competitive ability variance, audience size/composition, and ecological conditions; (3) a rigorous empirical test using spatial analysis of 69 Maya terminal monument dates; (4) an innovative geostatistical method (variogram analysis) for estimating the spatial scale of political competition (~50 km). The paper's framework translates directly to the Chaco model: replace Maya stelae with great houses, Maya elites with corporate groups, and the ecological disaster of soil erosion with the megadrought of AD 1130. The three-factor model of advertising intensity (quality of weakest competitor, variance in quality, payoff to advertising) provides testable predictions for the Chaco case.
