## Claims

### Claim 1

**Statement**: Costly signaling theory (CST) provides "a quantitative theory of symbolic behavior" that bridges materialist theories of political economy with interpretive approaches to meaning, offering a "middle ground" between the two.
**Page**: p. 275 (abstract)
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: General framing; draws on Bliege Bird & Smith 2005, Zahavi 1975
**Depends on**: Acceptance of CST framework as applicable to human cultural behavior
**Notes**: This frames CST as uniquely positioned to address both material costs and symbolic meaning, which is relevant to the project's theoretical positioning.
**Evidence strength**: Assertion without evidence
**Assessment**: A reasonable characterization of CST's theoretical positioning, though whether it actually succeeds as a "middle ground" is debatable.

### Claim 2

**Statement**: Four necessary conditions must hold for a signaling system to be honest and stable: (1) signalers vary in some underlying quality relevant to receivers; (2) signals are observable and associated with the quality of interest; (3) signals are costly to produce and the cost-benefit ratio depends on signaler quality; (4) both signalers and receivers obtain net fitness benefits from the signaling interaction.
**Page**: pp. 276-277
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Grafen 1990, Johnstone 1997, Maynard Smith & Harper 2003, Searcy & Nowicki 2005, Bliege Bird & Smith 2005
**Depends on**: Formal signaling theory from biology
**Notes**: These four conditions constitute the analytical framework Quinn proposes for evaluating archaeological applications. They map onto the Spence condition and handicap principle in the project's formal model.
**Evidence strength**: Strong (derived from formal models)
**Assessment**: Well-established in the biological signaling literature. The challenge is operationalizing these conditions with archaeological data.

### Claim 3

**Statement**: The handicap principle (Zahavi 1975) holds that "signal reliability is maintained by making signals costly enough that dishonest signalers (those signaling a quality they do not possess) could not afford to produce them." This was formalized by Grafen (1990) who showed that "an honest signaling equilibrium in which signalers of different quality levels signal at different intensities is an evolutionarily stable strategy."
**Page**: pp. 276-277
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Zahavi 1975, Grafen 1990
**Depends on**: Evolutionary game theory
**Notes**: This is the formal foundation for Layer 1 of the project model (individual signaling equilibrium, Spence condition).
**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: The Grafen 1990 formalization is the canonical proof. Quinn accurately summarizes it.

---

### Claim 4

**Statement**: A key distinction exists between "assessment signals" (where cost is inherent in the signal production itself) and "conventional signals" (where cost is imposed through social punishment for dishonesty). Maynard Smith and Harper (2003) argued that many signals previously attributed to the handicap principle are actually assessment signals.
**Page**: p. 277
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Maynard Smith & Harper 1995, 2003; Searcy & Nowicki 2005
**Depends on**: Signal classification framework
**Notes**: This distinction matters for archaeological applications: monumental architecture may function as an assessment signal (building capacity is inherent in the signal) rather than a pure handicap.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: The distinction is theoretically important but difficult to operationalize archaeologically.

### Claim 5

**Statement**: CST has been applied in archaeology to three main domains: (1) unconditional generosity and public goods provisioning (feasting, food sharing), (2) monumental architecture and conspicuous construction, and (3) artistic or craft traditions and prestige goods.
**Page**: pp. 278-279
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Reviews literature including Smith & Bliege Bird 2000, Neiman 1997, Boone 1998, Graves & Ladefoged 1995, and others
**Depends on**: Literature survey
**Notes**: All three domains are relevant to Chaco: (1) feasting at great houses, (2) great house construction, (3) turquoise, macaws, cacao. The project's three-layer model maps onto these domains.
**Evidence strength**: Strong (comprehensive literature review)
**Assessment**: Accurate summary of the literature to 2019.

### Claim 6

**Statement**: Neiman (1997) was the first to apply CST to monumental architecture, arguing that "Classic Maya monument construction represents wasteful advertising of competitive political ability." His spatial analysis of terminal monument dates showed that "spheres of political competition on the eve of the Maya collapse averaged about 50 km in diameter."
**Page**: pp. 279-280
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Neiman 1997
**Depends on**: Neiman's empirical analysis
**Notes**: Quinn identifies Neiman 1997 as the foundational archaeological application of CST to monuments. The spatial scale of competition (~50 km) provides a comparative benchmark for the Chaco model.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate (secondary report of Neiman's findings)
**Assessment**: Accurate characterization of Neiman's contribution.

### Claim 7

**Statement**: Archaeological applications of CST face a persistent challenge: "it is often difficult or impossible to demonstrate signal honesty" with archaeological data. Specifically, demonstrating that signal costs are quality-dependent (the Spence condition) requires "linking costly behaviors to underlying variation in signaler quality, which is often not directly observable."
**Page**: pp. 281-282
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Conolly 2017, Codding & Bird 2015
**Depends on**: Epistemological limitations of archaeology
**Notes**: This is the central critique that the project's formal model must address. The three-layer model derives monument investment from individually rational behavior, which could be tested even without directly observing signaler quality, by examining whether investment patterns match the model's predictions for quality-dependent signaling.
**Evidence strength**: Strong
**Assessment**: A valid and important critique. The project should address this directly.

### Claim 8

**Statement**: Most archaeological applications of CST "remain largely qualitative and descriptive" rather than employing formal models or quantitative tests. **Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: General assessment of the literature
**Depends on**: Literature review
**Notes**: This critique directly motivates the project's approach: building a formal three-layer model with quantitative predictions rather than verbal arguments.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate (assessment based on literature survey)
**Assessment**: Largely accurate. The project's formal model is a direct response to this gap.

### Claim 9

**Statement**: Signals that provide benefits to receivers in addition to information ("altruistic signals" or "costly helping") present a different equilibrium structure than purely wasteful displays. In altruistic signaling, "the signal itself generates direct benefits to receivers, which can stabilize the signaling system even when the cost-quality relationship is weaker."
**Page**: pp. 277-278
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Zahavi 1995, Bliege Bird & Smith 2005, Hawkes & Bliege Bird 2002, Gintis et al. 2001
**Depends on**: Theoretical distinction between wasteful and altruistic signaling
**Notes**: This is directly relevant to the project's Layer 3 (cooperation networks). Great house construction and feasting could be altruistic signals: they provide direct benefits (shelter, food, ceremonial space) in addition to information about the signaler's quality/resources.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate
**Assessment**: The theoretical distinction is important but the archaeological implications are underexplored.

### Claim 10

**Statement**: Monumental architecture is "one of the most commonly cited examples of costly signaling in archaeology" because monuments are durable, visible, and their construction costs can often be estimated. Archaeological examples include Maya stelae (Neiman 1997), Polynesian ceremonial architecture (Graves & Ladefoged 1995), Irish hillforts (O'Driscoll 2017), and Mongolian stone mounds (Wright 2017).
**Page**: pp. 279-280
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Multiple sources as listed
**Depends on**: Literature review
**Notes**: Provides comparative context for the Chaco application. The Polynesian and Maya examples are most relevant comparisons.
**Evidence strength**: Strong (well-documented comparative cases)
**Assessment**: Accurate. Chaco great houses fit squarely within this tradition.

### Claim 11

**Statement**: The show-off hypothesis (Hawkes 1991, 1993) proposes that men's hunting of large game evolved as competitive display rather than household provisioning. Costly signaling models resolve the collective action problem inherent in wide sharing of meat by showing that "the hunter's benefit does not depend on collecting repayments from each recipient" but on the reputational information conveyed.
**Page**: p. 278
**Type**: interpretive
**Evidence cited by author**: Hawkes 1991, 1993; Hawkes & Bliege Bird 2002; Bliege Bird et al. 2001
**Depends on**: Ethnographic data from Ache, Hadza, Meriam
**Notes**: The show-off hypothesis provides the theoretical foundation for understanding how individually costly behavior can be maintained without reciprocity, which is the mechanism underlying Layers 1 and 3 of the project model.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate (ethnographic evidence is strong but archaeological application is inferred)
**Assessment**: Well-supported by ethnographic data.

### Claim 12

**Statement**: Quinn proposes an analytical framework for evaluating CST applications in archaeology organized around four steps: (1) identify the proposed signal; (2) specify the signaler, receiver, and signaled quality; (3) demonstrate that the signal is costly and that costs are quality-dependent; (4) show that the signaling system produces a net benefit for both parties.
**Page**: pp. 283-285
**Type**: methodological
**Evidence cited by author**: Synthesized from Grafen 1990, Bliege Bird & Smith 2005, Searcy & Nowicki 2005
**Depends on**: Formal signaling theory
**Notes**: This four-step framework can serve as a checklist for evaluating the project's Chaco application.
**Evidence strength**: Moderate (proposed framework, not yet empirically validated)
**Assessment**: Useful organizational tool. The project should explicitly address each step.

## Summary Assessment

Quinn 2019 provides the most comprehensive recent review of CST applications in archaeology. It establishes the formal conditions for honest signaling, surveys the range of archaeological applications (including monuments, feasting, and prestige goods), and identifies key methodological challenges: demonstrating signal honesty, operationalizing quality-dependence, and distinguishing CST from alternative frameworks. The paper's call for more rigorous, formal models directly motivates the project's three-layer approach. Quinn also notes Safi (2015) as a direct CST application to Chaco outlier great houses, which should be investigated further.
