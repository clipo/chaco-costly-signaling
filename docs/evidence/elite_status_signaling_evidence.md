# Elite Status Signaling Evidence

Compiled from claims extractions: `docs/references/claims/`. All page numbers refer to the PDF/publication pages documented in the claims files. See individual claims files for full quotes and context.

## Key Questions

- What evidence exists for individual-level status differentiation at Chaco?
- How were exotic goods (turquoise, macaws, cacao, shell) distributed between great houses and small houses?
- Was access to long-distance exchange networks restricted to elites or broadly distributed?
- Does the distribution of exotic goods support quality-dependent signaling (Spence condition)?

## Evidence

### From Kantner & Vaughn 2012

**Findings:**

- High-investment burials in exclusive Pueblo Bonito locations contained conch shell trumpets, decorated staves, and unique vessels for imported cacao (p. 75; citing Akins 2003, Crown and Hurst 2009)
- These individuals "were almost certainly religious leaders who benefited from the canyon's role as a pilgrimage center" (p. 75)
- Leader status was "likely sustained by the enormous costs of the monumental architecture of Chaco, the spectacle of feasts and kiva ceremonies... the importation of large quantities of exotic materials" (p. 75)
- Long-distance exchange decreased during the Chaco era while local sharing continued; restricted access to trade networks coincided with emerging socioeconomic inequities (p. 77; citing Kantner et al. 2000, Kantner 2010b)
- Pilgrimage was "especially important for local priestly leaders in outlying villages far from Chaco Canyon who not only wished to signal that they were aligned with the pilgrimage center's beliefs, but who may also have worked towards enhancing their own status in local sociopolitical contexts" (p. 76)

**Key observations:** Kantner & Vaughn document both the existence of elite status differentiation (burial evidence) and the restriction of long-distance exchange to elites. The finding that exchange access became restricted aligns with the Spence condition: as the signaling system matured, the cost of acquiring exotic goods became quality-dependent. The outlier leader dimension is relevant to understanding how signaling operated at different spatial scales.

### From Wills 2017

**Findings:**

- Genetic evidence from Pueblo Bonito burials indicates "at least one matriline in the burial population" (p. 397; citing Kennett et al. 2017)
- Matrilocality was "useful in recruiting men and their labor through marriage, both pulling them into new social networks and splitting them away from (at least in part) their natal communities" (p. 397)
- There is genetic evidence for "at least two biological groups among the Pueblo Bonito burials" (p. 397; citing Akins 1986, Peregrine and Ember 2002, Schillaci 2003, Schillaci and Stojanowksi 2002, Schillaci et al. 2001)
- In the context of immigration, "matrilineal systems can create powerful positive reinforcing dynamics that accelerate recruitment" (p. 397)
- Great houses and Chacoan-style structures established outside the Chaco Core "may then have marked or signaled corporate control under conditions of increasing population and perhaps escalating competition for labor" (p. 398)

**Key observations:** Wills 2017 provides indirect evidence for elite status signaling through the matrilineal dynasty evidence from Kennett et al. 2017 (which is not directly available in the repository). The matrilineal structure provides a mechanism for hereditary status differentiation, which is a precondition for the Spence condition (quality-dependent signaling costs). The connection between matrilineality and labor recruitment is relevant because it suggests that elite status was partly a function of network size and kinship connections, not just material wealth, which aligns with a signaling model where quality is multidimensional.

### From Kennett et al. 2017

**Findings:**

- All nine successfully sampled individuals buried in the Room 33 crypt at Pueblo Bonito share identical mitochondrial DNA genomes (B2y1 haplotype), with consensus sequences averaging 98.1% of the complete mitochondrial genome at 7.4x to 117x coverage (p. 3)
- Nuclear genome SNP genotyping of six individuals identified two pairs with significant genetic relatedness: crania 1 and 7 are likely first-degree relatives (mother-daughter: 35-45-year-old female and 23-27-year-old female), and crania 8 and 10 are first- or second-degree relatives (grandmother-grandson: 30-40-year-old female and 30-35-year-old male) (pp. 4-5)
- AMS radiocarbon dating places the lineage founder (Burial 14) at 800-850 CE, with continuous interment in the crypt through approximately 1130 CE, a span of approximately 330 years (pp. 2-3)
- Burial 14, a male in his 40s, was interred with more than 11,200 turquoise and more than 3,300 shell beads and pendants, multiple abalone shells from the Pacific Coast, and a conch shell trumpet, making it the richest burial known in the North American Southwest (p. 2)
- Room 33 was purpose-built as a crypt during Pueblo Bonito's initial construction phase in the ninth century CE (p. 2)
- Adjacent rooms contained caches of ceremonial staffs, cylindrical ceramic vessels, scarlet macaw remains, and jewelry (p. 2)
- The authors conclude that "hereditary inequality and societal complexity emerged in Chaco by the early ninth century CE and that this matrilineal dynasty persisted at the centre of this complex society for ~330 years until its rapid collapse in the early twelfth century CE" (p. 2)
- Matrilineal systems are ethnographically documented in all Western Pueblos (Hopi, Zuni, Acoma, Laguna) and among Keresan-speaking Rio Grande Pueblos (p. 5)

**Key observations:** Kennett et al. 2017 provides the most direct evidence for hereditary elite status at Chaco Canyon. For the costly signaling model, the key implications are threefold. First, the matrilineal dynasty provides the quality differentiation that the Spence condition requires: if elite status is heritable, signaler type (q) is correlated within lineages, creating a stable basis for separating equilibria. Second, the 330-year persistence demonstrates that the signaling equilibrium endured across multiple generations including periods of environmental variability, terminating only with the system-wide collapse around 1130 CE. Third, the institutionalization of elite status at the founding of Pueblo Bonito (Burial 14 in the early ninth century) constrains models that treat inequality as emergent during the florescence; status differentiation was present from the start. The concentration of the highest-value exotic goods (turquoise, macaws, cacao vessels, conch shells) in and around Room 33 supports the inference that this matriline controlled access to the costliest signaling media.

### From Hull et al. 2014

**Findings:**

- Over 200,000 turquoise pieces recovered from Chaco Canyon, the nearest source (Cerrillos Hills) being 200+ km away (p. 187). The sheer scale of accumulation at a location remote from any geological source establishes turquoise as a high-cost signaling medium.
- Isotopic provenance analysis of 74 turquoise artifacts using hydrogen and copper isotope ratios identifies a stark divergence in procurement patterns within Chaco Canyon: Pueblo Bonito turquoise was sourced 81% (13/16) from Rio Grande Rift deposits (Cerrillos Hills, Villa Grove, Orogrande), while small-site turquoise in Marcia's Rincon was 100% (6/6) from western Nevada deposits (Royston, Lone Mountain) with zero overlap (p. 191).
- This demonstrates "diverse populations living contemporaneously in the canyon" with distinct exchange connections (p. 193). Pueblo Bonito elites organized expeditions to or maintained trade networks with Rio Grande Rift sources, while Marcia's Rincon inhabitants were "long-distance merchants or linked to a trade network that extended to the western resource areas" (p. 193).
- The great house/small site procurement split persists at northern San Juan Basin sites: Aztec Ruin (6/8 sourced artifacts from eastern deposits) mirrors Pueblo Bonito, while Salmon Ruin (3/3 sourced from western deposits) mirrors Marcia's Rincon (p. 193).
- Trade was bidirectional: Villa Grove (Colorado) turquoise reached the Moapa Valley in Nevada, and Orogrande (New Mexico) turquoise reached Eagle's Watch in Utah (p. 193), indicating reciprocal exchange flows rather than one-directional extraction.
- Two 10th-century Room 33 burials contained "the most elaborate turquoise artifacts and thousands of turquoise pieces" (p. 190), linking turquoise accumulation directly to the hereditary elite documented by Kennett et al. 2017.
- Pueblo Bonito accessed at least five distinct turquoise geological sources (Cerrillos Hills, Villa Grove, Orogrande, Crescent Peak, Lone Mountain), demonstrating broad network maintenance (Table 2, p. 192).

**Key observations:** Hull et al. 2014 provide the strongest isotopic evidence that turquoise procurement was socially structured. The complete divergence between great house and small site sourcing patterns within a single canyon directly supports the Spence condition for Layer 1: turquoise acquisition cost was quality-dependent because access to specific trade routes was mediated by social position. Elite households at Pueblo Bonito controlled access to the closer, presumably more easily maintained, Rio Grande Rift sources. The pattern persisting at Aztec and Salmon Ruins indicates this was not a local anomaly but a structural feature of the Chaco exchange system. The bidirectional flow of turquoise and the multi-source procurement at Pueblo Bonito both indicate that turquoise exchange was embedded in broader social relationships (alliances, cooperation networks), consistent with Layer 3's predictions about cooperation networks.

### From Hawkes & Bliege Bird 2002

**Findings:**

- Good hunters in multiple forager societies enjoy "greater social, political, and reproductive success than do poorer competitors" (p. 61). Among the Ache, better hunters had much higher fertility (Hill & Hurtado 1996); among the Hadza, better hunters had younger wives (Blurton Jones et al. 1997); among the Meriam, turtle hunters had higher age-specific reproductive success (Smith & Bliege Bird 2000).
- Status competition intensifies investment: "When some individuals gain competitive advantages over others by engaging in activities with display value, and when productive activities are effective displays, increased productivity can be the outcome of status competition" (p. 64, Box 3).
- The benefits of signaling come from "the different ways that others treat them in light of the quality they reveal. Others use the information of hunting reputations to their own advantage in the numerous decisions of social life" (p. 61).

**Key observations:** While Hawkes & Bliege Bird 2002 focuses on individual-level signaling through hunting in forager societies, the mechanisms translate to the Chaco case. The key insight is that status benefits from signaling are mediated by social treatment (alliance formation, mate choice, deference) rather than direct material returns, which parallels how elite status at Chaco may have been maintained through ritual authority and network access rather than material extraction.

### From Neiman 1997

**Findings:**

- The "hidden quality" advertised by Maya monuments is "competitive ability," meaning "the likelihood that individuals will win political contests with competitors, contests whose outcomes ultimately determine access to mates and resources" (p. 270).
- Competitive ability encompasses "physical size, physiological condition, fighting skill, and psychological cleverness, all of which contribute to an individual's ability to build and maintain coalitions comprised of kin and non-kin" (p. 270).
- Maya epigraphic evidence shows "time depth in the elite status of individuals whose life histories are catalogued and trans-generational continuity in the elite status of their predecessors" at Copan and other sites (p. 271; citing Mathews 1985, Stuart 1993).
- Self-inflicted bloodletting rituals are interpreted as "an advertisement of political quality": "the ability to withstand blood loss without fainting on the spot or more long-term deleterious effects is a function of physiological and nutritional status and hence an accurate rate indicator of fighting ability and resource access" (p. 271).

**Key observations:** Neiman's multidimensional characterization of "competitive ability" (physical, social, coalition-building) is more nuanced than simple wealth or military power. Applied to Chaco, this suggests that the "quality" signaled by great house construction is not merely resource holding but the capacity to organize labor, maintain social networks, and sustain ritual authority over time. The trans-generational dimension (documented by Maya epigraphy and by the Pueblo Bonito matrilineal dynasty evidence of Kennett et al. 2017) strengthens the case for hereditary status as a component of signaled quality.

### From Barclay et al. 2021

**Findings:**

- Among Martu Aboriginal women, the proportion of income shared (not absolute amount) determines reputation for generosity and attracts cooperative partners (Section 1b; Bliege Bird et al. 2012, 2018)
- The welfare trade-off ratio (WTR) quantifies "how much you value another person, relative to how much you value yourself"; helping occurs when WTR x b > c (Section 1a)
- Stake is "relationship-specific" and can be "asymmetric: you may depend upon someone more than they depend on you" (Section 2c)
- Costlier or more "genuine" forms of generosity engender more trust than "more selfish or aggrandizing forms" (Section 1b)

**Key observations:** Barclay et al. 2021 refine the signaling quality concept for the elite status domain. The proportion-based criterion (share of income, not absolute wealth) is important for archaeological operationalization: at Chaco, the ratio of construction investment to community size (not just absolute monument size) should be the relevant signal of elite commitment. The asymmetric stake concept maps onto the core-periphery structure: outlier community leaders may have had greater stake in the canyon center than vice versa. The finding that "genuine" generosity signals more effectively than aggrandizing forms suggests that elite status was best maintained through costly prosocial investment (monument construction, feasting) rather than through conspicuous personal consumption alone.

### From Kohler et al. 2023

**Findings:**

- House-size Gini coefficients driven by the appearance of Chacoan Great Houses increased markedly for the northern Southwest as a whole around AD 900. (Sec. 2f)
- The CMV showed a simultaneous decline in Gini coefficients, suggesting active resistance to the Chaco model of wealth inequality. (Sec. 2f)
- Wealth inequality returned to low levels in the early 1200s in the CMV, but increased again beyond the northern Southwest norm by mid-century before both declined during the depopulation. (Sec. 2f)
- Periods of high wealth inequality are followed by high violence, and vice versa, a statistically significant bidirectional pattern. (Sec. 2f)
- The best time to found a village-spanning polity is when growth is still rapid and has not quite become throttled by density-dependent constraints, when a polity will look attractive for its ability to buffer spatial and temporal shortfalls. (Sec. 4)

**Key observations:** Kohler et al. 2023 provide the most comprehensive temporal dataset on wealth inequality in the Pueblo Southwest. The finding that Chacoan great houses drove regional inequality increases is consistent with the signaling interpretation: great houses were a mechanism through which certain corporate groups established status differentiation. The active resistance by the CMV constrains the model's predictions about the spatial extent of signaling-based inequality. The timing prediction (polity formation at the growth-to-constraint transition) aligns with the lambda(sigma) prediction.

### From Mattson 2025

**Findings:**

- The lapidary tool assemblage from Pueblo Bonito totals 758 objects (335 from the structure, 423 from the mounds), almost eight times the number at the known workshop site 29SJ629 and more than three times the combined assemblages from all Chaco Project sites (p. 95, Table 1).
- Statistical analysis shows no significant difference in abrader ratios between Pueblo Bonito and the workshop site 29SJ629 (chi-squared = 0.687, p = 0.406). After normalizing for population, production indexes are comparable: 0.18 for Pueblo Bonito and 0.21 for 29SJ629 (p. 105).
- The highest concentration of lapidary tools is in rooms near the northern burial cluster (interpreted as the core of the primary house), which also contain "abraders of socially charged shapes (sandals); greater quantities of valuable and imported raw materials that may indicate differential access to exchange networks; and probable evidence of a more ritualized ornament production process" (p. 106).
- Mattson interprets the evidence as "embedded production" (Ames 1995:174): elite household members producing ornaments as part of their social roles, not as dependents of elites but as elite individuals themselves (p. 106).
- Early excavations at Pueblo Bonito recovered over 100,000 finished ornaments of turquoise, marine shell, jet, and shale, particularly concentrated in sumptuous burials and dedicatory offerings in ritual spaces (p. 92; Judd 1954; Pepper 1920).
- The consumption-to-production index for Pueblo Bonito (131.93) far exceeds the average for Chaco Project sites (21.67), indicating disproportionate ornament consumption at the great house (p. 107).

**Key observations:** Mattson 2025 provides the strongest direct evidence for ornament production as an elite activity at Chaco. The embedded production interpretation is important for the signaling model because it shows that the cost of producing signaling goods was borne by the elites who used them, consistent with the Spence condition requiring quality-dependent costs. If elites produced their own ornaments (or had household members do so), the cost of signaling through exotic goods was integrated into elite household production rather than extracted from non-elite households, making the signal more honest because the costs and benefits were internalized within the same agent.

### From Van Dyke et al. 2024

**Findings:**

- Conch-shell trumpets (Strombus sp. and Murex sp.) were procured from the Pacific Ocean, approximately 1000 km to the south-west. A 200mm-long shell produced a 96dB sound at 329.84Hz (pp. 779-780; Brown 2005; Loose 2012).
- Of the 46 known conch shells or fragments from the ancient Southwest, 17 were found at Chaco Canyon (37% of all known specimens). A conch-shell trumpet was found under a plank floor in Room 33 of Pueblo Bonito, the site's most elaborate burial (p. 780; Brown 2005:299-300; Mills and Ferguson 2008).
- Great house walls reaching up to 8m high, with caches of "carved and painted birds and flowers, cylinder jars that held cacao, decorated baskets, and turquoise and shell jewellery" (p. 778).

**Key observations:** The concentration of 37% of all known Southwest conch shells at Chaco Canyon, and their association with the most elaborate burial context at Pueblo Bonito, identifies conch-shell trumpets as among the highest-tier exotic goods in the Chaco signaling repertoire. Their procurement distance (1000 km) exceeds that of turquoise (200+ km) and is comparable to scarlet macaws (Mesoamerica), placing them at the extreme end of the signaling cost spectrum. The acoustic function adds a dimension not present in other exotic goods: conch shells were not just display items but active signaling instruments with a community-scale broadcast range.

### From Mills et al. 2018

**Findings:**

- Inequality was part of Chaco's social fabric dating to Pueblo Bonito's earliest construction in the ninth century AD. Several Chaco scholars have explicitly tied this inequality to immigration. Differences between the two major burial clusters at Pueblo Bonito (Akins 1986) and the contrast between small and great house architecture within the canyon are cited as evidence for people with disparate origins and identities (p. 923; citing Plog & Heitman 2010; Crown & Wills 2018; Wills 2009).
- Smudged brown and red wares occur in "unusual elongated bowl forms" from Pueblo Bonito's Room 32, a room containing hundreds of ceremonially retired items (Mills 2008), and from the Room 33 "debris," associated with multiple rich burials related through matrilineal descent (Kennett et al. 2017). Both examples are rare, unusual forms used for storage and retirement of ritual objects (pp. 929-930).
- The earliest smudged ware (Woodruff Smudged) was concentrated in Chaco Canyon great houses between AD 800 and 1000, not at small house sites. Crown (2016) argues iridescent smudged wares may have served as mirrors (p. 930).
- Chuskan ritual technologies from the Basketmaker III period, including "burden baskets (and their ceramic effigies), twined yucca sandals and wooden ritual objects" were distributed throughout the Chaco area by AD 850-900 (p. 929; citing Webster et al. 2014).

**Key observations:** Mills et al. 2018 strengthens the case for early elite status differentiation by documenting the concentration of ritual wares and ceremonial materials in great houses from the earliest period of Chaco's development. The restriction of specialized ceramic forms (Woodruff Smudged, elongated bowls) to great house contexts, and their association with elite burials (Room 33), supports the signaling model's assumption of quality variance among individuals from the system's origin. The distribution of Chuskan ritual technologies provides evidence for a shared ritual repertoire that extended across the emerging Chaco network, consistent with a signaling system that used standardized material symbols to communicate status.

### From Watson et al. 2015

**Findings:**

- New AMS 14C dates reveal scarlet macaw procurement at Pueblo Bonito began by approximately A.D. 900-975, 50-150 years earlier than previously assumed. Twelve of 14 sampled macaws predate the apogee of architectural expansion (A.D. 1040-1110) (p. 8238).
- 30 of 35 canyon macaws (86%) were recovered from Pueblo Bonito alone, demonstrating highly restricted access to this exotic good (p. 8238).
- Of 34 macaws from Chaco analyzed by Hargrave (1970), only a single macaw may have reached breeding age. Nestlings/juveniles/breeding-age birds comprise only 2.9% of the Chaco population vs. 14.0% at Paquime (z=1.82, P<0.05). No breeding pens or eggshells reported from Pueblo Bonito (pp. 8240-8241).
- Procurement distance: scarlet macaw range extends no further north than Gulf Coast of Tamaulipas (~1,800 km from Chaco) or Pacific coast of southern Oaxaca (~2,500 km) (p. 8238).
- Scarlet macaws are of particular significance in Pueblo cosmology: primary species marking directional association by color (red/orange), designating southern and/or nadir positions in Pueblo cosmological systems (p. 8240).
- AMS 14C dates for elite burials in Room 33 of Pueblo Bonito (from Plog and Heitman 2010) date to no later than A.D. 775-875, establishing hierarchical sociopolitical structures 200 years before the architectural florescence (p. 8239).
- Marine shell ornaments at Chaco number in the thousands; approximately two dozen copper bells or pieces of raw copper; cacao residue in distinctive vessels resembling Mesoamerican chocolate pots. Quantities are "orders of magnitude greater than in most sections of the northern SW" (p. 8239).

**Key observations:** Watson et al. 2015 provide the strongest chronological evidence for the early emergence of elite status signaling at Chaco. The finding that exotic goods acquisition preceded (by 50-150 years) the peak of monumental construction supports a model where individual-level status signaling (Layer 1) precedes and drives group-level monument signaling (Layer 2). The extreme concentration of macaws at Pueblo Bonito (86%) demonstrates quality-dependent access consistent with the Spence condition. The absence of local breeding means each macaw represents the full acquisition cost (1,800+ km travel, specialized avicultural knowledge), establishing macaw procurement as among the costliest individual signaling investments at Chaco. Multiple exotic goods types (macaws, shell, copper, cacao) represent redundant signaling channels, each requiring different procurement networks and reflecting different dimensions of "quality."

### From Van Dyke 2004

**Findings:**

- Near the end of the Classic Bonito phase, "burial evidence indicates material and nutritional differences between great house and small site inhabitants were at their most extreme" (p. 426; citing Akins 1986, 2003).
- Two Classic Bonito phase burial areas at Pueblo Bonito contain great wealth in the form of turquoise, shell, jet, wooden and other exotic items (p. 416; citing Akins 2003, Mathien 2003, Neitzel 2003b, Pepper 1909).
- Based on burial analyses, Akins (2003) postulates the existence of a three-tiered ascribed social hierarchy (p. 416).
- "Power need not be grounded exclusively in economics (Yoffee 2001); access to ritual knowledge is a basis for authority in ethnographic Pueblo societies" (p. 416; citing Brandt 1977, 1980; Levy 1992; Ortiz 1969; Whiteley 1985, 1986).

**Key observations:** Van Dyke 2004 provides independent support for the intensification of status differentiation over time, with inequality reaching its peak near the end of the Classic Bonito phase. The three-tiered social hierarchy inferred from burial analysis establishes that quality variance was substantial, a prerequisite for quality-dependent signaling. The emphasis on ritual knowledge (not just material wealth) as a basis for authority aligns with Watson et al.'s finding that macaw procurement signaled access to cosmological knowledge.

## Synthesis

The processed sources document elite status signaling through complementary lenses. Kantner & Vaughn 2012 provide Chaco-specific burial evidence showing restricted access to exotic goods and ritual authority. Wills 2017 adds genetic evidence for matrilineal dynasties and a labor-recruitment mechanism for status maintenance. Kennett et al. 2017 now provides the direct archaeogenomic evidence underlying the matrilineal dynasty claim: identical mitochondrial genomes across nine Room 33 individuals spanning approximately 330 years (800-1130 CE), with nuclear DNA confirming mother-daughter and grandmother-grandson relationships. This is the strongest available evidence for hereditary quality differentiation at Chaco, demonstrating that elite status was transmitted matrilineally from the founding of Pueblo Bonito through the system's collapse. The lineage founder (Burial 14) was interred with more than 11,200 turquoise pieces and more than 3,300 shell items, the richest burial in the North American Southwest, establishing that extreme status differentiation existed from the system's inception. Hawkes & Bliege Bird 2002 demonstrate from ethnographic data that signalers gain measurable social and reproductive benefits. Neiman 1997 provides a multidimensional characterization of "competitive ability." Barclay et al. 2021 add the important refinement that signaling reputation depends on proportional generosity (share of income, not absolute amount), the distinction between genuine and aggrandizing generosity, and the concept of asymmetric stake in cooperative relationships. Trigger 1990 (abstract only) provides the foundational argument that luxury goods production is a channel of conspicuous energy consumption in the same analytical class as monument construction. Watson et al. 2015 provide the critical chronological finding that exotic goods acquisition (macaws, by A.D. 900) preceded the peak of monumental construction (A.D. 1040-1110) by 50-150 years, with 86% of canyon macaws concentrated at a single site (Pueblo Bonito), and Van Dyke 2004 documents increasing inequality reaching its peak near the end of the Classic Bonito phase with a three-tiered social hierarchy. Together these sources support the project's Layer 1 prediction that signaling investment should be quality-dependent (the Spence condition), with quality understood as a composite of resource control, network connectivity, and ritual authority, and with proportional investment relative to capacity as the signal that matters. The Kennett et al. 2017 finding that quality is heritable (matrilineal) strengthens the case for stable separating equilibria: because signalers' type is correlated across generations, the signaling system does not need to be re-established each generation.

### From Safi 2015

**Findings:**

- Great houses have higher proportional use of visually distinctive smudged brown and red wares compared to community household sites dominated by white wares. This differential ceramic use may have marked great house activities as notably separate from everyday activities (pp. 151, 154-155)
- The use of red and smudged brown wares at the Largo Gap great house "may have been an important component of a costly signal" with use restricted to great house leaders or other influential community members (p. 155)
- Great house construction served as a credibility-enhancing display (Henrich 2009) that facilitated the recruitment and retention of group members, increased community prestige, and increased social and trade relationships with neighbors (p. 58)
- All three great house communities display multi-ethnic settlement (Mogollon and Pueblo traditions), suggesting great houses attracted diverse populations (pp. 153-154)
- Aspiring leaders form kin-based coalitions that mobilize local resources to support the leader's activities; kin members reap more benefits from contributing resources to a leader's bid for power than general group members (pp. 56-57; citing Kantner 1996)

**Key observations:** Safi's ceramic evidence showing differential access to visually distinctive wares at great houses supports the interpretation that great house leaders maintained special access to display items used during signaling events (feasts, rituals). The multi-ethnic settlement pattern is consistent with Layer 1's prediction that signals attract followers across social boundaries. The aspiring leader/kin-coalition dynamic provides a mechanism for how individual signaling translates into group-level investment.

### From Plog & Heitman 2010

**Findings:**

- New AMS radiocarbon dates demonstrate that Burials 13 and 14 in Room 33 at Pueblo Bonito date to the late ninth century (combined medians AD 781 and AD 873), approximately 150-200 years earlier than the previously assumed post-AD 1020 date (pp. 19623; Table 1).
- Burial 14 was interred with more than 10,000 turquoise beads, 500 turquoise pendants, and 3,000 shell beads and pendants, along with a shell trumpet. Burial 13 had nearly 6,000 turquoise beads and pendants (p. 19624).
- More than 25,000 turquoise pieces were recovered from Room 33 alone, exceeding all other prehistoric Southwestern sites combined (p. 19622; citing Snow 1973).
- Room 33 was used as a mortuary crypt for approximately 300-400 years (late ninth through late eleventh or early twelfth centuries), with interments stratigraphically spanning 1.2 m of deposit (pp. 19622-19623).
- Delta-15N values for Room 33 burials (10.0-12.4 per mil) indicate higher animal protein intake than regional averages, a pattern commonly associated with elites (p. 19625; Table 1; citing Coltrain et al. 2007).
- The disparity between great house burials (restricted to rooms, few individuals with rich assemblages) and small house burials (in trash middens, common but with modest goods) reflects cultural rules restricting great house burial to high-status individuals (pp. 19625).
- Corner caches of artifacts in Room 33 included 1,500+ turquoise pieces, wooden ceremonial sticks, eight of nine flutes, and human mandibles arranged in directional pattern referencing Pueblo cosmological organization (p. 19625).

**Key observations:** Plog & Heitman 2010 provides the critical chronological evidence that elite status differentiation was present from the founding of Pueblo Bonito, not just during the florescence. The concentration of 25,000+ turquoise pieces (raw material sourced 170+ km from Cerrillos Hills) in a 2x2 m room demonstrates extreme quality-dependent access to the costliest signaling medium. The 300+ year continuity of elite burial, subsequently confirmed genetically by Kennett et al. 2017, supports a stable signaling equilibrium rather than an ephemeral phenomenon. The dietary evidence (elevated delta-15N) provides an independent line of evidence for status differentiation beyond material wealth.

### From Crown 2018

**Findings:**

- Cylinder jars at Pueblo Bonito contained residue of cacao and/or holly, imported caffeinated beverages that were "probably the highest-status drinks in Chaco Canyon after around AD 900" (p. 400; citing Crown et al. 2015; Crown and Hurst 2009).
- Roughly 200 cylinder jars found in Chaco Canyon, with approximately 175 from Pueblo Bonito; none from kivas (p. 397; Table 1). Room 28 alone contained 112 vessels.
- Cylinder jars were corporate property, not individual possessions: only one was associated with an individual burial (adult female, Skeleton #5 in Room 33), and most were recovered from ceremonial or performative deposits (p. 398).
- Outsiders brought cylinder jars made from their own traditional wares to Pueblo Bonito, where the jars remained (p. 399). At least four Chuska White Ware jars, four Showlow Red jars, one Flagstaff B/W jar, and one Black Mesa B/W jar were imported. The form was "neither widely traded prestige items nor widely emulated" outside Chaco (p. 399).
- The shift from large communal cylinder jars (average orifice diameter 15.1 cm) to smaller individual vessels (average 9.8 cm) crafted in matched sets of 2-4 occurred around AD 1030 (p. 394; Figure 4).
- The drinking ritual system terminated abruptly around AD 1100 when 99 cylinder jars were placed on a shelf in Room 28, sprinkled with turquoise and shell beads, and deliberately burned (p. 398). This "concentrated termination" indicates "the end of their ritual use and perhaps the end of the competitive events in which they were used" (p. 402).
- The group that controlled cylinder jar production and use at Pueblo Bonito may have used this control to help establish hierarchy: "one group had control over their use and the knowledge and power associated with it" (p. 402; citing Mills 2004:240, 247).
- The intensification of drinking activity occurred "when construction events were at their height" (p. 402; citing Lekson 1984).

**Key observations:** Crown 2018 documents a signaling channel (ritual drinking with exotic beverages) that operated in parallel with monument construction and exotic goods acquisition. Cacao and holly, imported from Mesoamerica, represent individual-level costly signals: the ingredients required long-distance procurement, specialized preparation knowledge, and access to exclusive ritual settings at Pueblo Bonito. The corporate ownership of cylinder jars and the concentration of nearly all vessels at a single site demonstrate quality-dependent access consistent with the Spence condition. The temporal correlation between drinking ritual intensification and peak construction suggests coordinated deployment of multiple signaling channels. The abrupt termination around AD 1100 provides a precise chronological marker for the breakdown of one component of the signaling system.

### From Schwarz 2023

**Findings:**

- Imported fineware ceramics (Plumbate, Fine Orange, Fine Gray) constituted costly signals in the Terminal Classic and Early Postclassic Maya. These finewares made up only 0.3-0.58% of ceramic assemblages at seven Peten Lakes sites (pp. 16-18; Table 1), indicating rarity consistent with restricted elite access.
- Obsidian functioned as a costly signal of elevated status among the Classic Maya: obsidian-to-chert ratios at major Classic centers reached 1.2 (Seibal) while rural hamlets had ratios as low as 0.03. By the Postclassic, ratios converged to 0.05-0.19 across community types (pp. 21-22; citing Schwarz 2013a: Table 2; Kingsley 2014:205-206).
- "It appears that a resource that was restricted greatly to the elites and elite control in the Classic period was less restricted, and new strategies for tool procurement were enacted in the Postclassic" (p. 22).
- The acquisition of Plumbate pottery was "not just an expensive acquisition, but an acquisition of a product whose qualities were not easily successfully copied or knocked-off" (p. 17; citing Neff 2014:6), satisfying the unfakeability criterion for costly signals.
- Slipped ceramics in the Postclassic served as markers of both status and ethnicity, with archaeometric provenance linking "certain Postclassic slipped ceramic types with individual sites, lake basins, and ethnic groups" (p. 19; citing Cecil 2001; Cecil and Neff 2006).

**Key observations:** Schwarz 2023 provides the most detailed comparative case for exotic goods as individual-level costly signals. The convergence of obsidian-to-chert ratios from the Classic to the Postclassic provides a quantitative template for testing whether similar signal degradation occurred at Chaco. At Chaco, turquoise is the closest analogue to Maya obsidian: both were imported from distant sources, both were concentrated in elite contexts during the florescence, and both had intrinsic material qualities that made them difficult to counterfeit. Mattson (2025) documents the concentration of turquoise production tools in elite rooms at Pueblo Bonito, suggesting restricted access. The Plumbate case (difficult to replicate due to specialized glazing technology) parallels the turquoise case (distinctive material properties not replicable with local stone). The dual function of Postclassic slipped ceramics (status markers + ethnic markers) is relevant to understanding how signaling media can serve multiple functions simultaneously, a pattern also seen at Chaco where Chuska ceramics mark both exchange participation and regional identity.
