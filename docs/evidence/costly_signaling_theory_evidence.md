## Evidence

### From Kantner & Vaughn 2012

**Findings:**

- Four criteria for costly signaling: (1) within-group variance in unobservable attribute, (2) observers benefit from reliable information, (3) higher-quality signalers benefit from broadcasting but deception is possible, (4) cost/benefit correlated with signaler quality (p. 70; from Boone 1998, Smith & Bliege Bird 2003)
- Pilgrimage centers are costly signals of leader status and supernatural authority (p. 66)
- The pilgrimage act is a costly signal of pilgrim commitment, facilitating cooperation among strangers (p. 66)
- The two signal types (center and pilgrimage) "synergistically fuel one another," with leader status and pilgrim reputation as distinct signaled qualities (p. 73)
- Extension of CST beyond competitive prowess: "it is not necessary that the hidden quality play a potential role in competitive interactions; it can also factor into prosocial behavior" (p. 70)

**Key observations:** Kantner & Vaughn provide the only direct application of CST to Chaco. Their dual-signal framework (center = status, pilgrimage = commitment) anticipates the multi-scale structure of the mls-monuments model, but remains verbal rather than formal. The prosocial extension of CST is important for Layer 3 (cooperation networks).

### From Wills 2017

**Findings:**

- Wills argues that great house corporate groups grew through labor recruitment rather than surplus extraction: "Financing great house construction by extracting labor surpluses through recruitment to corporately owned territories is a more efficient and less costly explanation than extracting both food and labor surpluses from households occupying a difficult farming environment" (p. 399)
- The labor-recruitment model resolves the "circular argument" of surplus-extraction models where "surpluses are not observed but rather inferred from energy investments, which are explained by surpluses" (p. 395)
- Stone and Downum (1999) proposed that great house communities recruited new members "through the granting of access to agricultural areas and protection of those rights," creating a "self-reinforcing sociopolitical process" (p. 394)
- Great houses outside the Chaco Core may have "marked or signaled corporate control under conditions of increasing population and perhaps escalating competition for labor" (p. 398)
- The Chaco Core as a "community of communities" (Toll 2006, 2008) formed the central node of a settlement system "organized around property rights in dispersed agricultural fields" (p. 400)

**Key observations:** Although Wills 2017 does not explicitly use costly signaling theory, the labor-recruitment model he proposes is structurally compatible with the signaling framework. Great houses as costly signals would function precisely as Wills describes: attracting labor and members to corporate groups by demonstrating the group's capacity and commitment. The self-reinforcing dynamics (more members leads to more construction leads to more recruitment) map onto the positive feedback loop between signaling investment and cooperation network value in Layer 3 of the model. Wills's use of the word "signaled" (p. 398) regarding outlier great houses marking corporate control suggests an implicit signaling logic, even though the paper does not formally engage with CST.

### From Penn & Szamado 2020

**Findings:**

- The Handicap Principle (signals are honest because they are wasteful/costly) has no theoretical or empirical support and should be retired (p. 267). This challenges any framing of Chaco monument investment as "handicap signaling."
- Zahavi's (1977b) second hypothesis (condition-dependent signaling with differential marginal costs) is theoretically sound and distinct from the handicap hypothesis (p. 277). This is what the Spence condition formalizes.
- Grafen's (1990a) widely-cited model is "virtually identical to Spence's" (1973) job-market signaling model (p. 282). The mls-monuments model's Spence condition is thus on established theoretical ground.
- "Signalling costs paid at the equilibrium are neither sufficient nor necessary to maintain signal reliability" (p. 283; citing Hurd 1995, Szamado 1999, Lachmann et al. 2001). Observing high absolute costs (C=0.35 for Chaco) does not itself explain why signals are honest; differential costs are what matters.
- Signal honesty can also be maintained through "differential benefits" (p. 283; citing Godfray 1991, Getty 1998a). This is relevant to the lambda(sigma) mechanism: the benefits of monument investment (cooperation network access) may be differentially greater for higher-quality groups.
- Enquist (1985) "established the foundations of ESS signalling theory" and distinguished between "performance" (index) and "choice" (strategic) signals (p. 280). Monument construction may combine both: physical capacity constrains construction (index component), while investment level is chosen (strategic component).
- Veblen's (1899) conspicuous consumption theory parallels the handicap hypothesis (p. 276), and shares similar logical weaknesses. Exotic goods signaling at Chaco is better explained by condition-dependent models than by conspicuous waste.
- Acceptance of handicap conclusions involved "affirming the consequent" (p. 286): observing costly signals and inferring that costliness is the mechanism maintaining honesty is logically invalid. The model must demonstrate the specific mechanism.

**Key observations:** Penn & Szamado 2020 confirms that the mls-monuments model's theoretical approach (Spence condition, differential cost structure) is correct and on stronger theoretical ground than the handicap framing. The project should avoid "handicap principle" language and frame itself within the condition-dependent/strategic choice signaling tradition. The finding that differential benefits can also maintain honesty provides an additional theoretical pathway through the lambda(sigma) mechanism.

### From Quinn 2019

**Findings:**

- Four necessary conditions for honest signaling: (1) signalers vary in quality relevant to receivers; (2) signals are observable and associated with quality; (3) signals are costly and cost-benefit depends on quality; (4) both parties obtain net fitness benefits (pp. 276-277; from Grafen 1990, Bliege Bird & Smith 2005)
- The handicap principle (Zahavi 1975) was formalized by Grafen (1990), proving that honest signaling equilibrium is an evolutionarily stable strategy (pp. 276-277)
- Distinction between assessment signals (cost inherent in production) and conventional signals (cost imposed through punishment): many signals may be assessment signals rather than pure handicaps (p. 277; from Maynard Smith & Harper 2003)
- Altruistic signals (providing direct benefits to receivers) have different equilibrium structure from purely wasteful displays; they can stabilize signaling even with weaker cost-quality relationships (pp. 277-278)
- Three main archaeological domains: unconditional generosity/feasting, monumental architecture, and prestige goods/craft traditions (pp. 278-279)
- Most archaeological CST applications "remain largely qualitative and descriptive" rather than formal; calls for "a more rigorous analytical framework" (pp. 282-283)
- Central challenge: "it is often difficult or impossible to demonstrate signal honesty" archaeologically; demonstrating quality-dependent costs requires linking costs to variation in signaler quality (pp. 281-282)
- Safi (2015) applied CST to Chaco outlier great houses [CITE-CHECK: Safi 2015] (p. 280)
- CST predictions often converge with alternative frameworks; distinguishing tests needed (pp. 285-286)

**Key observations:** Quinn provides the most comprehensive recent review of CST in archaeology and identifies the exact methodological gaps the project's formal model addresses. The four-condition framework maps onto the model's Spence condition and handicap principle. The call for formal models with quantitative predictions directly motivates the three-layer approach. The note on Safi (2015) is a lead for a direct CST application to Chaco that should be tracked down.

### From Hawkes & Bliege Bird 2002

**Findings:**

- The handicap principle makes waste a common outcome of signal selection because "the capacity to bear the cost reveals the show-off's hidden qualities" (p. 58)
- Critical distinction between information-only displays and altruistic signals that "provide benefits to the audience in addition to information" (p. 58; from Zahavi 1995)
- "Collective-action problems do not arise in handicap models because it is mutually beneficial to both show-off and audience to have the information about the show-off's qualities revealed" (p. 61)
- "Social prestige functions like a peacock's tail or the song of a songbird. It attracts collaborators and deters rivals" (p. 61; quoting Zahavi 1995)
- Audience/receiver bias shapes signal form: signals designed for group-level prestige should be directed at the group at large; "broadcast effectiveness" favors public goods as signal media (p. 65)
- Altruistic signals may be preferred by receivers over purely wasteful displays because they provide "more than simply information" (p. 64)
- Individual competitive advantage through costly signaling produces collective benefits: "the showoff hypothesis and costly signaling can help explain how individuals seeking competitive advantages can increase their own standing and so earn preferential treatment by acting in ways that supply highly valued benefits to others" (p. 65)

**Key observations:** Three insights are central for the project. First, CST resolves collective action problems without reciprocity, explaining why great house construction can be maintained without systems of mutual obligation. Second, the altruistic signal concept (providing direct benefits beyond information) characterizes great houses that served as ceremonial/storage/community facilities. Third, the dual function (attracting allies + deterring rivals) maps directly onto Layers 2 and 3 of the model.

### From Neiman 1997

**Findings:**

- Conspicuous consumption is wasteful in a Darwinian sense because it represents fitness costs diverted from maintenance, mate acquisition, and offspring production (pp. 269-270)
- "Investment in waste does not confer a direct selective advantage on the individual making it"; advantage comes from the information conveyed to receivers about the sender's hidden quality (p. 270)
- Two key properties of honest signals: (1) advertising is on average honest (individuals of greater ability advertise more); (2) advertising is costly (costs scale with amount of advertising and guarantee honesty) (p. 270)
- Three factors control advertising intensity: competitive ability of worst advertiser, variance in competitive ability, and payoff to advertising investment (pp. 270-271)
- Greater variance in competitive ability leads to more advertising; larger proportion of non-kin in audience increases payoff (pp. 270-271)
- Maya monuments were "engineered to be impossible to counterfeit" through costs of supporting literate elite, skilled carvers, and construction labor (p. 271)
- An explicit mechanistic theoretical framework "facilitates model construction -- the valid deduction of precise empirical expectations" (p. 285)

**Key observations:** Neiman provides the foundational framework for applying CST to monumental architecture. The three-factor model of advertising intensity provides testable predictions for Chaco: advertising should increase with quality variance and audience size (especially non-kin), and decrease when ecological conditions reduce the range of political quality. The emphasis on mechanistic theory over "covert, common-sense assumptions" parallels the project's approach.

### From Barclay et al. 2021

**Findings:**

- Costly helping honestly signals one's stake in another's welfare (fitness interdependence), extending CST from quality/ability signals to intent/stake signals (Section 1)
- The welfare trade-off ratio (WTR) condition (WTR x b > c) provides a more general foundation for the signaling cost condition than the traditional handicap principle (Section 1a)
- Formal equilibrium condition (Inequality 6): V_st(b+R-P)+(R-P) > c > V_su(b+S-P)+(T-P), specifying that signal cost must deter untrustworthy senders but remain affordable for trustworthy senders (Section 2e)
- Low-cost signals can be honest when temptation to defect is low (Stag Hunts, Harmony Games); expensive signals needed only in Snowdrift Games (Section 2e)
- Signaling emerges at intermediate levels of interdependence: "highly interdependent pairs will have no signalling because receivers do not demand signals, whereas weakly interdependent pairs will have no signalling because senders are unwilling to pay costs" (Section 2g)
- Signals need not benefit receivers (effective at b = 0): "The 'help' need not even provide any real benefit to the receiver" (Section 3)
- Signaling predicted to be costlier "where communities are more fluid and residentially mobile, conflict and competition are more common" (Section 3)
- Signals can evolve from cues: "if a cue is correlated with some hidden trait, observers will start attending to that cue, whereupon it pays to invest in actively signalling that trait" (Section 3)
- Martu Aboriginal women's sharing provides empirical illustration: proportion of income shared (not absolute amount) determines reputation for generosity (Section 1b; Bliege Bird et al. 2012, 2018)

**Key observations:** Barclay et al. 2021 provides the formal theoretical foundation for Layer 3 of the mls-monuments model. Three results are directly incorporated: (1) the intent-signaling extension justifies treating monument construction as signaling cooperative intent, not just competitive quality; (2) the medium-interdependence result generates spatial predictions about where signaling should occur (boundaries of the interaction network, not among already-familiar neighbors); (3) the b = 0 result provides formal justification for why "non-functional" monument investment is adaptive. The four-game-type analysis suggests the Stag Hunt best characterizes Chaco intergroup dynamics.

### From Gauthier 2021

**Findings:**

- Free-riders who avoid the effort of maintaining social networks can damage critical social infrastructure when it is most needed. A simulation approach could better capture these processes. (p. 8; citing Kohler and West 1996)
- Model residuals display evidence of transitivity and triad closure, with more closed triangle structures than expected by chance. (p. 8)
- Ceramic assemblage similarity, measured by Jensen-Shannon divergence, serves as an inverse proxy for information flow. The measure is agnostic about mechanism (trade, migration, shared history, copying). (pp. 2-3)

The mls-monuments model fills this gap: costly signals of cooperative intent (monument construction, exotic goods acquisition) deter free-riders from accessing cooperation networks. The triad closure in model residuals is consistent with signaling-based partner selection, where well-connected, signal-emitting groups preferentially form mutual ties.

### From O'Shea & Halstead 1989 and Halstead & O'Shea 1989

**Findings:**

- Four basic categories of buffering mechanism: mobility, diversification, physical storage, and exchange. Storage and mobility are mutually incompatible; most societies deploy an array of strategies in a hierarchy of responses. (Halstead & O'Shea 1989, p. 4)
- Storage includes "indirect storage" through durable valuables, craft goods, and currencies, where present abundance is converted through social transactions into future obligations. The linking of durable valuables to buffering in "social storage" provides a platform for the unequal accumulation of wealth, status, and power. (O'Shea & Halstead 1989, pp. 123, 125)
- There is strong selective pressure for communities to embed "emergency" buffering mechanisms as regular aspects of the cultural repertoire, but this embedding can transform or undermine the latent buffering function. High-level mechanisms may be embedded to the point where they are irreversibly transformed, with radical consequences. (Halstead & O'Shea 1989, p. 4)
- Much of the "normal surplus" occasionally deployed as a buffer against scarcity was usually committed to the production of non-subsistence commodities. This created an incentive for continued overproduction even when buffering seemed unnecessary, but also obscured the risk-buffering role of the institution. (O'Shea & Halstead 1989, p. 125)
- When selective forces acting on high-level decision makers differ from those on producers, a structural conflict exists. Upper-level mechanisms may exacerbate shortage at the local level when centres are insulated from shortages or in the case of a parasitic elite. (O'Shea & Halstead 1989, p. 124)
- Societies face a trade-off between security (ability to withstand severe/rare fluctuations) and efficiency (normal productivity and avoiding investment in rarely-used precautions). (Halstead & O'Shea 1989, p. 4)

**Key observations:** The Halstead and O'Shea framework contributes three concepts directly relevant to the signaling model. First, the "social storage" interpretation of exotic goods provides an alternative/complementary framing: turquoise, macaws, and other exotics at Chaco may function simultaneously as costly signals and as stored social capital convertible during crises. Second, the embedding dynamic explains why great house construction persists during favorable periods: construction activity maintains the cooperation network as a latent buffering mechanism, consistent with the signaling model's prediction that ongoing investment is required to keep networks functional. Third, the security-efficiency trade-off is formalized as the cost parameter C: diverting 35% of productivity to signaling increases crisis-buffering capacity but reduces normal productivity, and the optimal C depends on sigma.

### From Van Dyke et al. 2024

**Findings:**

- The modelled reach of a conch-shell trumpet blown from atop a Chacoan great house consistently maps onto the distribution of associated habitation sites across five communities (pp. 777, 788). Statistical tests confirm the patterns are highly unlikely due to chance (p <= 0.001 at Kin Klizhin, Pierre's).
- The consistency across all five communities (chi-squared = 2.50, p = 0.645) indicates a system-wide phenomenon, not a local anomaly (p. 788).
- Conch-shell trumpets are themselves long-distance exotic goods (Pacific Ocean, ~1000 km), with 17 of 46 known Southwest specimens concentrated at Chaco Canyon (p. 780).
- The acoustic broadcast range of approximately 1418m from a great house defines a community boundary, with habitation sites non-randomly located within this audibility zone (p. 783).

**Key observations:** Van Dyke et al. 2024 provide empirical evidence for the broadcast dimension of great house signaling. In the signaling model, great houses serve as platforms from which information about group quality is broadcast to surrounding populations. The soundshed analysis provides a literal, physical manifestation of this concept: a conch-shell blast from a great house physically reaches the surrounding community. The consistent mapping of audibility zones onto community boundaries suggests that the spatial organization of Chacoan communities was structured, in part, around the signaling reach of the great house. This supports the model's Layer 2 (intergroup assessment), where monument signals provide information to surrounding groups about the signaler's capacity and status.

### From Wills et al. 2022

**Findings:**

- The paper distinguishes between literal communication "signaling" (smoke, fire, light) and the complex transactional face-to-face communication required for construction labor mobilization (p. 474).
- Light signals were deemed "unlikely to have had a necessary or significant role in mobilizing construction workers" because they could convey only binary information, while construction coordination required complex information about where, when, and who (p. 474).
- Great houses outside the lower portion of Chaco Canyon were often not intervisible, undermining the visual signaling interpretation (p. 474; Dungan et al. 2018).
- The Chaco Core's grid-like network of time pathways provided multiple communication routes between great houses, suggesting information flow was not hierarchically constrained (pp. 465-466, 474).

**Key observations:** Wills et al. 2022 use "signaling" in the literal communication sense (transmitting messages), which is distinct from costly signaling theory (conveying information about hidden qualities through costly displays). However, their findings are relevant to the CST framework: the spatial structure of the communication network (grid-like, with multiple redundant pathways) suggests that information about great house communities was widely available across the Chaco region, which is a precondition for signal effectiveness in the evolutionary model. If signals were only visible/audible to nearby neighbors, the audience for costly signals would be too small to justify the investment. The LCA results show that information could traverse the entire Chaco network in a few hours, ensuring that great house construction was "broadcast" (in the signaling theory sense) to a large audience.

### From Dungan et al. 2018

**Findings:**

- Great houses were systematically placed in high-visibility locations (approximately half in the 8th-10th visibility deciles), while great kivas show a more even distribution across moderate and lower deciles. K-S tests confirm the difference from background landscape visibility is significant at alpha = 0.001 (pp. 913-914; Table 1).
- Great houses and great kivas differ significantly from each other in visibility placement (K-S Max D = 0.16, significant at alpha = 0.001), despite both being forms of monumental architecture (p. 914).
- The visibility preference is geographically consistent across the entire Chaco World, from Chaco Canyon to outlier communities (Table 2; Figure 8).
- The "legibility" of great houses was "frequently enhanced through the choice of setting; great houses were intended to be seen by individuals across the wider landscape, rather than only by viewers in the immediate vicinity of the building" (p. 918).
- Great kivas, by contrast, were experienced primarily from within as communal religious venues; their monumentality was not designed for landscape-scale visibility (p. 916).

**Key observations:** Dungan et al. 2018 provide the first large-scale empirical test of signal observability for Chaco architecture. One of the four necessary conditions for honest signaling (Quinn 2019, p. 277; Bliege Bird & Smith 2005) is that signals must be observable and associated with quality. The finding that great house builders consistently selected high-visibility locations demonstrates that observability was engineered into the signal system, not incidental. The contrast between great houses (external visibility for distant audiences) and great kivas (internal experience for proximate participants) maps onto different signaling functions: Layer 2 (intergroup assessment, which requires the signal to be visible from outside the community) vs. within-community ritual coordination. The broadcast effectiveness concept from Hawkes & Bliege Bird 2002 (signals directed at the group at large should use public, highly visible media) is directly supported.

### From Mills et al. 2018

**Findings:**

- Dynamic social network analysis of 325 sites across the Chaco World shows that network connectivity peaked during AD 1050-1100, the same period as peak monument construction. More nodes and more connected nodes are present than in any other 50-year interval (p. 931; Figure 7).
- Chaco's earliest networks (AD 800-900) show connections primarily to the south and west, not to the Northern San Juan, contradicting the northern migration hypothesis (pp. 927-929).
- The San Juan River defined a persistent connectivity gap throughout Chaco's history, eventually bridged via the Great North Road and Aztec in the late AD 1000s-1100s (pp. 927-931).
- Inequality was part of Chaco's social fabric from the ninth century, with the contrast between great house and small house architecture cited as evidence for "the presence of people with disparate origins and identities" (p. 923; citing Plog & Heitman 2010; Wills 2009).

**Key observations:** Mills et al. 2018 provides the empirical foundation for Layer 3 (cooperation networks) by documenting the temporal and spatial evolution of the Chaco network. The co-occurrence of peak network connectivity and peak monument construction (AD 1050-1100) supports the model's prediction that signaling investment and cooperation network value co-evolved. The persistent connectivity gap at the San Juan River and its eventual bridging via specific infrastructure (Great North Road) provide a spatial mechanism for how signaling-based cooperation networks expand: signals may be particularly important at network boundaries where prior relationships are weak (consistent with Barclay et al. 2021's medium-interdependence prediction).

### From Enquist & Leimar 1983 (original sequential assessment paper)

**Note:** This is the foundational 1983 paper (J. theor. Biol. 102:387-410) that introduced the sequential assessment model. A separate entry below covers the 1987 follow-up (J. theor. Biol. 127:187-205), which extends the model to incorporate resource value variation.

**Findings:**

- The sequential assessment model formalizes how contestants with incomplete information resolve conflicts through sequential costly interactions, each providing a noisy observation of relative fighting ability theta = ln(c_B/c_A). Observation noise is normally distributed with standard deviation sigma, and after n steps the sampling error reduces to sigma/sqrt(n). (pp. 390-391)
- Per-step costs are exponential in theta: c_A = c exp(-theta/2) and c_B = c exp(theta/2). Small differences in relative fighting ability produce large cost asymmetries during extended contests, making accurate pre-contest assessment highly valuable. (p. 391, Equation 2)
- The ESS is a switching line in causal factor space: "When an animal's trajectory goes below the switching line that animal gives up" (p. 392). The ESS is pure and unique for given parameters c and sigma. (p. 394)
- Assessment-based fighting produces "good fighting economy": expected cost is 0.049 (only ~10% of the contested resource value of 1.0), with expected utility 0.451 for the population using the ESS. (p. 395)
- Wars of attrition (no assessment) yield zero expected utility; assessment-based strategies are strictly superior. "A common feature of these models is that the ESS is a mixed strategy and that the expected utility of fighting prior to the fight is zero." (p. 401)
- Contests between equally matched opponents are the costliest: "it is more costly to meet an opponent of equal strength than one that is stronger. This is due to the assessment taking place during the fight." (p. 396)
- Higher sigma produces costlier contests. Table 1 (p. 399) quantifies this: for c = 0.005, expected cost rises from 0.029 (sigma = 0.5) to 0.049 (sigma = 1.0) to 0.063 (sigma = 1.5). Higher c also increases expected cost (0.049 for c = 0.005 to 0.095 for c = 0.02).
- Probability of correct outcome is near-perfect for |theta| > 0.5 but errors occur for closely matched contestants. (p. 396, Fig. 4)
- Assessment-based strategies are evolutionarily stable against convention-based alternatives. A convention mutant (defer in intruder role) earns U(C, S) = 1/2 * U(S, S), strictly less than U(S, S). (p. 402)
- The causal factor state compresses to a sufficient statistic: "the sampling average x and the number of samplings n sum up the functionally relevant information contained in the sequence." (p. 404)

**Key observations:** The 1983 paper is the foundational source for the sequential assessment mechanism that underpins Layer 2. Three contributions are central to the costly signaling model. First, the sigma-cost relationship provides the formal basis for the claim that monument signals reduce conflict costs: reducing effective sigma from 1.5 to 0.5 cuts expected conflict costs by more than half (Table 1). Monument signals operate as a sigma-reducing technology by providing pre-contest information about group quality. Second, the comparison with the war of attrition quantifies the selective value of assessment: zero expected utility without assessment vs. 0.451 with assessment. Monument signals further improve on raw assessment by providing information before any costly interaction. Third, the equal-opponent cost result generates the spatial prediction that signaling should be most intensive where similarly matched groups coexist. Assessment errors (and therefore conflict costs) peak at theta = 0, precisely the situation among canyon core great house communities. The evolutionary stability of assessment against conventions (p. 402) ensures the mechanism is robust. Penn & Szamado (2020, p. 280) note that Enquist (1985), building on this 1983 paper, "established the foundations of ESS signalling theory," placing this work at the origin of formal signaling theory.

### From Enquist & Leimar 1987 (filed as Enquist_Leimar_1983)

**Findings:**

- The fundamental utility equation U = pV - C (p. 188) formalizes the trade-off at the core of costly signaling: the cost C of a signal (or a contest) must be offset by the probability-weighted value pV of the outcome. For the Chaco model, the cost C of monument construction is offset by the deterrence value (reduced conflict probability p) times the resource value V.
- The sequential assessment model demonstrates that the cost of acquiring information about relative quality scales with assessment noise sigma: "If the cost of acquiring information about relative fighting ability is decreased, either by decreasing sigma or c, the ESS changes so that the switching lines come closer together" (p. 195). Monument signals function as a sigma-reducing technology: they provide low-cost information about group quality prior to any direct interaction.
- ESS strategies become "more daring" as resource value V increases (p. 202, Appendix A). This means the cost C(V, V) of a contest increases with V, establishing the quality-dependent cost structure that the Spence condition requires: higher-quality groups (higher V) face higher absolute signaling costs but obtain proportionally greater benefits from honest signaling.
- The empirical validation across 18 species (Table 1, p. 199) showing that fight duration, offensive behavior, and probability of winning all increase with resource value provides cross-taxonomic support for the general principle that investment in costly displays scales with the value of what is at stake.

**Key observations:** Enquist & Leimar provide the game-theoretic foundation linking costly signaling to conflict reduction. The sequential assessment model is not itself a signaling model (it models direct contests), but the information-acquisition mechanism it formalizes is the same mechanism that monument signals exploit: by providing pre-contest information about relative quality, signals substitute for costly assessment rounds. The sigma parameter bridges the contest model and the signaling model: in the contest model, sigma is inherent measurement noise; in the signaling model, sigma can be reduced by the quality and visibility of the signal. Penn & Szamado (2020, p. 280) note that Enquist (1985), a related paper, "established the foundations of ESS signalling theory," placing this body of work at the origin of formal signaling theory in evolutionary biology.

### From Safi 2015

**Findings:**

- Great houses functioned as costly signals at two levels: (1) to attract/retain group members (internal audience) and (2) to signal competitive qualities to competing leaders (external audience) (Table 3.1, p. 50)
- Three viewer responses to competitive signals: accurate assessment leading to investment; recognition of superiority leading to reduced competition; escalation of own signal to exceed rival's (p. 48, from Neiman 1997:270)
- Derives 17 data expectations to distinguish CST from three alternative models (Chacoan outpost, Chaco emulation, ideological system); of these, 11 supported at community scale, 3 at individual great house scale (Table 3.2, pp. 60-61; p. 228)
- Great house serves as a "stable, physical signal of ability that could be reinforced by regular costly displays" such as feasting and ritual (p. 59)
- Costly signaling currently better accounts for the distribution and use of Chaco-style great houses than the Chaco-directed, emulation, or ideological system models (pp. 261-262, 271)
- The quality-dependent nature of signal investment is visible in architectural variation: "poor emulation" (low-quality materials, smaller structures, lack of veneers) represents a weak costly signal rather than failed emulation (p. 262)
- Construction during productive periods, not during environmental downturns, suggesting surplus availability rather than environmental stress drives construction timing (p. 56)

**Key observations:** Safi 2015 is the only other study besides Kantner & Vaughn 2012 to directly apply CST to Chaco outlier great houses. Her two-level signaling framework (internal: attract members; external: deter rivals/form alliances) anticipates our three-layer model's structure, though she does not formalize it or separate intergroup assessment (Layer 2) from cooperation networks (Layer 3). The 17 data expectations provide the most systematic empirical evaluation of CST for Chaco, but the model remains entirely verbal. Our formal approach advances beyond Safi by specifying equilibrium conditions, modeling environmental uncertainty as a parameter (sigma), and generating quantitative predictions. Her finding that construction occurs during productive rather than stressful periods requires engagement in our manuscript: we argue that environmental uncertainty (not current productivity) modulates signaling returns via lambda(sigma).

### From Gintis, Smith & Bowles 2001

**Findings:**

- Cooperation among unrelated individuals evolves because it constitutes an honest signal of underlying quality. The model involves no repeated or assortative interactions, so non-cooperation would be dominant without signaling benefits (p. 1, abstract).
- The honest signaling equilibrium requires the Spence-equivalent condition: pc' > s > pc and h > l, which can be rewritten as c' > s/p > c (Equation 4, p. 8). The cost for low-quality types must exceed the alliance benefit per unit signaler frequency, while the cost for high-quality types must be less.
- The equilibrium is frequency-dependent on the proportion p of high-quality types: honest signaling exists only when p is in the interval (s/c', s/c) (Theorem 1, p. 8). This frequency dependence was absent from Grafen's (1990a,b) pioneering model.
- Both signaling and non-signaling equilibria can be simultaneously stable (Theorem 2, p. 12). A ridge line in the phase space separates the basins of attraction (Figure 2, p. 13). Movement from nonsignaling to signaling requires perturbation across this boundary.
- Honest signaling by providing a public good enhances average group fitness when g(n-1) > c (Equation 5, p. 9). The signaling equilibrium has higher average payoffs even when conferring harm (g < 0), provided sufficient high-quality alliances result.
- Four mechanisms favor prosocial signals over wasteful or harmful alternatives: (1) Partners receive higher private benefits from prosocial signalers when a > 0 (Section 6.1, p. 17); (2) Partners prefer agents who conferred highest group benefits (reciprocity, Section 6.2, p. 18); (3) prosocial signals attract larger audiences through "broadcast efficiency" (Section 6.3, pp. 18-19); (4) group selection favors equilibria with higher group fitness (Section 6.4, p. 20).
- Punishment of non-cooperators can serve as the costly signal: enforcement itself is the benefit to others that signals high quality (p. 22; citing Boyd and Richerson 1992). This resolves the second-order collective action problem of who pays to punish.
- Inclusive fitness facilitates initial evolution of prosocial signaling: in a group with relatedness r > 0, the inclusive fitness cost c = c_o - (n-1)gr may be negative, meaning signaling is directly beneficial. Once established, the signaling equilibrium persists even under zero relatedness (p. 14).
- If quality is heritable, the equilibrium fraction p* of high-quality types is self-regulating, preventing runaway fixation (Equations 12-13, p. 15).

**Key observations:** Gintis, Smith & Bowles 2001 provides the formal game-theoretic foundation linking costly signaling to the evolution of cooperation, the core mechanism of Layer 3 in the Chaco signaling model. Five results are directly relevant. First, the Spence-equivalent condition (c' > s/p > c) specifies when honest signaling equilibria exist and maps to the requirement that great house construction be less costly for higher-quality groups. Second, the bistability result explains both why the Chaco signaling system required specific conditions to emerge (perturbation across the basin boundary) and why it collapsed rapidly once perturbed. Third, the four mechanisms favoring prosocial signals explain why Chaco signaling took prosocial forms (monument construction serving community functions, feasting that provisioned visitors) rather than purely wasteful displays. Fourth, broadcast efficiency (Section 6.3) directly applies to great houses: highly visible, persistent monuments have maximal broadcast strength per unit cost. Fifth, the inclusive fitness pathway (Section 4.3) is relevant to early Chaco where founding matrilineal groups (Kennett et al. 2017) cooperated on kinship basis before extending cooperation to unrelated immigrants. The model's multi-player framework (n = 10-100) is more appropriate for the Chaco system than dyadic signaling models. The frequency dependence of the equilibrium on p constrains parameterization: the proportion of high-quality groups must be neither too common nor too rare.

### From Schwarz 2023

**Findings:**

- Costly signaling theory applies not only to the rise of complex societies but also to political collapse and subsequent regeneration of complexity; signaling behavior is "subject to historical events, such as political collapse" (pp. 1-2).
- Two forms of cost modification explain signaling dynamics during and after collapse: "cost shifting" (decreased signal production due to inability to mobilize labor) and "cost masking" (the full expense for production of a costly signal is hidden or not paid for completely by the signaler) (pp. 9-11).
- A triadic model of status communication through architecture involves the sponsor, builder, and receiver, with the sponsor mobilizing both "allocative resources (materials and labor) and authoritative resources (canonical principles of design, schema for building, etc.)" (pp. 7-8; citing Gell 1998; Quinn 2019).
- The collapse of a complex state is "a decline in the marginal cost of costly signal production... But it was not the complete failure, at least in the Maya case, of costly signaling, but a marked departure from the previous growth trajectory" (pp. 23-24).
- The re-expansion of costly signaling margins above a baseline functions as "a principal mechanism for the regeneration of the state" (p. 25).
- Local communities produced imitation finewares ("local similars") that referenced the broader status system but represented bricolage rather than mere copying, constituting cost masking at the community level (p. 18).

**Key observations:** Schwarz 2023 makes the most significant extension of CST in archaeology by demonstrating that signaling frameworks explain collapse and regeneration, not only the rise of complexity. The cost shifting and cost masking concepts provide operational mechanisms for how signaling degrades gradually rather than collapsing in a binary fashion. For the Chaco model, cost shifting maps directly onto the Late Bonito McElmo-style great houses (Van Dyke 2004), which used illusory architectural features to maintain signal appearance at reduced cost. The triadic sponsor-builder-receiver model adds nuance beyond simple signaler-receiver dyads. The finding that local communities produced bricolage versions of imported finewares parallels the way small-house communities in the Chaco system built Chacoan-style architectural features (berms, great kivas) that referenced but did not replicate great house signaling.
