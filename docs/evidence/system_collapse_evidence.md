# System Collapse Evidence

Compiled from claims extractions: `docs/references/claims/`. All page numbers refer to the PDF/publication pages documented in the claims files. See individual claims files for full quotes and context.

## Key Questions

- What conditions led to the abandonment of Chaco Canyon (ca. 1130-1150 CE)?
- Does the collapse pattern match the model's prediction (sustained stress exceeding buffering capacity)?
- Did signaling investment decline before or simultaneously with abandonment?
- What happened to the regional network after Chaco's collapse?

## Evidence

### From Kantner & Vaughn 2012

**Findings:**

- "If, however, conditions are so consistently bad that the entreaties of all religious leaders are seen as ineffectual, we might expect pilgrimage centers and pilgrimage activity to eventually end" (p. 73)
- The Late Bonito period (AD 1130-1200) was characterized by "lengthy mid-1100s drought, followed by still low precipitation" (Table 2; mean 14.30 in., s=1.73, r1=0.231)
- Canyon was "mostly depopulated, with some reuse of old buildings" during Late Bonito (Table 2)

**Key observations:** Kantner & Vaughn frame collapse as loss of perceived supernatural efficacy under sustained drought. The model provides an alternative mechanism: sustained environmental stress makes the cost of signaling investment unsustainable (C exceeds the benefit B(lambda) at low sigma). Both mechanisms predict the same outcome (cessation of investment and abandonment) but through different causal pathways. The Late Bonito precipitation data (lower mean, still-high variability) is consistent with both interpretations.

### From Lentz et al. 2021

**Findings:**

- A multi-decadal drought from AD 1130 to 1180 "coincided with the departure from the canyon by many Chacoan inhabitants" (p. 4; citing Dean et al. 2002)
- The "Great Drought" from AD 1276 to 1299 led to emigration of remaining inhabitants after Mesa Verde-affiliated reoccupation (p. 4)
- By the Bonito phase, local PJW was essentially eliminated: juniper pollen at 2% by AD 1100, juniper charcoal absent after AD 750, all useful woody species severely depleted (pp. 6-7, 9)
- "The destabilizing impact resulting from recurrent woodland harvesting likely contributed to the environmental unpredictability and difficulty in procuring essential resources suffered by the Ancestral Puebloans prior to their ultimate departure from Chaco Canyon" (p. 1, abstract)
- Erosion and sedimentation increased during the occupation, degrading agricultural infrastructure (p. 14; citing Scarborough et al. 2018)
- Local fuel and game were both depleted, with meat sources obtained from 40+ km away, creating "tremendous pressure on the inhabitants to obtain essential resources" (p. 13)

**Key observations:** Lentz et al. 2021 adds an important dimension to the collapse narrative: environmental degradation was cumulative and partly self-inflicted. The Chacoan system did not simply collapse because of exogenous drought; centuries of resource extraction had already degraded the landscape, reducing resilience and amplifying the impact of the mid-1100s drought. For the signaling model, this suggests a specific collapse mechanism: the cost of maintaining signaling investment (C) was rising due to resource depletion (timber from increasingly distant sources, fuel from a denuded landscape) while the environmental buffer that signaling-supported cooperation networks provided was being eroded by the very activities that signaling required. When the AD 1130 drought hit, the system had less capacity to absorb the shock than it would have had centuries earlier.

### From Barclay et al. 2021

**Findings:**

- Signaling systems exist only when "some receivers... value senders little enough to still demand signals, yet some senders... value receivers highly enough to pay the signal costs" (Section 2g)
- When environmental or social conditions shift such that no senders can afford signal costs, or all receivers already trust senders, the signaling system ceases to function (Section 2g)
- The model requires only a single two-stage interaction; it does not require sustained relationships, meaning signaling systems can collapse rapidly when conditions change (Section 3)

**Key observations:** Barclay et al. 2021 provide a formal mechanism for signaling system collapse: the system fails when the cost-benefit structure shifts so that either (a) no sender can afford to signal (costs exceed benefits for all sender types) or (b) receivers no longer demand signals (because trust is already established or because the stakes are too low to justify information-seeking). For Chaco, mechanism (a) maps onto the megadrought scenario: sustained drought raises effective signaling costs (C increases as resources deplete) while reducing the benefits of cooperation networks (if all groups are experiencing the same catastrophic conditions, inter-regional exchange provides no buffering). The rapid collapse is consistent with the Barclay et al. prediction that the system can fail in a single interaction round.

### From Gillreath-Brown & Kohler 2024

**Findings:**

- The ENSO Index reached its most El Nino-like (favorable) values in the AD 1000s, when the Chacoan system was expanding most rapidly. High-frequency drought in the mid-1100s accompanying a diminution of El Nino-like conditions may have contributed to the decline of the Chaco Canyon center itself. (p. 470)
- Around AD 1200, ENSO dynamics entered a brief La Nina-like phase, the first in over a millennium. The social chaos of the 1200s (population movement, frequent conflict, depopulation) is connected to the cessation of the El Nino pattern. (pp. 470-471)
- The depopulation of the SWUS was due to an interaction of demographic, environmental, and social factors; the cessation of the El Nino-like pattern is proposed as an additional contributing cause. (p. 470)
- At AD ~1300, life expectancy at birth began plunging and crude birth rates followed shortly thereafter. (p. 470; citing Kohler and Reese 2014)
- Dean (1996:38-44) documented an extraordinary breakdown between AD 1250 and 1450 in a long-stable pattern of spatial relationships in precipitation, proxied from a network of 27 tree-ring chronologies. (p. 470)

**Key observations:** Gillreath-Brown & Kohler 2024 adds the low-frequency ENSO dimension to the collapse narrative. The system did not simply face a megadrought; the fundamental climate regime shifted from El Nino-like (favorable for maize) to La Nina-like (unfavorable). For the model, this means sigma changed not just in magnitude but in kind: the structure of spatial climate correlations changed, meaning that exchange networks calibrated to the previous climate regime would have lost their risk-buffering value. This is consistent with Gauthier 2021's finding that "social adaptations to one mode of variability are fragile to changes in the nature of that variability."

### From Kohler et al. 2023

**Findings:**

- Chaco had enough characteristics of a tributary state to be unable to survive a lack of taxes or tribute flowing to the centre from the periphery. (Sec. 3)
- Mid-1100s drought and replacement of El Nino by La Nina conditions in the 1200s caused production failures. (Sec. 3)
- Some Chaco-area linkages were able to survive into the 13th century by moving their centers of interaction north to better-watered, irrigable locations along the San Juan and Aztec rivers. (Sec. 3; citing Benson and Berry 2009; Reed 2008)
- Wealth inequality returned to low levels in the early 1200s in the CMV, but increased again to levels beyond the northern Southwest norm by mid-century before both declined during the depopulation. (Sec. 2f)

**Key observations:** The survival of some Chaco-era linkages through relocation to better-watered areas (San Juan/Aztec) is important: it suggests that the signaling system did not simply collapse everywhere simultaneously but was partially restructured. The northern successor center at Aztec represents a spatial shift in the signaling system rather than outright disappearance. The oscillation of inequality in the CMV during the 1200s suggests ongoing competition for political reorganization during the post-Chaco transition.

### From Mills et al. 2013

**Findings:**

- The large southern network tied together by Salado polychrome grew after AD 1300 but ultimately proved unstable and disintegrated in the AD 1400-1450 period. (p. 5789)
- The smaller, more fragmented northern networks (Hopi and Zuni) persisted and "in fact still exist today." (p. 5789)
- The AD 1300-1350 period was a transitional interval with the lowest average degree settlement centrality of all periods analyzed. (Table 1, p. 5787)
- By AD 1450, northern and southern Southwest areas were maximally disconnected. (p. 5788)

**Key observations:** Mills et al. 2013 documents what happened to regional networks after Chaco's collapse. The large southern Salado network provides a post-Chaco case study of a different integrative system that also ultimately failed. The persistence of smaller, denser networks (Hopi, Zuni) while the larger network collapsed suggests that network resilience is inversely related to network scale, with implications for the model's predictions about signaling system stability. Large signaling-based networks may be inherently more vulnerable to cascade failures.

## Synthesis

### From Neiman 1997

**Findings:**

- The cessation of monument construction across the Maya Lowlands was ultimately caused by ecological disaster: "an ecological disaster in which erosion played a leading role" (p. 284). Terminal monument dates span AD 731 to 909 across 69 sites, indicating a time-transgressive collapse over 178 years (pp. 273-277, Table 15.1).
- Strong correlation between rainfall and terminal dates (Spearman's r = -0.69): areas with highest rainfall experienced the earliest cessation of monument construction, interpreted as erosion-driven ecological collapse proceeding fastest where soil was most vulnerable (pp. 279-281).
- Under the signaling model, collapse means a "shrinkage in political-quality range and audience size, marked by the cessation of monument construction" (p. 277). This is driven by two mechanisms: (1) "a reduction in the range of political quality among competitors, caused by inability of individuals to produce or extract from others enough food to feed and maintain large cohorts of political allies"; (2) "a reduction in population size and thus the number of political competitors" (pp. 279-280).
- The fitted loess surface shows "expected dates for crossing the monument-construction threshold varying widely across the region from c. AD 760 to 900," indicating the collapse was "a single time and space-transgressive event" driven by ecological conditions, not an abrupt political rupture (pp. 277, 284).
- After monument cessation, "the severity of warfare during the Late Classic" increased, indicating that the signaling system's conflict-deterrence function failed as monument construction ceased (p. 278; citing Demarest 1992, 1996).

**Key observations:** The Maya collapse documented by Neiman 1997 provides the closest structural parallel to the Chaco collapse. Both cases show: (1) environmental degradation (erosion/deforestation in Maya, drought/deforestation at Chaco) as the ultimate cause; (2) cessation of monumental construction as the marker of collapse; (3) increased violence following the end of signaling investment; (4) spatial and temporal variation in the collapse process. The key difference is timescale: the Maya collapse was gradual (178 years), while Chaco's was relatively rapid (~20-40 years), possibly reflecting differences in the severity and speed of the environmental shock. Neiman's two-mechanism model (shrinking quality range + shrinking audience) provides specific predictions for the Chaco case: construction should cease first in communities with the lowest agricultural productivity and smallest populations.

### From O'Shea & Halstead 1989

**Findings:**

- Change resulting from risk-buffering dynamics may be catastrophic, cyclical, or directional. Catastrophic change results when populations encounter problems so severe or unexpected that greatly increased mortality and/or collapse of the institutions, conventions, and currencies that underpin the society occurs. (p. 125)
- When the selective forces acting on high-level decision makers differ from those on producers, a structural conflict can exist: upper-level mechanisms may exacerbate shortage at the local level. In cases of a parasitic elite, the decisions and activities of upper-level mechanisms may worsen conditions for the broader population. (p. 124)
- Buffering mechanisms adopted as solutions to short-term instability have often instigated radical and sustained change. Once firmly established, these institutions may assume a dynamism of their own, becoming irreversibly transformed. (p. 125)
- High-level coping mechanisms are an unstable element at the core of human culture, which can trigger a chain reaction of changes throughout society. (Halstead & O'Shea 1989, p. 6)

**Key observations:** O'Shea & Halstead contribute three concepts directly applicable to Chaco collapse. First, the catastrophic change pathway describes what happened at Chaco: the institutions (great houses, exchange networks, ritual system) that underpinned the Chaco Phenomenon collapsed under the megadrought, and no immediate replacement emerged within the canyon. Second, the elite-producer structural conflict provides a mechanism for why the system did not adapt: if great house elites were insulated from shortages (through stored surplus, exchange access), they may have continued extracting construction labor even as conditions deteriorated, accelerating rather than buffering the crisis. Third, the institutional path dependence argument explains why the system could not pivot: the signaling institutions had become so embedded in regular practice that they could not be redirected toward crisis response.

### From Scarborough et al. 2018

**Findings:**

- The canal system showed progressive narrowing of channels and construction of berms (Units C-3, C-4) as water availability dropped, indicating increasing environmental stress during the Bonito Phase (p. 876).
- The canal system was in use "possibly as late as the early twelfth century" (p. 880), meaning it was maintained through the period of increasing environmental stress leading up to the AD 1130 abandonment.
- Quick social responses were necessary to harness multiple water sources, "probably drawing upon labour provided by occupants of the great houses and the numerous small sites throughout the canyon" (p. 884).
- The centralization of leadership through ritual authority "may, in part, have sustained the Chaco social system by establishing certainty in the face of unpredictable environmental factors" (p. 885).

**Key observations:** Scarborough et al. 2018 document a system that was actively adapting to increasing environmental stress right up to the point of collapse. The canal narrowing represents a deteriorating capacity to harness water, paralleling the rising C (cost) in the signaling model. The interpretation that ritual authority "established certainty" under uncertainty provides a mechanism for how the signaling system could maintain investment as conditions deteriorated: leadership based on perceived ability to manage environmental risk could sustain itself as long as the ritual-labor mobilization system continued to produce results, but would fail catastrophically when prolonged drought rendered all responses ineffective.

### From Wills et al. 2022

**Findings:**

- The 3-hour labor mobilization radius defined by LCA places a spatial constraint on the cooperation network: beyond approximately three hours, travel time increases rapidly, "in nearly all cases exceeding eight hours" (p. 475). This means the practical labor pool was geographically bounded.
- "Once workers arrived in Chaco, every day there was a day lost to farming at home" (p. 476), quantifying the opportunity cost that would have made the system vulnerable to prolonged stress.
- The construction window (July-September) coincided exactly with the agricultural season, meaning that any drought that threatened agriculture simultaneously threatened the labor supply for construction.

**Key observations:** Wills et al. 2022's spatial constraints imply a specific collapse mechanism: as environmental stress increased, the opportunity cost of sending labor to Chaco for construction would have risen (farmers could less afford to leave their fields), simultaneously reducing the labor supply for monument construction and undermining the signaling system. The 3-hour limit means that the system could not simply expand its labor catchment to compensate for reduced per-community contributions.

### From Mills et al. 2018

**Findings:**

- During the AD 1100-1150 interval, community-detection analyses show three network communities: the large northern and southern communities, plus a third transitional component at their boundaries, characterized by mixed ceramic assemblages (p. 933).
- Although the first half of the twelfth century marks the end of the Bonito Phase, there were still many connected great houses and great kivas after AD 1150 (p. 933).
- The cluster of Chaco structures near Aztec is not as central to the post-1150 network as expected; the greatest density of high-centrality sites remains in Chaco Canyon and to the south and southwest (pp. 934-935).
- Aztec's ascendancy as the capital of the post-Chaco World is especially evident in the AD 1150-1200 interval, but the transition is not a simple north-south replacement (p. 935).
- A twelfth-century retraction of networks separated the Northern and Southern San Juan areas prior to regional depopulation (p. 936, abstract/conclusions).

**Key observations:** Mills et al. 2018 documents the network structure of Chaco's decline in detail. The finding that network connectivity remained high into the AD 1100-1150 interval (even after the Bonito Phase ended) suggests the cooperation network persisted after peak monument construction ceased, with a lag between the cessation of signaling investment and the collapse of the network it maintained. The emergence of a transitional boundary community during this period is consistent with the model's prediction that collapse proceeds through fragmentation at network margins. The incomplete transfer of centrality from Chaco to Aztec suggests the successor system was not a simple continuation but a restructured, more northerly network. The retraction separating northern and southern components prior to depopulation matches the prediction that large networks are more vulnerable to cascade failure than small, dense ones (consistent with Mills et al. 2013's finding that smaller Hopi and Zuni networks persisted while the larger Salado network collapsed).

### From Polyak et al. 2022

**Findings:**

- The puebloan Late Holocene (1260 to 370 yr B2K) was the most arid and variable climatic period of the last 3400 years (p. 1, abstract).
- Pueblo IV (ca. AD 1315-1440) was the driest interval, ending in an approximately 210-year "super drought" growth hiatus (p. 7).
- The Pueblo II interval (ca. AD 860-1140) included an approximately 90-year growth hiatus or very slow growth around AD 1050 (p. 6).
- The transition from Pueblo period aridity to post-Pueblo conditions began at approximately 370 yr B2K (ca. AD 1630) with a return to pluvial climate (p. 7).

**Key observations:** Polyak et al. 2022 provide the long-term climatic context for the collapse. The Chaco system did not simply face a single megadrought; it existed within the driest and most variable climatic epoch of the last 3400 years. The approximately 90-year drought signal coinciding with peak Chaco florescence suggests the system was pushing through increasingly severe conditions, making it vulnerable to the sustained drought that began in the AD 1130s. The subsequent Pueblo IV "super drought" drove the final abandonment of remaining Pueblo settlements in the region, suggesting that the environmental conditions never recovered sufficiently to support a Chaco-scale system.

### From Van Dyke 2004

**Findings:**

- Six new great houses were built in the Late Bonito phase (A.D. 1100-1140) as an attempt to "generate renewed interest in Chaco as a center place and to restore confidence in the rituals that took place there" (p. 426).
- The building program was undertaken by "a leadership that had less labor at its disposal" than Classic Bonito leaders, with Late Bonito McElmo-style construction requiring only 55,000-90,000 person-hours compared to 170,000-192,000 for Classic Bonito construction (p. 423; citing Lekson 1986).
- The building program "failed": it lasted only about a generation, and some buildings were never completed, including Roberts Small Pueblo and Hillside Ruin (p. 427; citing Roberts 1926-27, Stein et al. 2003).
- Chaco was in competition with Aztec by the early 1100s. Construction at Salmon Ruin began A.D. 1088 and at Aztec during the same decade. "If Chaco was in competition with Aztec during the early 1100s, Aztec won" (p. 426).
- A sharp downturn in agricultural production during the 1090s may have "shaken the faith of the populace in their ritual leadership" (p. 426; citing Sebastian 1992:135).

**Key observations:** Van Dyke 2004 provides the most detailed account of the Late Bonito building program as a failed attempt to restore declining signal credibility. The reduced labor investment, the use of cost-saving construction methods (standardized McElmo template, illusory architectural features), and the incomplete structures together document a signaling system in terminal decline. The competition with Aztec adds an important dimension: Chaco's collapse was not simply an internal failure but a loss in an intergroup signaling competition. The incomplete buildings provide physical evidence for the abruptness of the collapse transition.

### From Wills et al. 2014

**Findings:**

- Construction energy investment in Chaco great houses began declining in the late A.D. 1000s, before the onset of documented drought periods (pp. 3-4; Fig. 5).
- The paleoclimate record provides no obvious correlation between drought and societal collapse (p. 7).
- The abandonment of the canyon great house community was not abrupt, although the exact process leading to depopulation by the end of the 13th century is unclear (p. 7).
- "There is no substantive evidence for deforestation at Chaco and no obvious indications that the depopulation of the canyon in the 13th century was caused by any specific cultural practices or natural events" (p. 1, abstract).

**Key observations:** Wills et al. 2014 challenge the standard drought-collapse narrative by documenting a pre-drought decline in construction investment. For the signaling model, this is important because it suggests that declining returns from the signaling system (audience loss to Aztec, reduced payoff from cooperation networks) may have driven reduced investment before environmental conditions deteriorated. This is consistent with Barclay et al.'s (2021) formal mechanism where signaling ceases when receivers no longer demand signals or when no sender can afford the cost. However, Lentz et al. 2021 contest the claim that deforestation was not a factor.

### From Crown & Wills 2018

**Findings:**

- Around AD 1100, termination rituals involved sealing rooms with masonry, placing 173 ceramic vessels (including 112 cylinder jars associated with cacao consumption) in Room 28, and setting fires that destroyed rooms along the northern arc of the West Court (p. 901).
- These fires "were probably part of termination or retirement rituals, which closed the rooms from further use and removed the power from objects accorded animacy as spiritual beings" (p. 901; citing Mills 2008: 103; Mills & Ferguson 2008; Crown 2018).
- Post-florescence occupation continued into the AD 1200s, "perhaps by migrants from the Mesa Verde area to the north." Fewer construction events "indicate a focus on renovating ritual structures and the late construction of semi-subterranean houses scattered along the edge of the plazas" (p. 901; citing Windes & Ford 1996: 303; Wills 2009).
- Room 28b (formerly fitted with ceremonial T-shaped doorways) may have been reused as a turkey pen after the termination events. "It is unclear whether Room 28b was ever re-roofed; evidence suggests it may have been used as a turkey pen" (p. 901).
- Construction at other great houses in the canyon continued after Pueblo Bonito's termination events, and "Ancestral Puebloans probably abandoned Pueblo Bonito by the fourteenth century" (p. 901; Windes & Ford 1996: 297).

**Key observations:** Crown & Wills 2018 document the terminal phase of Pueblo Bonito's signaling cycle with unusual detail. The termination rituals represent a specific collapse mechanism not well-captured by existing theoretical frameworks: deliberate, ritualized destruction of signal value rather than gradual decay or abrupt abandonment. The entombment of 173 ceramic vessels (including 112 cylinder jars used in cacao-drinking ceremonies) represents the intentional sacrifice of accumulated signaling capital, both architectural and material. The post-termination trajectory, from monumental architecture to small semi-subterranean houses and turkey pens in formerly elite spaces, documents the full arc of signal depreciation from peak investment to functional abandonment. The fact that other great houses continued construction after Pueblo Bonito's termination suggests the collapse was structure-specific rather than system-wide initially, with the signaling system fragmenting rather than collapsing simultaneously.

---

The collapse evidence now includes seven complementary mechanisms: (1) Kantner & Vaughn 2012 propose loss of perceived supernatural efficacy under sustained drought; (2) Lentz et al. 2021 document cumulative environmental degradation that reduced system resilience before the megadrought; (3) Barclay et al. 2021 provide a formal mechanism where signaling systems fail when costs exceed benefits for all sender types; (4) Neiman 1997 specifies that collapse proceeds through shrinking quality variance and audience size, with the Maya case demonstrating a strong empirical link between environmental conditions (rainfall/erosion) and the spatial pattern of monument cessation. Trigger 1990 (abstract only) adds the energy-surplus framework, and Gillreath-Brown & Kohler 2024 identify the ENSO regime shift as a fundamental change in climate structure. (5) O'Shea & Halstead 1989 identify the structural conflict between elite decision-makers and producers, institutional path dependence preventing adaptive reallocation, and the catastrophic change pathway where buffering institutions collapse when overwhelmed. (6) Crown & Wills 2018 document deliberate termination rituals (sealing and burning rooms with 173 ceramic vessels c. AD 1100) as a ritualized form of signal retirement, followed by a post-florescence trajectory of declining investment and repurposing of elite spaces, demonstrating that collapse could proceed through intentional signal destruction as well as gradual decay. These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive. The mls-monuments model's collapse prediction integrates all six: rising costs (C increases from resource depletion), declining benefits (cooperation networks lose value when all groups experience the same drought), the formal condition that signaling ceases when no sender type can afford the cost (Barclay et al.), Neiman's prediction that collapse proceeds time-transgressively through communities with the least capacity first, and the possibility that signal retirement through termination rituals represents a deliberate exit from the signaling equilibrium rather than a forced abandonment.

### From Safi 2015

**Findings:**

- Southern Cibola great house communities had momentary populations of 73-273 people (Table 6.2, p. 162), all below the 475-person reproductive viability threshold. This demographic vulnerability implies the system was dependent on inter-community connections.
- No great kivas were identified among the tested southern Cibola great houses, contrary to expectations of an ideological system model (p. 265).
- Construction occurs during productive periods rather than during environmental downturns (p. 56), suggesting the system was built during favorable conditions and therefore vulnerable when conditions deteriorated.
- Great house architectural variability correlates with construction timing; the temporal pattern of increasing elaboration suggests a competitive arms race that may have increased system fragility (pp. 169, 271).

**Key observations:** Safi's data from the southern periphery illustrate how collapse may have proceeded differently in outlier regions compared to the canyon core. Small populations and lack of great kivas suggest these communities were more loosely integrated with the canyon core. The finding that construction required productive conditions implies sustained drought would halt both the ability to construct (no surplus) and the value of the signal (cooperation networks lose relevance when all groups face the same shortfall), consistent with the model's collapse prediction. The competitive escalation in architectural elaboration may have increased system fragility by committing resources to signaling that might otherwise have been available for buffering.

### From Crown 2018

**Findings:**

- The dramatic termination of cylinder jars around AD 1100 involved the deliberate destruction of 102 vessels in Room 28 at Pueblo Bonito. Ninety-nine jars were placed on a shelf, sprinkled with turquoise and shell beads, a fire was lit beneath them, and the room was sealed. Ten additional jars were smashed on the upper-story floor (p. 398).
- The termination "indicates the end of their ritual use and perhaps the end of the competitive events in which they were used or the emigration or even expulsion of the group that used them" (p. 402).
- The cylinder jar form was "almost completely repudiated in the region" after termination. The only known post-destruction cylinder jar is a Tusayan Polychrome (AD 1125-1290) from northeastern Arizona (p. 399).
- Following termination, individually owned and generalized drinking forms (mugs) became the norm, signaling a shift from corporate to individual identity expression (p. 402).
- "Whatever value and status the use of cylinder jars once held was gone but that the jars themselves still held power that had to be destroyed through respectful termination" (p. 402).
- "This was undoubtedly a time of upheaval as one form of drinking vessel and the behavior and rituals associated with it disappeared and an entirely different form of drinking vessel appeared" (p. 402).

**Key observations:** Crown 2018 provides the most detailed evidence for the deliberate termination of a specific signaling channel at Chaco. The destruction of cylinder jars around AD 1100 predates the full abandonment of Chaco Canyon by approximately 30-50 years, suggesting that the signaling system began to collapse in stages rather than all at once. The ritual destruction (not simple abandonment) implies the jars retained symbolic power even as the social system that supported them was dissolving. The shift from corporate (cylinder jars) to individual (mugs) drinking vessels parallels the broader fragmentation of the Chaco regional system. For the model, this provides evidence that signal channel collapse can precede system collapse: the cooperation network may have begun losing coherence before the final abandonment.

### From Schwarz 2023

**Findings:**

- The collapse of a complex state, in terms of costly signaling, is "a decline in the marginal cost of costly signal production... But it was not the complete failure, at least in the Maya case, of costly signaling, but a marked departure from the previous growth trajectory" (pp. 23-24).
- Cost shifting during the Maya Terminal Classic (AD 800-950) resulted in continued monument construction but at reduced scale: "There was less monumentality than in the Late Classic period, but it still existed, suggesting that there was stabilization at a lower level" (p. 10).
- Cost masking in post-collapse Maya contexts took the form of appropriating Classic-period monuments and architectural settings. "The Postclassic builders and their sponsors, the emerging elites in the new era, commandeered these past monumental constructions and settings and so did not have to pay the full cost of building de novo" (pp. 15-16).
- The re-expansion of costly signaling margins above a baseline functions as "a principal mechanism for the regeneration of the state" (p. 25), meaning collapse is potentially reversible if conditions allow new elites to re-engage in costly signaling.

**Key observations:** Schwarz 2023 provides a comparative framework for understanding Chaco's collapse as a signaling-system failure. The Maya case suggests collapse proceeds through cost shifting (reduced but continued signaling) rather than binary cessation, followed by potential regeneration through cost masking (appropriating prior constructions). At Chaco, the Late Bonito McElmo-style great houses (Van Dyke 2004) may represent cost shifting, while the reuse of Pueblo Bonito during post-florescence occupation (Crown and Wills 2018) and the continuation at Aztec Ruins may represent forms of cost masking. The key difference from the Maya case: Chaco did not regenerate locally; instead, the system relocated to Aztec and then (per Lekson 2002) possibly to Paquime, suggesting that the cost-masking pathway was geographically displaced rather than occurring in situ.

### From Lekson 2002

**Findings:**

- Aztec Ruins continued construction until approximately 1275 despite the return of resource unpredictability around 1250. This lag "can be understood as evidence of political failure. The end of regional order does not result in the immediate abandonment of the center" (p. 619).
- The Late period (1250-1600) saw "unprecedented" village-on-village and alliance-on-alliance warfare, representing a qualitative escalation from Early period patterns (pp. 615, 620).
- "Political disintegration, environmental deterioration, and rising levels of warfare all surely contributed to the 'abandonment' of the Four Corners. At least 20,000 people left the Four Corners and joined existing communities" in the modern Pueblos region (p. 619).
- Three and one-half centuries of Middle period EP Events "must have produced lasting cultural patterns, internal causes, that exacerbated and escalated warfare in the Late period" (pp. 620-621).
- The end of the Chaco/Aztec political system and the return of resource unpredictability coincided to produce compound effects: "the remarkable increase in intensity and scale of Late period violence after centuries of Middle period peace surely played a role in the totality and finality of the out-migration from the Four Corners area" (p. 619).

**Key observations:** Lekson 2002 provides the most detailed reconstruction of what happened after the Chaco signaling system collapsed. The lag between environmental deterioration (~1250) and the end of construction at Aztec (~1275) supports the model's prediction of system inertia: accumulated symbolic capital and institutional momentum delay collapse even as conditions deteriorate. The subsequent explosion of warfare confirms the Layer 2 prediction that conflict returns when the signaling system fails. The compound effect of environmental stress, political disintegration, and escalating warfare produced a more severe outcome than any single factor alone, consistent with a multi-layer model where the simultaneous failure of all three layers (individual signaling, conflict deterrence, cooperation networks) creates a cascading collapse.
