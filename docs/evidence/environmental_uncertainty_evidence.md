# Environmental Uncertainty Evidence

Compiled from claims extractions: `docs/references/claims/`. All page numbers refer to the PDF/publication pages documented in the claims files. See individual claims files for full quotes and context.

## Key Questions

- What was the magnitude and frequency of environmental shortfalls at Chaco?
- How did precipitation variability change across Chaco cultural periods?
- Does signaling investment correlate with environmental uncertainty (the lambda(sigma) prediction)?
- What was Chaco's agricultural carrying capacity and how dependent was the population on imported food?

## Evidence

### From Kantner & Vaughn 2012

**Findings:**

- Table 2 provides period-level precipitation statistics from dendroclimatological reconstructions:
  - Pueblo I (AD 750-850): mean 14.56 in., s=1.51, r1=0.146 (high interannual variability)
  - Early Bonito (AD 850-1020): mean 14.31 in., s=1.59, r1=0.305 (somewhat dryer, lower variability)
  - Classic Bonito (AD 1020-1130): mean 15.09 in., s=1.94, r1=0.275 (wetter overall but with long droughts bracketed by long wet periods)
  - Late Bonito (AD 1130-1200): mean 14.30 in., s=1.73, r1=0.231 (lengthy mid-1100s drought)
  (pp. 75-76; from Force et al. 2002, Grissino-Mayer 1996, 1997)
- The pilgrimage era (Classic Bonito) had higher standard deviation and lower autocorrelation than Early Bonito, indicating less predictable rainfall despite higher averages (pp. 76-77)
- "Climatic unpredictability that promotes desire among adherents to engage with successful religious leaders" listed as a context for emergence of pilgrimage signaling (p. 73, Table 1)

**Key observations:** The Table 2 data are the most specific paleoclimate statistics tied to Chaco cultural periods in the literature reviewed so far. The key finding for the model: peak signaling investment (Classic Bonito) coincides with higher precipitation variability (s=1.94 vs. 1.59) and lower year-to-year predictability (r1=0.275 vs. 0.305). This supports the lambda(sigma) prediction, though the mechanism Kantner & Vaughn propose (demand for religious authority) differs from the model's (endogenous signaling returns from cooperation network value).

### From Lentz et al. 2021

**Findings:**

- Pollen data demonstrate a robust pinyon-juniper woodland (PJW) existed in Chaco Canyon before 600 BC (juniper pollen 20%, pine pollen 12%), but juniper pollen declined to 8% by AD 400, 4% by AD 600, and 2% by AD 1100 (p. 6)
- The woodland decline was anthropogenic rather than climate-driven: juniper populations survived the Late Archaic "great aridity" but declined during the more mesic conditions of population growth; adjacent areas within 100 km retain PJW at similar elevations; PJW survived prior severe aridification events (pp. 12-13)
- "The destabilizing impact resulting from recurrent woodland harvesting likely contributed to the environmental unpredictability and difficulty in procuring essential resources" (p. 1, abstract)
- Over 1 million m^3 of fuelwood were needed during the 340-year Bonito phase (calculated from 8.5 m^3/family/year, 2,100-2,700 inhabitants); daily fuel demand far exceeded construction timber demand as a driver of deforestation (p. 13)
- Deer and other large mammals at Pueblo Bonito were obtained from distances greater than 40 km, indicating broad ecosystem degradation beyond woodland loss (p. 13; citing Mills 2018, Grimstead et al. 2019)
- Sedimentation and erosion increased during the occupation period, further degrading agricultural productivity (p. 14; citing Scarborough et al. 2018)
- The Bonito florescence (AD 990-1130) coincided with increasing precipitation, while a multi-decadal drought from AD 1130 to 1180 coincided with departure from the canyon (p. 4; citing Dean et al. 2002)

**Key observations:** Lentz et al. 2021 provides critical evidence that the environmental uncertainty parameter (sigma) in the signaling model was partly endogenous. Woodland deforestation from centuries of fuel consumption and timber extraction degraded the resource base, amplifying vulnerability to drought and increasing effective environmental unpredictability. This creates a positive feedback loop: signaling activities (construction, ceremonial fuel use) required resource extraction that degraded the landscape, which increased sigma, which in turn would have increased the value of cooperation networks and potentially driven further signaling investment, until the system crossed a threshold of unsustainability. The paper also demonstrates that the resource base was already severely degraded before the Bonito phase construction boom, meaning the cost parameter C was elevated from the outset due to local resource scarcity (timber imported from 75+ km by AD 850).

### From Wills 2017

**Findings:**

- Pre-Bonito Phase agriculture was "almost certainly heavily reliant on runoff production" using dispersed floodwater farming methods that are inherently risk-minimizing (pp. 375, 377; citing Bocinsky et al. 2016, ethnographic record)
- "Locally unpredictable rainfall made agricultural intensification impossible" at Chaco (p. 394; citing Stone and Downum 1999:117)
- Broadly reconstructed climate patterns from tree-ring records "do not capture local hydrological conditions in the past, particularly high intensity storms, which are more accurately measured by evidence for flooding, erosion, sedimentation, and destruction of water control systems" (p. 377; citing Dominguez and Holm 2005; Huckleberry and Duff 2007; Sandor et al. 2002, 2007)
- Agricultural productivity in the Chaco Core was "probably sufficient to support most projected population sizes at least at subsistence levels" using dispersed extensive farming (p. 398; citing Dorshow 2012, Wills and Dorshow 2012)
- Hungo Pavi's watershed (1,190 ha) could support 175-250 people; with 8-10 km farming catchments, the total agricultural zone exceeds 11,000 km2 (p. 398; citing Dorshow 2012:2113)
- Experimental maize cultivation through prehistoric water control features "did not show obvious improvements in productivity over less formal cultivation methods" (p. 371; citing Toll et al. 1985:104-105)

**Key observations:** Wills 2017 adds important nuance to the environmental uncertainty parameter. The paper argues that macro-scale climate reconstructions (PDSI, tree-ring records) understate the actual agricultural uncertainty experienced by Chaco farmers because they miss localized stochastic events (floods, erosion) that determined farm-level outcomes. This means the effective sigma experienced by producers may have been higher than what tree-ring-based estimates suggest, strengthening the case for the lambda(sigma) prediction. The paper also establishes that Chaco agriculture was spatially extensive rather than intensive, meaning the agricultural base was large but diffuse, making coordination through cooperation networks (Layer 3) more valuable.

### From Gauthier 2021

**Findings:**

- Six empirical orthogonal functions (EOFs) explain 83% of observed drought variability in the American Southwest. These represent different oceanic and continental moisture transport pathways: tropical Pacific (EOF1), Gulf of Mexico (EOF2), polar continental (EOF3), Pacific westerly (EOF4), Great Plains (EOF5), and Colorado Plateau local (EOF6). The patterns are robust across observational data and millennial reconstructions. (p. 5)
- A distance-only model explains 37.8% of ceramic similarity variance; adding climatic dissimilarity (absolute difference in EOF loadings) increases this to 42.5%, a moderate but statistically significant improvement. (pp. 5-6)
- The improvement from climate terms is most pronounced at and after AD 1300, during and after the period of regional relocation, suggesting that drought-driven migration strengthened climate-regime-based ties. (p. 6)
- "Social adaptations to one mode of variability are fragile to changes in the nature of that variability" (p. 8), implying that when climate modes shift, existing network structures may fail.

**Key observations:** Gauthier demonstrates that different sites experience different drought patterns depending on their position relative to moisture transport pathways. This spatial heterogeneity in drought exposure is what makes inter-regional exchange ties valuable for crisis buffering, directly supporting the model's Layer 3 mechanism. The finding that social adaptations are fragile to regime shifts is consistent with the model's prediction that signaling systems collapse when environmental conditions change fundamentally.

### From Gillreath-Brown & Kohler 2024

**Findings:**

- A new pollen-based low-frequency temperature reconstruction for the SWUS shows that summer temperatures were generally warmer (relative to 1961-1990 average) before AD 1, generally cooler from AD 1 to ~1000, with a warming trend beginning ca. AD 400, interrupted by marked cooling in the AD 900s and 1500s. (pp. 458-459, Figure 1)
- The difference between the SWUS reconstruction and Moberg et al. (2005) Northern Hemisphere reconstruction indexes the mean state of ENSO. El Nino-like conditions (cool/wet in SWUS) dominated the first millennium AD; La Nina-like conditions (warm/dry) dominated AD 1200 to ca. 1900. (pp. 460-462, Figure 3)
- The ENSO Index reached its most El Nino-like (favorable) values in the AD 1000s, precisely when the Chacoan system was expanding most rapidly. (p. 470)
- Around AD 1200, ENSO dynamics entered a brief La Nina-like phase, the first such period in the SWUS in more than a millennium. Dean (1996:38-44) documented an extraordinary breakdown in a long-stable pattern of spatial precipitation relationships between AD 1250 and 1450. (p. 470)
- Low-frequency processes set the baseline from which high-frequency variability departs. A decadal drought during an l-f hot/dry (La Nina) period is more severe than the same drought during an l-f cool/wet (El Nino) period. (pp. 462-463; Dean 1988b, 1996)

**Key observations:** This paper adds a critical low-frequency dimension to the environmental uncertainty parameter. Sigma should be decomposed into: (1) a low-frequency baseline state (El Nino-like vs. La Nina-like) and (2) high-frequency perturbations around that baseline. The Chaco florescence occurred during a long (~1100-year) favorable baseline (El Nino-like), and the system's decline coincided with a shift to an unfavorable baseline (La Nina-like). The Dean (1996) finding that spatial precipitation patterns broke down after AD 1250 implies that exchange networks calibrated to previous climate correlations would lose their risk-buffering value.

### From Kohler et al. 2023

**Findings:**

- Maize production is generally favored by low-frequency trends toward cooler temperatures (limiting evapotranspiration) and El Nino activity (above-average winter precipitation). These trends were quite favorable for nearly the entire period of Pueblo occupation, though the ENSO phase began to turn unfavorable in the mid-1200s. (Sec. 2b)
- The most favored soils could produce 10 bushels of maize per year for only about 18 years under optimal conditions; the best Morefield Canyon locations could last approximately three times that (54 years). (Sec. 2e; Benson soil analyses)
- As "room to walk" (available dry-farming niche) decreased, house occupation duration increased, indicating that mobility constraints intensified over time. (Sec. 2c)
- Mid-1100s drought and replacement of El Nino by La Nina conditions in the 1200s caused production failures that undermined the Chacoan system. (Sec. 3)

**Key observations:** The soil field-life data (18-54 years) provides ground-truth for the assumption that agricultural sustainability at Chaco was precarious. The "room to walk" metric documents increasing environmental constraint: as farmland became scarce, groups could no longer move away from stress, increasing the value of in-place buffering strategies (including signaling-based cooperation networks).

### From Halstead & O'Shea 1989 and O'Shea & Halstead 1989

**Findings:**

- Three diagnostic characteristics of environmental variability determine the scope of the buffering problem: temporal structure (timescale and duration of shortfalls), spatial structure (size and homogeneity of affected area), and relative intensity (severity and variation in severity) (Halstead & O'Shea 1989, p. 3)
- The basic structure of variability can often be predicted quite accurately for any given situation, and therefore the basic structure of an effective coping mechanism can also be specified (Halstead & O'Shea 1989, p. 1)
- The structure of variability may be more significant than average conditions for understanding human society. Variability structure can be established even when the average is unknown (Halstead & O'Shea 1989, p. 6)
- When native Americans cultivated maize using small, optimally located, widely separated fields, micro-scale effects (soil type, micro-climate, insects, fire, hail) increased in importance as factors producing significant variability. Spatial variability was probably greater than that for modern farmers, making exchange a useful buffering option (Halstead & O'Shea 1989, p. 5)
- The effects of environmental perturbation are not homogeneous over space; regional buffering networks linking different cultures and economies represent the middle ground between individual societies and world systems (O'Shea & Halstead 1989, p. 126)

**Key observations:** The Halstead & O'Shea framework provides the theoretical foundation for the model's approach to parameterizing environmental uncertainty. Their three-dimensional characterization of variability (temporal, spatial, intensity) maps directly onto the model's sigma parameter and its decomposition. The finding that small-field agriculture experiences higher spatial variability reinforces Wills 2017's argument that macro-scale climate reconstructions understate the actual uncertainty experienced by Chaco farmers using dispersed floodwater farming. The concept of "supra-cultural buffering systems" operating at intermediate spatial scales characterizes the Chaco outlier network precisely.

### From Polyak et al. 2022

**Findings:**

- Stalagmite HC-1 from Hidden Cave (Guadalupe Mountains, southeastern NM) provides a 3400-year high-resolution paleoclimate record showing that the entire Pueblo period (1260 to 370 yr B2K, ca. AD 740-1630) was the most arid and climatically variable interval of the Late Holocene (p. 1, abstract).
- The preceding interval (3400 to 1260 yr B2K, ca. 1400 BC to AD 740) was characterized by pluvial conditions: continuous calcite growth at 0.085 mm/yr with no aragonite layers or significant hiatuses (p. 3).
- The Pueblo period (puebloan Late Holocene) shows a nearly six-fold reduction in stalagmite growth rate (0.015 mm/yr), frequent aragonite layers indicating droughts, and multiple growth hiatuses (p. 3).
- Pueblo II (ca. AD 860-1140 in the HC-1 record) was much drier than Pueblo I, with an approximately 90-year growth hiatus or very slow growth period after ca. AD 1050, indicating severe, sustained drought coinciding with peak Chaco florescence (p. 6).
- Pueblo III had an approximately 140-year span of numerous but more evenly spaced droughts, which the authors describe as "predictable" climate variability that would have been "possibly easier for cultures to adapt to" (pp. 6-7).
- Pueblo IV (ca. AD 1315-1440) was the driest interval, with a 210-year "super drought" growth hiatus (p. 7).
- Strong covariation of delta-18O and delta-13C (R = 0.82, p < 0.00001) independently confirms the mineral assemblage proxy, with puebloan sub-period samples clustering toward the arid/kinetic fractionation end of the trend (pp. 3-4, Figure 4).

**Key observations:** Polyak et al. 2022 adds a critical multi-millennial baseline to the environmental uncertainty evidence. The Pueblo period was not simply a time of occasional drought but the driest and most variable interval of the last 3400 years. This reframes the entire Chaco trajectory as occurring within an anomalous climatic period, relative to the preceding millennia of pluvial conditions. For the signaling model, the finding that the approximately 90-year drought signal at ca. AD 1050 coincides with peak Chaco construction is consistent with the lambda(sigma) prediction: signaling intensifies when environmental stress is high but cooperation networks can still buffer shortfalls. The speleothem is from southeastern New Mexico (Guadalupe Mountains), approximately 200 km from Chaco, so the record represents a regional climate signal rather than local Chaco conditions specifically.

## Synthesis

Six independent lines of evidence converge on the importance of environmental uncertainty for the Chaco system:

1. **High-frequency variability**: Kantner & Vaughn 2012 document higher precipitation standard deviation during Classic Bonito (s=1.94) vs. Early Bonito (s=1.59), with lower autocorrelation, indicating less predictable year-to-year conditions during peak signaling investment.

2. **Low-frequency baseline**: Gillreath-Brown & Kohler 2024 show that the entire Chaco florescence occurred during a long El Nino-dominated period, providing a favorable baseline for agriculture. The shift to La Nina-like conditions around AD 1200 changed the fundamental climate regime.

3. **Spatial heterogeneity**: Gauthier 2021 demonstrates that different regions experience different drought patterns, making inter-regional exchange ties valuable for crisis buffering. The six EOF patterns identify which climate zones provide complementary risk profiles.

4. **Resource constraint**: Kohler et al. 2023 document increasing constraints on mobility and agricultural sustainability, with soil field-life limited to 18-54 years and "room to walk" declining over time.

5. **Endogenous degradation debate**: Lentz et al. 2021 present pollen evidence for progressive anthropogenic deforestation, while Wills et al. 2014 challenge this narrative, arguing that packrat midden data are insufficient (only 2 dated samples), pollen records may indicate no canyon woodland, fuel use was dominated by shrubs and maize cobs rather than trees, and strontium isotopes cannot reliably discriminate timber sources. Wills et al. further observe that construction investment declined before documented drought, suggesting endogenous factors (declining returns, competition from Aztec) rather than escalating resource costs may have driven reduced investment. This disagreement represents an active empirical debate about whether effective sigma was partly endogenous to the signaling system.

6. **Multi-millennial baseline**: Polyak et al. 2022 demonstrate from a 3400-year speleothem record that the entire Pueblo period was the driest and most variable interval of the Late Holocene, with the preceding two millennia characterized by pluvial conditions. The Chaco florescence occurred during the most drought-prone sub-period (Pueblo II), with a severe approximately 90-year drought coinciding with peak construction at ca. AD 1050.

7. **Water management under uncertainty**: Scarborough et al. 2018 definitively date a canal network to the Bonito Phase (AD 800-1130) using OSL, demonstrate multi-source water diversion confirmed by ED-XRF and strontium isotopes, and document canal narrowing as water availability declined. Depositional changes occurred at the scale of minutes to hours. The characterization of Chaco water management as a "highly complex set of risk-aversion strategies" using multiple independent water sources directly parallels the model's cooperation network structure.

8. **Construction-agriculture scheduling conflict**: Wills et al. 2022 show that mortar-based wall construction was limited to July-September (the same window as agriculture), that water made up 30-40% of wall/ceiling volume and was available only from unpredictable storm runoff, and that no portion of the population was free from agricultural responsibilities. The construction window and agricultural season perfectly overlap, meaning environmental uncertainty affected both food production and signaling investment simultaneously.

9. **Theoretical framework**: Halstead & O'Shea 1989 and O'Shea & Halstead 1989 establish that buffering must match the scale and structure of variability, that spatial heterogeneity in perturbation effects makes exchange-based buffering valuable, and that small-field prehistoric agriculture experiences higher micro-scale spatial variability than modern farming, justifying the model's focus on exchange networks.

These findings collectively support the model's lambda(sigma) prediction: signaling investment should intensify when environmental uncertainty is high (high-frequency variability) but within a regime where buffering is still possible (favorable low-frequency baseline). The system collapses when the baseline itself shifts to unfavorable conditions and existing network structures lose their buffering value.

8. **Comparative case (Maya)**: Neiman 1997 demonstrates a strong correlation (Spearman's r = -0.69) between mean annual rainfall and terminal monument dates across 69 Maya sites. Areas with the highest rainfall experienced the earliest cessation of monument construction, interpreted as ecological disaster (soil erosion in high-rainfall zones). Under the ecological disaster scenario, "the cessation of monument construction was the predictable outcome of collapse in the range of political quality and the number of competitors" (pp. 279-280). The Maya case demonstrates how environmental degradation, not just drought, can drive signaling system collapse, providing a structural parallel to the Chaco case where prolonged drought was the precipitating environmental shock.

### From Safi 2015

**Findings:**

- Environmental uncertainty is a less likely explanation for the construction of great houses overall. Many episodes of construction at Chaco Canyon are within intervals of environmental productivity while lulls in construction appear to coincide with environmental downturns. Agricultural productivity increases during the time period in which southern Cibola great houses are constructed (p. 56; citing Dean 1992).
- Safi explicitly rejects environmental instability as a "causal mechanism" for great house construction, arguing that a mechanism other than environmental instability is necessary (p. 56).
- Great house communities in southern Cibola had momentary populations of 73-273 people (Table 6.2, p. 162), well below the 475-person threshold for reproductive viability, necessitating inter-community connections that signaling may have facilitated.

**Key observations:** Safi's rejection of environmental stress as a driver of construction timing requires careful engagement in our manuscript. Her observation that construction occurs during productive periods is not necessarily incompatible with our model: lambda(sigma) predicts that signaling becomes more valuable under uncertainty, but construction still requires resources that are only available during productive periods. The critical distinction is between current productivity (which enables construction) and environmental uncertainty (which makes the insurance function of cooperation networks, built through signaling, more valuable). Our model can accommodate construction during good years precisely because signaling is forward-looking: it builds networks that buffer against future shortfalls. The population data (73-273 per community) provide calibration for carrying capacity parameters.

### From Lekson 2002

**Findings:**

- Periods of "high temporal variability" in tree-ring departures occurred four times: 310-380, 750-1000, 1250-1560, and 1730-1825 (pp. 612-613; from Dean 1988, 1996).
- After incorporating "high seasonal variability" (unique regional disruption of precipitation patterns from 1250 to 1450), the compound periods of resource unpredictability remain the same, but the 1250-1560 period was uniquely severe because both types of variability operated simultaneously (pp. 612-613).
- The 1250-1450 disruption of seasonal precipitation was unprecedented: "a 200-year, regional-scale disruption of a climatic pattern that characterized the Southwest for the preceding 550 years and the following 550 years" (p. 613; citing Dean 1996:43).
- Resource unpredictability is the strongest cross-cultural predictor of warfare (R = 0.631, p < 0.001), from Ember and Ember 1992 (p. 611).
- The 750-1000 period of high temporal variability overlaps with the early development of Chaco (construction begins ~850-900) (p. 619).
- Chaco "expanded beyond the ecological limits of the San Juan Basin only after 1000; that is, after the 750-1000 episode of high temporal variability ended" (p. 619; citing Judge 1989).
- "From the perspective of the individual farmer, living in the pattern 'low temporal variability' must have been perceived as more predictable or more constant than regimes of 'high temporal variability'" (p. 612).

**Key observations:** Lekson 2002 provides the most important integration of Dean's dendroclimatological variability periodization with Chaco's political chronology. Three findings are particularly relevant for the sigma parameterization. First, the 750-1000 period of high temporal variability coincides with Chaco's genesis, supporting the interpretation that the system arose as a response to resource unpredictability (high sigma creating demand for cooperation networks). Second, Chaco's expansion after 1000 occurred during a period of relatively low temporal variability, suggesting that the cooperation networks built under high uncertainty remained valuable even as conditions improved, consistent with the model's prediction that networks have momentum. Third, the compound unpredictability after 1250 (both temporal and seasonal variability) created conditions far more severe than anything the Chaco system had been designed to handle, explaining why the system collapsed rather than adapting.
