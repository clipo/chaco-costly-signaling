# Construction Costs Evidence

Compiled from claims extractions: `docs/references/claims/`. All page numbers refer to the PDF/publication pages documented in the claims files. See individual claims files for full quotes and context.

## Key Questions

- What was the labor cost of great house construction at Chaco, measured in person-days or person-hours?
- How does construction investment scale with great house size and over time?
- What were the transport costs for timber and other construction materials?
- Does the empirical evidence support the model's C = 0.35 (35% productivity diversion) parameter?

## Evidence

### From Kantner & Vaughn 2012

**Findings:**

- Construction intensity during Classic Bonito "clearly exceeded the labor available from canyon residents" (p. 75; citing Lekson 1984, Metcalf 2003)
- More than 200,000 timbers "had to be hand-carried across the San Juan Basin from distant mountains" (p. 75; citing Windes and McKenna 2001)
- Figure 2 shows great house construction labor totals (person-hours/1000) at 5-year increments, with peak construction around AD 1080-1100 (from Lekson 1984, p. 263, Fig. 5.2)
- Over 50% of ceramics and 30% of lithics at Chaco were non-local, and few imported items ever left the canyon (p. 75; citing Cameron 2001, Toll 2006)

**Key observations:** Kantner & Vaughn provide secondary evidence on construction costs (drawing on Lekson 1984 and Windes & McKenna 2001). The Figure 2 labor curve is particularly valuable for tracking temporal patterns of construction investment. The one-directional flow of materials into the canyon supports a consumption/signaling model over redistribution.

### From Lentz et al. 2021

**Findings:**

- Local conifers (juniper and pinyon) made up 96% of construction wood at Chaco Canyon sites during the Basketmaker III period, but thereafter high-elevation woods from distant mountains became dominant (p. 11; citing Toll 1985)
- At Pueblo Alto (AD 1020-1140), pinyon comprised 31% of tree ring specimens for floors/ceilings while juniper contributed only 9%, indicating juniper was infrequently used for construction beams during the Bonito phase (p. 11; citing Toll 1987)
- Juniper wood for construction was being imported from the Zuni Mountains, 75 km away, as early as AD 850 (pp. 6-7; citing Guiterman et al. 2016)
- Over 1 million m^3 of fuelwood were needed during the 340-year Bonito phase; daily fuel consumption far exceeded construction timber demand as a driver of deforestation (p. 13; citing Samuels and Betancourt 1982)
- At Pueblo Alto, juniper and pinyon together comprised 32% of overall fuel use; juniper was in 56% of firepits and 73% of warming pits, while pinyon was in 44% and 36% respectively (p. 11; citing Toll 1987)

**Key observations:** Lentz et al. 2021 demonstrates that the cost parameter C for construction signaling was not static but increased over time as local resources were depleted. The shift from 96% local conifers (BMIII) to predominantly distant high-elevation woods (Bonito phase) represents a substantial increase in procurement costs (transport from 75+ km vs. local harvest). The paper also shows that construction timber was only part of the wood demand: daily fuel needs for cooking domesticated foods represented a much larger total extraction pressure. For the signaling model, this means that monument construction costs included not just direct labor but also the opportunity cost of timber procurement in a resource-depleted landscape, making the C parameter more onerous than simple labor estimates would suggest.

### From Wills 2017

**Findings:**

- The construction costs of the 18 identified headgates and associated intake ditches "were inconsequential compared to the enormous amount of material, sophisticated building methods, and communal organization of production sustained over decades to build great houses" (p. 393). The entire corpus of headgate material in Chaco would be "less material than required to construct a single large room in Pueblo Bonito" (p. 393).
- 90% or more of great house architecture by mass or size in the San Juan Basin occurs within Chaco Canyon proper and/or within 20 km of Pueblo Bonito (p. 395; citing Cordell 1982, Mills 2002, Van Dyke 2007, Wills 2000, Windes et al. 2000)
- Water control features appear to have been built late in the Classic Bonito Phase: headgate complexes probably date after ca. AD 1075 based on geological context and associated ceramics; the Chetro Ketl gridded field system postdates AD 1100 (pp. 382-393)
- The argument that water control technology financed great house construction is challenged: "there is no indication that these features required skilled labor of the sort evident in great house construction" (p. 393)
- Great house construction is instead proposed to have been financed by labor recruitment to corporate land-holding groups rather than surplus food extraction (pp. 394-399)

**Key observations:** Wills 2017 recasts the relationship between agricultural infrastructure and monument construction. Rather than water control generating the surplus that funded great houses, the water control features appear to be a late, minor addition to a construction program already centuries old. The trivial material investment in water control (less than one room's worth of masonry) relative to great house construction underscores that the signaling cost C = 0.35 is primarily a labor cost, not a technological capital cost. The labor-recruitment model is directly compatible with the signaling framework: great houses attract labor through their signal value, rather than requiring surplus extraction to motivate construction.

### From Neiman 1997

**Findings:**

- Maya monuments (altars, stairs, stelae) represent "expenditures of resources to assemble and supervise the individuals who quarried, transported, assembled, and carved them" (p. 270). These are fitness costs diverted from maintenance, mate acquisition, and offspring production.
- Monument costs were structured to guarantee signal reliability: supporting a literate elite, skilled carvers, and the labor required for construction made the signal "impossible to counterfeit" (p. 271)
- The costs included not just physical construction but also training and maintenance of specialized skills: "the complex system of hieroglyphic writing, the accompanying iconographic repertoire, and in carving and painting both on stone" (p. 270)
- Several elite members "were personally engaged in wasteful activities," including self-inflicted bloodletting rituals that served as advertisements of political quality (p. 271; citing Schele & Miller 1986, Stuart 1988)

**Key observations:** Although Neiman analyzes Maya rather than Chacoan monuments, the structural parallels in cost composition are instructive. Both systems involve: (1) labor mobilization for quarrying, transport, and construction; (2) specialized knowledge requirements (Maya: hieroglyphic literacy; Chaco: masonry techniques, astronomical alignment); (3) personal costs borne by elites in addition to communal labor costs. The anti-counterfeiting argument (costs structured to prevent fakery) provides an additional dimension for evaluating Chaco's construction cost parameter beyond simple labor estimates.

**Findings:**

### From Kohler et al. 2023

**Findings:**

- Typical house use-life was sub-generational until about AD 1020 (for hamlets), never less than about 28 years in villages. Frequent movement likely reduced intergenerational transmission of wealth, especially inheritance of superior fields. (Sec. 2c)
- As "room to walk" (available dry-farming niche) decreased, duration of house occupation increased. (Sec. 2c)
- Chacoan Great Houses drove a marked increase in regional Gini coefficients around AD 900. (Sec. 2f)
- Systems of balanced reciprocal exchange "would have done little to level these societies," while "ceremonial systems may have been effective leveling devices in some Pueblo I societies." (Sec. 4)

**Key observations:** Kohler et al. 2023's finding that house use-life was sub-generational before AD 1020 implies that the commitment to sustained construction investment represented by great houses was a significant departure from prevailing practice. The willingness to invest in permanent architecture when the norm was short-duration housing is itself evidence that construction represented a costly commitment signal. The increasing persistence of settlement as farmland became scarce provides the mechanism: as groups became more committed to place, the payoff to monument investment (signaling to neighbors, attracting labor, deterring conflict) increased.

### From Glatz & Plourde 2011

**Findings:**

- Landscape monuments were constructed "away from urban centers," implying additional transport and construction costs beyond what urban construction would require (p. 33, abstract)
- A CST-based model generates spatial expectations about monument placement, implying that placement costs are part of the signaling calculus (p. 33, abstract)

For Chaco, the analogous observation is that outlier great houses required construction effort in locations distant from the canyon core, with materials and possibly labor traveling substantial distances. The strategic choice of location is itself a cost that contributes to signal honesty. Full text required for specific Hittite construction cost data.

### From Dungan et al. 2018

**Findings:**

- Total viewshed analysis of 269 great houses and 161 great kivas shows that great houses were systematically placed in high-visibility locations (approximately half in the 8th-10th visibility deciles), while great kivas show a more even distribution across moderate and lower deciles (pp. 913-914).
- K-S tests confirm that the great house distribution within visibility deciles differs significantly from the uniform landscape background (Max D = 0.24, significant at alpha = 0.001; Table 1).
- Great houses and great kivas differ significantly from each other in visibility placement (K-S Max D = 0.16, significant at alpha = 0.001; Table 1).
- The visibility preference is geographically consistent across the Chaco World (Table 2), suggesting a system-wide construction logic rather than locally variable practices.
- Great houses are inherently more visible from a distance than great kivas due to their above-ground massing; this "legibility" was frequently enhanced through the choice of highly visible building sites (p. 916).
- The "overbuilding that defines great houses was frequently enhanced by the selection of highly visible building sites" (p. 918).

**Key observations:** Dungan et al. 2018 adds a dimension to construction costs that goes beyond labor and materials: the cost of optimal site selection for visibility. Choosing high-visibility locations may have involved trade-offs with other desirable site attributes (proximity to water, agricultural land, shelter from wind). This additional cost component supports the signaling interpretation because it shows that construction investment was not just about creating a large structure but about ensuring that structure could be seen from across the local landscape. The distinction between great houses (external visibility, signal to distant audiences) and great kivas (internal experience, ceremonial function) maps onto different components of the signaling model: Layer 2 (intergroup assessment) depends on signal visibility, while the internal social mechanisms operated within the great kiva setting. This is direct evidence that great house placement was engineered for signal broadcast effectiveness.

### From Wills et al. 2022

**Findings:**

- Between AD 1050 and 1080, Lekson (1984:258-259) identified 11 construction stages requiring 20,000 to 55,000 person hours and 12 stages requiring 55,000 to 192,000 person hours (p. 458). He identified at least 32 major "construction events" at great houses dating between approximately AD 1025 and 1130, with roughly 90% of those hours consumed by creating mortar and erecting walls (p. 461; Lekson 1984:258-260, 278).
- Levin (1992) calculated 13 km of exposed masonry walls in Chaco Canyon National Historic Park (p. 458). The largest great houses encompassed 100-600+ rooms and 8,000-24,000 m^2 (p. 458).
- Water made up 30% to 40% of wall and ceiling volume as mortar and plaster. Water for large-scale construction was mainly available as storm runoff in late summer and early fall, which was "extremely unpredictable in frequency, size, and location" (p. 460; Vivian 1992; Love 1977).
- The construction window for mortar-based wall erection was limited to July through September, precisely overlapping with the agricultural growing season. Walls and roofs had to be completed before cold weather (below 5C average daily minimum) (p. 461; McHenry 1984).
- Assembling work parties required diverting labor from agricultural activities. There are "no indications that any portion of the able-bodied population would have been wholly free from agricultural activities and responsibilities during this critical period" (p. 461).
- All building material except timber was local, but timber was "initially been a nearby resource but over time was acquired from distant forested zones" (p. 460; Betancourt et al. 1986; Windes and McKenna 2001).

**Key observations:** Wills et al. 2022 provide the most detailed analysis of the scheduling conflict between construction and agriculture. The July-September construction window is exactly the agricultural growing season, meaning every hour of construction labor directly competed with food production. The water dependency of mortar (30-40% of wall volume) added another layer of unpredictability: builders depended on the same storm runoff events that farmers needed for crops. This dual dependency on unpredictable water reinforces the model's C parameter as capturing not just direct labor costs but also the opportunity cost of construction timing.

### From Scarborough et al. 2018

**Findings:**

- The canal system at Chaco evolved through multiple phases, with initial smaller channels (OSL age AD 900+/-100) giving way to a larger constructed canal with successive berms (Unit C-4: OSL AD 1000+/-100; AMS AD 997-1150 at 95.4%), and progressive narrowing as water availability declined (p. 876).
- Canal maintenance required quick, coordinated labor responses to unpredictable water availability, drawing upon labor from great house and small site occupants (pp. 872, 884).
- The canal system represents additional infrastructure costs beyond monument construction: a parallel labor demand for agricultural water management that competed for the same workforce used in great house construction.

**Key observations:** Scarborough et al. 2018 add an important cost dimension: the labor diverted to canal construction and maintenance competed with the labor required for great house construction. Both activities depended on the same seasonal window and the same workforce, compounding the scheduling pressures documented by Wills et al. 2022.

### From Van Dyke 2004

**Findings:**

- Late Bonito phase (A.D. 1100-1140) McElmo-style great houses represent "Class II" construction requiring 55,000-90,000 person-hours, approximately one-third to one-half the labor of Classic Bonito "Class IV" construction requiring 170,000-192,000 person-hours (p. 423; citing Lekson 1986:257-269).
- Late Bonito builders employed standardized McElmo unit templates that "would have facilitated speedy and straightforward construction of multiple buildings" (p. 423).
- Builders used illusory architectural features to inflate signal appearance: unfaced secondary parallel walls along the south sides of Casa Chiquita and Kin Kletso, with internal crosswalls creating functionless 150 x 50 cm cells that added mass without structural purpose (pp. 422-423).
- Beam transport was the most time-consuming activity in great house construction. Some Late Bonito great houses recycled beams from older Classic Bonito structures (p. 423).

**Key observations:** Van Dyke 2004 documents a significant reduction in construction costs during the Late Bonito phase, from 170,000-192,000 person-hours (Classic Bonito) to 55,000-90,000 person-hours (Late Bonito). This temporal decline in C is important for the model: it shows that signaling investment was responsive to labor availability. The illusory architectural features (false walls creating appearance of greater mass) represent a cost-reduction innovation that attempts to maintain signal intensity while reducing actual investment, a form of signal inflation that raises questions about honesty enforcement. The standardized template approach represents a shift from bespoke to mass-produced signaling, trading signal uniqueness for construction speed and cost efficiency.

### From Wills et al. 2014

**Findings:**

- The overall amount of spruce/fir in construction timber increased dramatically around A.D. 1030, concurrent with the beginning of the Classic Bonito Phase. However, spruce/fir was a consistent part of great house construction throughout the Bonito Phase, representing a third of all timber elements in the 11th century (pp. 3-4).
- Ponderosa and spruce/fir covary strongly through time (Spearman's rank correlation: r=0.66, P=0.01), indicating they "were part of a mix or 'recipe' for construction" rather than spruce/fir replacing depleted ponderosa (p. 4; Table 1).
- GIS least-cost path analysis identifies areas east of Chaco Canyon (above 2,500 m within its watershed) as least-cost potential ponderosa sources, challenging the assumption that most timber came from the Chuska Mountains 75-80 km to the west (p. 6; Fig. 7).
- Construction energy investment began declining in the late A.D. 1000s, before the onset of documented drought periods (pp. 3-4; Fig. 5).

**Key observations:** Wills et al. 2014 challenge the assumption that timber procurement costs escalated over time due to progressive depletion of nearby sources. The covariance of ponderosa and spruce/fir suggests long-distance procurement was built into the construction cost from the beginning rather than being a forced response to local depletion. If correct, the cost parameter C may have been relatively stable rather than increasing, which would alter predictions about the temporal trajectory of signaling investment. The finding that construction investment declined before drought is significant: it suggests endogenous factors (declining returns, competition from Aztec) rather than rising costs may have driven the reduction in signaling. Note: this interpretation is contested by Lentz et al. 2021, who argue deforestation was anthropogenic and progressive.

## Synthesis

The processed sources establish several complementary perspectives on construction costs. Kantner & Vaughn 2012, Lentz et al. 2021, and Wills 2017 provide Chaco-specific evidence showing that construction costs were substantial (exceeding canyon labor capacity), increased over time (as local timber was depleted), and were primarily labor costs rather than technological capital costs. Neiman 1997 provides a comparative framework from the Maya case showing how construction costs are structured to prevent counterfeiting. Trigger 1990 (abstract only) provides the foundational theoretical argument that energy expenditure itself, not the resulting structure, is the currency of power symbolism, meaning the C parameter is the signal, not the cost of producing the signal. Glatz & Plourde 2011 (abstract only) add that landscape monument placement (away from centers) increases costs strategically. Van Dyke 2004 documents a significant reduction in construction costs during the Late Bonito phase (from 170,000-192,000 to 55,000-90,000 person-hours), including the use of illusory architectural features to inflate signal appearance at reduced cost. Wills et al. 2014 challenge the assumption that costs escalated due to local timber depletion, arguing that long-distance procurement was always part of the construction recipe; however, Lentz et al. 2021 contest this interpretation. The C = 0.35 parameter appears reasonable given evidence that construction clearly exceeded local labor capacity and required materials transported from 75+ km, though the parameter should be understood as capturing total productivity diversion (including opportunity costs) rather than just direct labor hours, and the temporal trajectory of C (rising vs. stable) remains an active empirical debate.

### From Safi 2015

**Findings:**

- Three southern Cibola outlier great houses are much smaller than canyon great houses: Largo Gap ~22 rooms, Cox Ranch Pueblo ~44 rooms, Cerro Pomo ~43 rooms, compared to Pueblo Bonito's 350+ rooms (pp. 170, 229, Table 6.14)
- All three incorporate Chacoan architectural features to varying degrees: Type II-style masonry, compound walls (Largo Gap, Cox Ranch), core-and-veneer walls (Cox Ranch), over-tall rooms (Cox Ranch), non-local high-elevation timbers from the White Mountains ~80 km away (Cox Ranch), foundation trench and footing stones (Cerro Pomo) (pp. 170, 229)
- Architectural variability correlates with construction timing: later structures incorporate more Chacoan features, and remodeling events added Chacoan characteristics to existing structures, consistent with a signaling escalation dynamic (pp. 169, 271)
- Multiple episodes of remodeling at Largo Gap; possible PIII remodeling/use episode at Cerro Pomo (Table 6.14, p. 229)

**Key observations:** Safi's outlier great house data provide calibration points for the lower end of the signal investment distribution. If Pueblo Bonito represents peak investment (~350 rooms, 32 kivas, timber from 75+ km), southern Cibola great houses (22-44 rooms, limited Chacoan features, timber from ~80 km) represent communities with lower "quality" (smaller population, fewer resources) investing proportionally less, consistent with the Spence condition of quality-dependent investment. The remodeling-as-escalation pattern suggests signaling intensity increased over time in response to competitive pressure from neighboring communities, supporting the model's dynamic predictions.

### From Crown & Wills 2018

**Findings:**

- Pueblo Bonito was "a never-ending project of construction, remodelling, renewal and destruction" whose builders never considered it finished (p. 890). Construction spanned from the ninth century through the early twelfth century, with only one interruption.
- Tree-ring dates indicate a construction hiatus between c. AD 975 and 1040 (~65 years), during which existing rooms were "left unmaintained and then partly collapsed before new construction in the A.D. 1040s" (p. 899; citing Windes & Ford 1996: 301).
- The eleventh century saw intensified construction: at least 80 new rooms from AD 1040-1050, 24 rooms from AD 1050-1070, and 160-170 rooms from AD 1070-1100 (p. 899; citing Windes & Ford 1996: 302-303). This totals approximately 264-274 new rooms in 60 years.
- Early construction (AD 850-925) used diverse masonry techniques (large blocks with little mortar, tabular sandstone, jacal, widely-spaced courses with mud), suggesting household-level construction rather than coordinated building (pp. 897-898). Later construction employed standardized masonry types (Types II-IV).
- When construction resumed after the hiatus, the West Court surface had risen to approximately the level of first-storey rooftops through a combination of wind-blown sand, flood deposits, and possibly intentional filling. New rooms were built on accumulated deposits (p. 899; Judd 1964: 60; Lekson 1984: 133; Stein et al. 2003: 50).
- Around AD 1100, termination rituals involved sealing rooms with masonry, placing 173 ceramic vessels (including 112 cylinder jars) in Room 28, and setting fires that partially vitrified floors and collapsed upper rooms. Similar fires destroyed adjacent rooms along the northern arc of the West Court (p. 901).
- Post-florescence occupation continued into the AD 1200s with reduced investment focused on renovating ritual structures and constructing small semi-subterranean houses rather than monumental architecture (p. 901; Windes & Ford 1996: 303).

**Key observations:** Crown & Wills 2018 provides the most direct empirical support for the model's signal depreciation parameter (delta = 0.10). The ~65-year construction hiatus demonstrates physical signal decay: rooms collapsed when maintenance ceased. The subsequent construction boom (264-274 rooms in 60 years) quantifies the cost of signal renewal after a depreciation period, showing that restoration requires investment exceeding ongoing maintenance. The transition from diverse, household-level masonry (AD 850-925) to standardized construction methods (post-AD 1020) documents the emergence of coordinated signaling investment, consistent with the model's prediction that collective signaling requires overcoming coordination problems. The termination rituals (c. AD 1100) demonstrate that signal depreciation could be intentional as well as natural: deliberate destruction of architectural and material capital (112 cylinder jars, the largest whole-vessel assemblage in the region) represents a mechanism not captured by the continuous decay parameter. The compounding effect of surface accumulation between construction phases (builders constructing on top of debris and windblown sand) provides a mechanism by which signal maintenance costs escalate over time, even without changes in labor costs per unit of construction.

### From Schwarz 2023

**Findings:**

- Maya architectural monuments were "constructed with relatively modest annual labor outlays, as these monuments were built up over time," meaning the amount of architectural cost "does not have to be high on an absolute scale, if the architectural building program is maintained over a long period of time" (p. 6; citing Abrams 1994:114-119).
- The organizing and motivating of labor was "perhaps the most important factor in creating a costly outlay," more significant than material costs (p. 6; citing Abrams 1994:114-119, 120-122).
- Elites created a hierarchy of social power "in part, through periodically building up of the Maya acropolis of temples and palaces" over time (p. 6; citing Abrams 1994:127-129; Mann 1986:119; Trigger 1990).
- During the Terminal Classic (AD 800-950), monument construction continued at reduced scale with "new building and monument designs," representing cost shifting rather than complete cessation of construction activity (pp. 10, 14-15).
- McElmo-style Late Bonito construction at Chaco (Van Dyke 2004) represents an analogous cost-shifting dynamic: "Class II" labor estimates (55,000-90,000 person-hours) are roughly half of Classic Bonito "Class IV" estimates (170,000-192,000 person-hours).

**Key observations:** Schwarz 2023 provides a comparative framework for understanding construction costs as annual flows rather than lump-sum investments. The Maya case (Abrams 1994) demonstrates that even impressive monuments can be built with modest annual labor budgets when construction extends over decades, directly paralleling Crown and Wills (2018) on Pueblo Bonito's 250+ year construction history. The cost-shifting concept applies directly to the Late Bonito McElmo-style great houses, where construction investment dropped by roughly half while maintaining architectural presence. For the model's C parameter, this suggests that annual construction cost as a fraction of total productivity may be lower than the cumulative investment would imply, and that the parameter should be treated as time-varying rather than constant.
