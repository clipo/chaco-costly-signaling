# Conflict Patterns Evidence

Compiled from claims extractions: `docs/references/claims/`. All page numbers refer to the PDF/publication pages documented in the claims files. See individual claims files for full quotes and context.

## Key Questions

- What is the evidence for and against "Pax Chaco" (reduced violence during florescence)?
- Did conflict levels change before, during, and after the Chaco era?
- Does the spatial and temporal distribution of violence evidence support the mutual assessment prediction (Layer 2)?
- Was violence concentrated at the margins of the Chaco system or within it?

## Evidence

### From Kantner & Vaughn 2012

**Findings:**

- "The evidence for violent interaction that is so common before and especially after the Chaco era was largely absent during Chaco's height; Lekson refers to the pattern as a 'Pax Chaco'" (p. 77; citing Lekson 2002, LeBlanc 1999)
- Some violence did occur during the Chaco era: "some forms of violence occurred in specific times and places, especially in the years prior to Chaco's collapse" and "in areas outside of Chaco's most direct influence" (p. 77; citing Kantner 1999, Kohler and Kramer Turner 2006, Kohler et al. 2009)
- The development of the pilgrimage center "is correlated with widespread evidence of prosocial, cooperative behavior, at least in the beginning" (p. 77)

**Key observations:** The temporal pattern (low violence during florescence, high before and after) supports the model's Layer 2 prediction that monument signaling deters intergroup conflict. The spatial nuance (violence at margins of Chaco influence) is also consistent: the model predicts conflict reduction where signaling is visible, not necessarily at the periphery where signal strength attenuates. The caveat "at least in the beginning" suggests the system may have degraded over time.

### From Enquist & Leimar 1987 (filed as Enquist_Leimar_1983)

**Note:** The PDF in the repository is the 1987 paper (J. theor. Biol. 127:187-205), which extends the 1983 sequential assessment model to incorporate resource value variation. Both papers develop the sequential assessment framework central to Layer 2.

**Findings:**

- The utility of a contest strategy is U = pV - C, where p is probability of winning, V is resource value, and C is cost. This formalizes the trade-off between conflict gains and fighting costs. (p. 188)
- In the sequential assessment model, contestants acquire information about relative fighting ability through costly interaction steps, updating estimates and persisting until their estimate of relative quality crosses a switching-line threshold. Assessment errors are normally distributed with standard deviation sigma. (pp. 191-192)
- As resource value increases, ESS strategies prescribe more costly and persistent behavior: fight duration, offensive behavior frequency, and probability of winning all increase. This prediction is supported empirically across 18 species (Table 1, p. 199). (pp. 190, 193-194)
- Contests between closely matched opponents are the longest and costliest. "For contests where an individual, A, has a greater V than an opponent, B, the longest contests will be those where B is slightly stronger" (pp. 194-195). Mismatched contests resolve quickly.
- In owner-intruder interactions with information asymmetry, owners become more persistent as resource value increases. "The intruder, not having perfect information about the resource, tends to win the 'wrong' fights" over low-value resources while losing high-value contests (pp. 195-196). The owner's overall utility (0.26) exceeds the intruder's (0.10) despite winning only slightly more than half of interactions (p. 196).
- Decreasing assessment noise (sigma) makes fight outcomes "more determined by relative fighting ability than by asymmetries in subjective resource value," meaning accurate assessment reduces the role of motivation and increases the role of actual quality in determining outcomes (p. 195).
- Increasing resource value variance has a similar effect to increasing assessment noise: when resources vary widely in value, contest outcomes become more dependent on how much each party values the resource rather than on actual fighting ability (p. 195).

**Key observations:** Enquist & Leimar provide the formal mechanism underlying Layer 2's conflict deterrence prediction. Monument signals map onto the sigma parameter: by providing pre-contest information about group quality, great houses reduce the number of costly assessment steps needed for accurate evaluation, lowering the expected cost of intergroup interaction. The prediction that matched contests are costliest explains why signaling would be most valuable among communities of similar competitive ability, consistent with the concentration of great house construction in the canyon core where multiple large communities coexisted. The owner-intruder information asymmetry model maps onto the Chaco landscape: established great house communities (owners) have better information about local resource value than potential migrants or raiders (intruders), and monument construction communicates this information, reducing the frequency of costly mismatched contests.

### From Barclay et al. 2021

**Findings:**

- Signaling systems are "likely in Stag Hunts, Harmony Games and Prisoner's Dilemmas, but not in Snowdrift Games" (Section 2g)
- Costlier signaling is predicted "where communities are more fluid and residentially mobile, conflict and competition are more common" (Section 3)
- The model shows that signaling can sustain cooperation even in one-shot encounters, without requiring repeated interactions or direct reciprocity (Section 3)

**Key observations:** Barclay et al. 2021's game-type analysis is relevant to understanding conflict patterns. If Chaco intergroup dynamics were characterized by Stag Hunt dynamics (high value of coordination, high cost of coordination failure), signaling should be effective at reducing conflict. The prediction that costlier signaling arises where "conflict and competition are more common" provides a mechanism for why monument construction would intensify in response to increased conflict pressure, consistent with the observation that the construction boom coincided with population growth and potential resource competition.

### From Kohler et al. 2023

**Findings:**

- Periods with high wealth inequality are followed by periods of high violence, and conversely periods of high violence are followed by periods of high wealth inequality. This is a statistically significant bidirectional tendency documented for the VEPIIN area. (Sec. 2f)
- The steep decline in CMV Gini coefficients at the same time that Ginis across the northern Southwest increased (driven by Chacoan Great Houses) suggests that the Chaco model was "actively resisted" in some areas. (Sec. 2f)
- Male-biased sex ratios in the CMV in the 1200s coupled with female-biased ratios in Chaco/Aztec areas have been used to suggest raiding for women by the Chaco/Aztec system. (Sec. 3)
- The late 900s/early 1000s violence in the CMV may represent "resistance to initial Chacoan attempts to expand." (Sec. 3)

**Key observations:** The bidirectional inequality-violence relationship is important for the model. It suggests a cycle: inequality generates resentment leading to violence, while violence creates conditions for inequality to reassert itself (perhaps because strong leaders are favored in violent periods). The evidence for Chacoan coercion (raiding for women, resistance to expansion) complicates the purely cooperative signaling interpretation and suggests that monument signaling may have had both cooperative (Layer 2 deterrence) and coercive dimensions.

### From Gillreath-Brown & Kohler 2024

**Findings:**

- The social chaos of the 1200s, including high population movement, frequent conflict, and eventual depopulation of large portions of the Southwest, is connected to the cessation of the El Nino pattern. (pp. 470-471; citing Kohler et al. 2014; Lipe 1995, 2010; Schwindt et al. 2016)

**Key observations:** The timing of increased conflict (post-AD 1200) coincides with both the La Nina climate shift and the breakdown of the Chacoan regional system. For the model, this supports the prediction that the collapse of signaling-based cooperation networks leads to increased intergroup conflict, as groups lose access to the crisis-buffering benefits that monument signaling had previously provided.

## Synthesis

### From Hawkes & Bliege Bird 2002

**Findings:**

- "Social prestige functions like a peacock's tail or the song of a songbird. It attracts collaborators and deters rivals" (p. 61; quoting Zahavi 1995). The deterrence function of costly displays is a key mechanism for conflict reduction.
- "Demonstration of the capacity to bear the cost of these displays substitutes for overt threats, which potentially lead to fights that could be even more costly for the signaler and at least some of the audience" (p. 59). Among Arabian babblers, costly sentinel duty replaces direct aggression as the mechanism for establishing dominance.
- In the human case, "a reputation for good hunting generally affects a man's social standing relative to other men in foraging communities" (p. 61), determining "how desirable a neighbor and ally, and how dangerous a rival, a man might be" (p. 59).

**Key observations:** Hawkes & Bliege Bird 2002 provide the theoretical mechanism linking costly displays to conflict deterrence: displays substitute for direct aggression by allowing assessment of relative quality without the costs of fighting. This maps directly onto Layer 2, where great house construction signals group capacity, enabling mutual assessment that reduces costly intergroup conflict. The babbler analogy is apt: sentinel duty is both a costly signal and a direct contribution to group defense.

### From Neiman 1997

**Findings:**

- Competitive political signaling through monument construction functioned as a substitute for direct political contests: "if the two parties differ significantly in competitive ability, communication allows both of them to avoid the fitness costs of a contest whose outcome is a foregone conclusion" (p. 270).
- As the Maya collapse proceeded, "monument-constructing elites, or individuals competing for that status, abandoned their original sites to enter political competition at sites on average 65 km distant, thus increasing competition, and prolonging monument construction at those recipient sites" (pp. 282-283).
- Increased levels of violence during the Late Classic period coincided with the cessation of monument construction, supporting the interpretation that signaling and violence were inversely related (p. 278; citing Demarest 1992, 1996).
- Political instability and inter-polity warfare "received increased attention among Mayanists in recent years" as a factor in the collapse, but "explanations that invoke warfare remain incomplete unless they reference changing ecological or social conditions that changed the costs and benefits of violence" (p. 278).

**Key observations:** Neiman's finding that monument cessation coincided with increased warfare provides a key comparative datum for the Chaco case. The inverse relationship between signaling investment and violence levels supports Layer 2: when signaling systems function, conflict is suppressed through mutual assessment; when signaling ceases, the conflict-deterrence mechanism fails and violence increases. The Maya elite migration pattern provides an additional dynamic that may apply to Chaco.

### From Van Dyke et al. 2024

**Findings:**

- The audible reach of a conch-shell trumpet blast from a great house consistently mapped onto associated habitation sites across five communities (p. 788). This broadcast range defines a zone of community membership and potential coordination.
- The acoustic community-definition function suggests great houses were positioned to maximize their command over surrounding settlements, consistent with the Layer 2 prediction that monument signals serve to organize and bound groups for mutual assessment purposes.
- Conch shells were associated with the most elaborate burial at Pueblo Bonito (Room 33), linking acoustic signaling instruments to elite authority (p. 780).

**Key observations:** Van Dyke et al. 2024's soundshed analysis provides an additional mechanism for understanding how great houses reduced conflict: by acoustically defining community boundaries, trumpet blasts could serve as assembly calls for coordinated responses (including defense). The 1418m range means that a warning blast from a great house could alert an entire community within seconds, far faster than visual communication. The non-random placement of habitations within this zone suggests that community members valued proximity to this broadcast capability, which is consistent with the Layer 2 prediction that signaling reduces assessment costs and enables coordinated group responses.

### From Dungan et al. 2018

**Findings:**

- Great houses were systematically placed in high-visibility locations (approximately half in the 8th-10th visibility deciles), making them visible to individuals across the wider landscape, not just to those in the immediate vicinity (pp. 913-914, 918).
- The visibility preference is geographically consistent across the Chaco World, indicating a system-wide logic rather than locally variable placement (Table 2).
- Great kivas, by contrast, were not preferentially placed for landscape-scale visibility, suggesting they served a different communicative function (pp. 914-916).
- The pattern of high-visibility placement may also relate to pilgrimage: "The choice of highly visible locations may have allowed great houses to serve as landmarks for, and to make an impression on, visitors entering a community" (p. 917).

**Key observations:** Dungan et al. 2018's visibility findings are relevant to the conflict-deterrence mechanism because signal observability is a precondition for mutual assessment. If great houses are invisible to approaching groups, they cannot function as deterrent signals. The systematic placement of great houses in high-visibility locations ensures that any group approaching a community would encounter visual evidence of the community's capacity before engaging in potentially costly interaction. This maps directly onto Layer 2: the visibility of monument signals enables pre-encounter assessment of relative group quality, reducing the probability of costly miscalculation. The geographic consistency of this pattern across the Chaco World suggests that the deterrence function was not limited to the canyon core but operated throughout the system.

### From Enquist & Leimar 1983 (original sequential assessment paper)

**Note:** This is the foundational 1983 paper (J. theor. Biol. 102:387-410) that introduced the sequential assessment model. A separate entry above covers the 1987 follow-up paper (J. theor. Biol. 127:187-205), which extends the model to incorporate resource value variation.

**Findings:**

- Contests proceed through a sequence of costly interactions, each providing a noisy observation of relative fighting ability theta = ln(c_B/c_A). Observation noise is normally distributed with standard deviation sigma. After n steps, the sampling error reduces to sigma/sqrt(n). (pp. 390-391)
- The ESS is a switching line in causal factor space: a contestant retreats when its accumulated estimate of relative quality falls below this threshold. The ESS is pure and unique for given parameter values c and sigma. (pp. 392, 394)
- Contests between equally matched opponents are the most costly: "it is more costly to meet an opponent of equal strength than one that is stronger. This is due to the assessment taking place during the fight. A weaker animal will quickly realize that the situation is unfavourable and give up." (p. 396)
- Higher assessment noise sigma produces longer and costlier fights. For c = 0.005, expected cost rises from 0.029 (sigma = 0.5) to 0.049 (sigma = 1.0) to 0.063 (sigma = 1.5). (Table 1, p. 399)
- Assessment-based fighting produces positive expected utility (0.451 for V = 1), while wars of attrition without assessment yield zero expected utility. Assessment produces "good fighting economy" with costs only ~10% of the benefit. (pp. 395, 401)
- Assessment-based strategies are evolutionarily stable against convention-based alternatives. A convention mutant (e.g., always defer in intruder role) earns strictly less than the assessment strategy and cannot invade. (p. 402)
- Probability of correct outcome approaches certainty for |theta| > 0.5 but assessment errors ("mistakes") occur for closely matched opponents. (p. 396, Fig. 4)

**Key observations:** The 1983 paper provides the formal mechanism that the 1987 paper then extends. Three results are directly relevant to Layer 2's conflict deterrence prediction. First, the sigma-cost relationship (Table 1) provides the quantitative basis for the claim that monument signals reduce conflict costs: if great houses reduce effective sigma from 1.5 to 0.5, expected conflict costs drop by more than half. Second, the equal-opponent cost result explains why signaling is most valuable in the canyon core where multiple large groups coexisted: assessment errors and costs peak when theta is near zero, exactly the situation among communities of similar competitive ability. Third, the evolutionary stability of assessment against conventions means that the signaling-based conflict reduction mechanism is robust: it cannot be undermined by groups adopting simple deference rules. The comparison with the war of attrition (zero utility without assessment vs. 0.451 with assessment) quantifies the selective advantage of assessment, and by extension, of any mechanism (like monument signaling) that improves assessment accuracy.

### From Safi 2015

**Findings:**

- Weaker elites or groups can recognize their inferior competitive abilities based on the extravagant costs that go into competitive signals and potentially avoid conflicts they cannot win; followers align with those most likely to provide resource benefits and protection (p. 48; citing Aranyosi 1999:357)

**Key observations:** Safi's conflict avoidance argument directly supports Layer 2 of our model. Her second viewer response (recognizing superior signals and reducing competition) is the conflict deterrence mechanism formalized in our intergroup assessment layer. The additional insight that non-canyon groups may have signaled participation in the Chaco system to avoid conflict with canyon populations extends the deterrence function to a hierarchical scale not fully captured in our current model, where deterrence operates among peers.

### From Gintis, Smith & Bowles 2001

**Findings:**

- The model demonstrates that both signaling and non-signaling equilibria can be simultaneously stable (Theorem 2, p. 12). A ridge line separates their basins of attraction (Figure 2, p. 13). Movement from nonsignaling to signaling is more likely than the reverse because a small number of signalers and signal-monitoring partners can invade a nonsignaling group, while destroying signaling requires simultaneous extinguishing of both (p. 13).
- Punishment of non-cooperators can itself serve as the costly signal: enforcement is the benefit to others that signals high quality. This resolves the second-order collective action problem of who bears the cost of punishing free-riders (p. 22; citing Boyd and Richerson 1992).
- Group selection among alternative equilibria can favor the signaling equilibrium: groups with prosocial costly signaling will have members who are, on average, more fit than groups lacking such behavior. "Such groups, by withstanding extinction and dispersion, and by having superior strength in hostile interactions with other groups, can spread the prosocial practices beyond their original boundaries" (p. 20; citing Gintis 2000b).

**Key observations:** Gintis et al.'s formal results are relevant to the conflict patterns evidence in three ways. First, the bistability result provides a mechanism for understanding the spatial heterogeneity of Pax Chaco: regions that established the signaling equilibrium experienced reduced conflict (as predicted by Layer 2), while regions that remained in the non-signaling equilibrium continued to experience higher conflict rates, consistent with Kantner & Vaughn 2012's observation that violence persisted "in areas outside of Chaco's most direct influence." Second, the punishment-as-signal result provides a formal framework for understanding the coercive dimensions documented by Kohler et al. 2023 (raiding for women, resistance to expansion): punishment of defectors may have been a component of the signaling system rather than a contradiction of it. Third, the group selection mechanism predicts that signaling groups would have "superior strength in hostile interactions," consistent with the evidence for Chacoan military advantage over non-participating groups.

### From Lekson 2002

**Findings:**

- The two strongest cross-cultural predictors of warfare in non-state societies are "resource unpredictability" (R = 0.631, p < 0.001) and "socialization for mistrust" (R = 0.352, p < 0.025) (p. 611; from Ember and Ember 1992:242, Table 1).
- LeBlanc's (1999) Middle period (900-1150, extended by Lekson to 900-1250) was "an era of unprecedented peace" punctuated by EP Events (executions/extreme processing), not warfare (pp. 613-614).
- Early period violence terminated "abruptly" around AD 900, coinciding with the onset of Chaco's development (pp. 613-614; citing LeBlanc 1999:145-146).
- Middle period EP Events (~75 instances) were "spectacularly brutal" but distinct from warfare: "small groups of people were executed, dismembered, mutilated, and, less certainly, cannibalized" (pp. 614-615; citing Turner and Turner 1999; White 1992:52-53). These events were "sharply concentrated in the Middle period."
- EP Events are interpreted as politically directed coercive force: "A central authority, I argue, was enforcing peace and social order by coercion, and EP Events are the most spectacular archaeological evidence of that coercive control" (p. 618).
- The Late period (1250-1600) was marked by "unprecedented" village-on-village and alliance-on-alliance warfare, qualitatively different from Early period raiding (pp. 615, 620).
- "Violence correlates well with resource unpredictably between 750 and 1600 in the Pueblo Southwest, as do peace and resource predictability" (p. 616; Figure 2, p. 615).
- The coincidence of the end of Early period violence, the rise of Chaco, and changes in resource predictability is "close, if not precise" (p. 617).
- Middle period EP Events constituted "a dramatic socialization for fear" that may have intensified Late period warfare through lasting cultural patterns (pp. 620-621).

**Key observations:** Lekson 2002 provides the most comprehensive temporal alignment of violence and climate data for the Pueblo Southwest. The abrupt termination of Early period violence around AD 900 coinciding with Chaco's development, followed by 350 years of peace (Pax Chaco extended through Aztec), followed by explosive Late period warfare after the system collapsed, matches the Layer 2 prediction precisely: monument signaling deters conflict during the signaling period, and conflict returns when signaling ceases. The EP Events introduce an important complication: Lekson argues that Chacoan peace was enforced partly through targeted coercion, not purely through the honest-signaling mechanism. This suggests that Layer 2 may need to accommodate a coercion component alongside the mutual-assessment mechanism. The "socialization for fear" argument posits a negative legacy effect: the system may have suppressed violence in the short term while creating psychological conditions for more severe violence after collapse, a dynamic not currently captured in the model.
